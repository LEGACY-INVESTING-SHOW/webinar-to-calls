### OP.11.0 Learning outcome

After this lesson you can build a trailing-12-month report for your first property. That's the most recent 12 months of actual results. You set it beside the original underwriting, the forecast you made before you bought. You can price a cash-out refinance and a home equity line of credit (HELOC) in dollars per month and per year, including a weak year. Then you write a pass rule for property two and apply it. Here a pass rule is a short list of tests property one and your cash must pass before you shop. The result is a dated yes, not yet, or no, with the numbers that produced it.

### OP.11.1 Property two is a decision about property one

Most people start the second-property conversation with a listing. I want you to start it with a bank statement.

The case for buying a second property rests on one claim: the first one works about the way you said it would. If that claim is true, a second similar property is a repeat of a tested plan. If it is false, a second property doubles an untested plan and adds a second loan.

So the first job is proof. You need to know what property one actually earned, what it actually cost, and how much cash you have left after both. The underwriting from Sam's PA.4 lesson was a forecast. (Sam runs the live demos in this course; PA.4 is where you built that forecast.) Twelve months of operating records are evidence. This lesson is about reading the evidence before you commit more money.

The second job is liquidity. Buying property two usually means pulling cash from somewhere: savings, a refinance of property one, or a loan against your own home. Each source changes your monthly obligations and what you can survive. A second property funded by borrowed money needs a wider margin than one funded by savings.

### OP.11.2 Reading trailing performance against underwriting

Sam's OP.10 lesson shows the 30-day and 90-day review. Bring that report here. It is your baseline for the categories you track and how you pull the data. The trailing-12 is the same review stretched to a full year, so every season is in it.

Build it from records, not memory. Pull payouts and fees from each booking platform's earnings reports. Pull expenses from the property's bank account and card. Pull booked nights, stays, and average nightly rate from your property management software (PMS) or the platform calendar. If your bookkeeping mixes property one with personal spending, fix that first; the trailing-12 is only as clean as the account behind it.

Set the report up in three columns: the underwriting you bought on, the actual trailing 12 months, and the variance between them. You're looking for four things.

First, split the revenue miss into rate and occupancy. The rate is your average daily rate (ADR), the average nightly price per booked night. A lower nightly rate and a lower occupancy point to different fixes. Rate problems often trace to pricing settings or competition. Occupancy problems can trace to calendar gaps, minimum stays, reviews, or demand. Sam's OP.5 lesson covers pricing tools; you only need to know which lever moved.

Second, find the expense lines that grew. Utilities, insurance renewals, and repairs are the usual ones. One large repair in the year may be a one-off. A pattern of repairs is a budget problem.

Third, check whether you actually funded the capital reserve. Underwriting often includes a line such as 5% of gross for future replacements. If that money stayed in the operating account and got spent, your cash flow looks better than it is.

Fourth, compare the loan payment (debt service). Principal and interest (P&I) are fixed on a fixed-rate loan. Your lender may also collect property tax and insurance through escrow. That's a monthly add-on the lender holds and pays for you. Those amounts can change at renewal or reassessment. In the worksheet, keep tax and insurance in operating costs and debt service as P&I only. Then NOI is computed the same way every time.

The number that matters most is net operating income (NOI): revenue minus operating costs, before the mortgage. NOI is what a lender and a future buyer look at. Cash flow is NOI minus debt service. If trailing NOI is well under the underwritten base case, the second property should wait until you know why. You set what "well under" means in your pass rule. Maya and Chris use 90% in the worked example.

### OP.11.3 Refinance math

A cash-out refinance replaces your current loan with a bigger one and hands you the difference, minus costs. It can feel like free money from appreciation. In practice you take on a new loan with a new payment, a new term, and new closing costs.

Here is the arithmetic in order:

1. New loan amount = appraised value × the lender's maximum loan-to-value (LTV). LTV is the loan as a share of the appraised value.
2. Equity removed = new loan amount − current loan balance.
3. Cash out = equity removed − refinance closing costs.
4. New payment = the principal and interest on the new loan at the new rate and term.
5. New cash flow = NOI − new annual principal and interest.

Every input comes from a written lender quote, not a rule of thumb. The appraisal may come in lower than your estimate. The lender may cap LTV below what you expect for a short-term rental (STR). The lender may also apply its own coverage ratio. That's how many dollars of property income the lender counts for every dollar of loan payment. PA.6 shows three ways lenders compute it. Compare quotes the way PA.7 taught, using the [CFPB's guide to comparing Loan Estimates](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/compare/compare-loan-estimates/) where the lender issues one.

Watch three things.

The term reset. A new 30-year loan restarts the payoff schedule, and early payments are mostly interest. Your payment may look similar, but you are paying down principal more slowly.

Costs relative to cash. When the equity you remove is small, closing costs can eat a large share of it.

The weak year. Run the new payment against your weak-case NOI as well as the base case. The refinance makes the loan larger, so it makes a weak year worse.

### OP.11.4 HELOC mechanics and the risk to your home

A home equity line of credit is a revolving line secured by a home you already own, usually your primary residence. The [CFPB's HELOC explainer](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-home-equity-line-of-credit-heloc-en-107/) describes two phases. During the draw period (the CFPB gives 10 years as an example) you can borrow up to your limit. Many plans set minimum payments based on the balance. When the draw period ends, the repayment period begins. Depending on the plan, you may be allowed to repay over time or you may owe the whole balance at once ([CFPB HELOC brochure](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_heloc-brochure.pdf)).

HELOCs usually carry a variable rate. The rate is based on a publicly available index, such as a prime rate. That means your payment can change from month to month ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-home-equity-line-of-credit-heloc-en-107/)). Price the line at today's rate and at a higher stress rate.

Two risks deserve plain words.

Your home is the collateral. The CFPB warns that if you can't repay what you borrowed, you could lose your home ([CFPB HELOC booklet](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/201603_cfpb_booklet_heloc.pdf)). When a short-term rental funded this way underperforms, the house you live in is on the line too.

The line can shrink when you need it. The same CFPB booklet says plans generally let the lender freeze or reduce the line in two situations. One is a significant decline in the home's value. The other is when the lender reasonably believes a material change in your finances will stop you from paying ([CFPB HELOC booklet](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/201603_cfpb_booklet_heloc.pdf)). If you planned to use an undrawn HELOC as your emergency reserve, a market dip can remove it at the worst time. For that reason, don't count undrawn HELOC capacity as a reserve.

The lender must disclose the draw and repayment periods, fees, how the minimum payment is figured, and how the rate can change ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-home-equity-line-of-credit-heloc-en-107/)). Read those disclosures before you model anything.

There is also a tax question for your CPA. IRS Publication 936 limits the home mortgage interest deduction on a home equity loan or line. The money must buy, build, or substantially improve the home securing it ([IRS Pub. 936](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p936)). Using the line for a rental down payment does not meet that test. Whether the interest can be deducted another way, such as a rental expense, depends on how the CPA traces the loan proceeds. Don't assume either answer in your model.

### OP.11.5 Same market, new market, and team capacity

Once the numbers work, the operating question starts. Can your team run two properties as well as it runs one?

A second property in the same market can share things. The same cleaner, handyman, and supply run may cover both. You already know the permit process, the seasonality, and the guest profile. You can reuse your OP.8 playbook with small edits. The risk is concentration: one bad season, one new ordinance, or one storm now hits both properties.

A second property in a new market spreads that risk. It also resets the learning curve. You'll need a new market check (Sam's PA.2), a new address-level permission review (resource SR.1), a new local team, and new vendor backups. Your first-year results from property one don't transfer to a market you've never operated in.

Either way, test team capacity against the OP.7 role list. The roles are turnover cleaner, backup cleaner, handyman, emergency contact, guest messaging, inspections, bookkeeping, and supplies. For each one, write who covers it for property two and who backs them up. Say one cleaner covers both houses and both have Saturday turnovers. You likely need a second cleaner before you buy, unless one person can finish both turnovers in the same checkout window.

Then set up systems that work across properties. That means one bookkeeping file with a tag per property and one calendar view. Add one guest-message template set with property details, and one reserve policy. If a co-host helps, give them the least access they need. [Airbnb lets you set co-host permission levels](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/1534), and the listing owner remains responsible for what a co-host does.

### OP.11.6 Worked example: Cedar Ridge at the end of year 2

Maya and Chris are the example couple used throughout this course. They bought Cedar Ridge, a hypothetical lake-market house, for $385,000. They used a DSCR loan at 7.25% for 30 years, with 25% down. DSCR stands for debt service coverage ratio. The lender sized the loan mostly on the property's rental income, not their pay (PA.6). It's now the end of year 2. They're thinking about a second lake-market house. They have three funding ideas: savings, a cash-out refinance of Cedar Ridge, and a HELOC on their primary home.

**Step 1. Trailing 12 months vs underwriting.** The trailing-12 below is a hypothetical year I built for teaching. The underwriting column is their original plan (base case B1 in the course example file).

All figures are assumptions. Trailing-12: average daily rate (ADR) $289, occupancy 55%, 201 booked nights, 57 stays. Gross booking revenue also includes the $175 cleaning fee guests pay on each stay. So it's more than ADR × nights: $289 × 201 = $58,089, plus 57 × $175 = $9,975, gives $68,064.

| Line | Original plan (B1) | Trailing 12 (assumption) | Variance |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Gross booking revenue | $74,275 | $68,064 | -$6,211 |
| Platform service fee (15.5%) | -$11,513 | -$10,550 | +$963 |
| On-call handyman / inspection retainer | -$1,800 | -$1,800 | $0 |
| Cleaner pay | -$9,150 | -$8,550 | +$600 |
| Utilities, internet, trash | -$5,400 | -$5,850 | -$450 |
| Supplies | -$1,525 | -$1,596 | -$71 |
| Software | -$1,200 | -$1,200 | $0 |
| Repairs and maintenance | -$3,714 | -$5,200 | -$1,486 |
| Capital reserve (5% of gross, funded) | -$3,714 | -$3,403 | +$311 |
| Property tax | -$3,850 | -$3,850 | $0 |
| STR insurance | -$3,200 | -$3,450 | -$250 |
| **Operating costs** | **-$45,066** | **-$45,449** | **-$383** |
| **NOI** | **$29,209** | **$22,615** | **-$6,594** |
| DSCR loan principal and interest | -$23,637 | -$23,637 | $0 |
| **Cash flow** | **$5,572** | **-$1,022** | **-$6,594** |

One cost isn't in these lines: a $400-a-year permit renewal they found during due diligence (PA.9). Including it, trailing NOI is $22,215 and cash flow is -$1,422. The tables leave it out so they line up with the original plan. Every cash-flow gap below grows by $400, and no test result changes.

Read it the way section OP.11.2 described.

Revenue came in at 91.6% of the original plan ($68,064 / $74,275). Both levers moved. The rate is $11 below the $300 underwritten, and occupancy is 3 points below the 58% underwritten. Costs are only $383 higher in total. The platform fee and cleaner pay fell with lower revenue, while repairs, utilities, insurance, and supplies rose. Supplies ran about $28 a stay ($1,596 / 57) against the $25 planned. Restock prices went up, or the cleaner is restocking more per stay. Either way, it's worth a look.

NOI came in at 77.4% of underwriting ($22,615 / $29,209). An 8.4% revenue miss became a 22.6% NOI miss. Most costs stay put when revenue drops. Insurance, property tax, software, the handyman retainer, and most utilities don't shrink when you book fewer nights. So the miss grows on the way down.

Cash flow went from a planned $5,572 to -$1,022. Maya and Chris covered the gap from savings. The capital reserve was funded, which is good. It means the -$1,022 is honest.

The repairs line includes a water heater replacement (assumption). If that's a one-off, next year's repairs may fall back toward $3,714. They should still find out why rate and occupancy slipped before counting on a better year.

**Step 2. The refinance.** Value has grown 3% a year (assumption) from the $385,000 purchase price to $408,446 ($385,000 × 1.03 × 1.03).

- New loan at 75% LTV: $408,446 × 0.75 = $306,334.
- Equity removed: $306,334 − $282,951 balance = $23,383.
- Cash out: $23,383 − $7,658 refinance costs = $15,725. The $7,658 is 2.5% of the new loan (assumption). Your written quote replaces it.
- New principal and interest at 7.0% for 30 years: $2,038 a month, or $24,457 a year.
- Change in annual debt service: $24,457 − $23,637 = $820 more a year.

The costs take 32.8% of the equity removed ($7,658 / $23,383). The new 7.0% rate is a bit lower than the original 7.25%, so the payment only rises $820. But the loan is bigger and the 30-year clock restarts.

Cash flow after the refinance, against three cases. The original weak case is the bad year they underwrote before buying: $265 ADR and 50% occupancy.

| Case | NOI | New P&I | Cash flow |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Original plan (base case) | $29,209 | -$24,457 | $4,752 |
| Original weak case (B2) | $18,162 | -$24,457 | -$6,295 |
| Trailing 12 (assumption) | $22,615 | -$24,457 | -$1,842 |

The refinance delivers $15,725. That's about 11% of the $142,688 total cash it took to buy and launch Cedar Ridge (down payment, closing costs, and furnishing). It also turns the actual year's cash flow from -$1,022 to -$1,842.

**Step 3. The HELOC on their primary home.** Their home is worth $520,000 with a $260,000 mortgage. Suppose a lender allows combined loan-to-value (CLTV) of 80% (assumption; the lender sets the real limit). CLTV is your first mortgage plus the HELOC, as a share of the home's value.

- Maximum total debt: $520,000 × 0.80 = $416,000.
- Maximum line: $416,000 − $260,000 = $156,000.

Suppose they draw $100,000 (assumption) toward property two. I priced it at two variable rates (assumptions). During the draw period I assumed interest-only payments. For the repayment period I assumed a 20-year payoff.

| $100,000 drawn | 8.0% rate | 10.0% rate |
|---|---:|---:|
| Interest-only payment, monthly | $667 | $833 |
| Interest-only cost, annual | $8,000 | $10,000 |
| 20-year repayment payment, monthly | $836 | $965 |
| 20-year repayment payment, annual | $10,037 | $11,580 |

Now test what that does to a second house. Suppose property two is a Cedar Ridge twin with the same price and DSCR terms (assumption). Its base cash flow is $5,572 and its weak case is -$5,475 (original financing, B4). The HELOC payment is a household bill. I charge it to property two here because property two is the reason for the loan. Subtract the HELOC interest:

| Property two cash flow after HELOC interest | 8.0% | 10.0% |
|---|---:|---:|
| Base case: $5,572 − interest | -$2,428 | -$4,428 |
| Weak case: -$5,475 − interest | -$13,475 | -$15,475 |

A base case that was thin becomes negative once the down payment is borrowed. The weak case becomes a five-figure annual gap, and their primary home secures part of the money.

One more stress: say their home's value drops 10% to $468,000 (assumption). The 80% limit falls to $374,400. With $100,000 drawn, they owe $360,000 ($260,000 + $100,000), which is 76.9% CLTV. They're still under 80%, but the room left on the line falls from $56,000 to $14,400. The CFPB booklet in OP.11.4 also lists a significant value decline itself as a reason a lender may freeze or reduce the line. So even that $14,400 may not be there.

**Step 4. Liquidity.** After buying and launching Cedar Ridge, Maya and Chris had $80,471.50 left (PA.12 walks through each step). That figure is before two years of saving, spending, and the trailing shortfall (assumption). To keep the example simple, I hold it at $80,471.50. In your own worksheet, use today's actual balance, after any shortfalls you covered.

Their $51,000 emergency fund and the $15,342 property reserve sit inside that balance. The property reserve is six months of Cedar Ridge PITIA. PITIA means principal, interest, property tax, insurance, and association dues. Cedar Ridge has no HOA, so the dues are zero. Six months is $15,342 ($2,557 × 6). A second financed property would need its own reserve. A lender may require more ([Fannie Mae reserve requirements](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-4.1-01/minimum-reserve-requirements) show how conventional-loan reserves can extend past the down payment).

In the pass rule below I test the HELOC route. The other two ideas can't fund the twin alone. Savings of $80,471.50 can't cover $142,688, and the refinance yields only $15,725. Here's the reserve test for a twin funded with a $100,000 draw (assumption):

- Twin's total cash in (assumption: same price and terms as Cedar Ridge): $142,688.
- From savings: $142,688 − $100,000 HELOC = $42,688.
- Savings left: $80,471.50 − $42,688 = $37,783.50.
- Needed: six months of PITIA on two houses ($2,557 × 6 × 2 = $30,684), plus the $51,000 emergency fund. That's $81,684.
- Short by: $81,684 − $37,783.50 = $43,900.50.

Adding the refinance's $15,725 would leave $53,508.50, still short. It would also add $820 a year to Cedar Ridge's payment. So I leave it out.

**Step 5. Apply the pass rule.** Here is the rule they wrote before looking at listings. The thresholds are their choices (assumptions), not industry standards.

| Test | Their threshold | Cedar Ridge result | Pass? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trailing-12 NOI vs underwritten base | At least 90% | 77.4% | No |
| Property one covers its own debt service | Cash flow ≥ $0 without top-ups from savings | -$1,022 | No |
| Cash reserves after buying property two | 6 months PITIA per financed property plus personal emergency fund, none borrowed | $37,783.50 left vs $81,684 needed: $43,900.50 short (Step 4) | No |
| Property two weak case with financing at stress rate | Loss they can cover from W-2 (salary) income for 12 months without new debt | -$15,475 at 10% | Unknown: needs their household budget (not scored) |
| Team backups | Named backup for every OP.7 role for both houses | One cleaner, no backup | No |

The -$15,475 uses interest-only payments. Once the HELOC's repayment period starts, the annual cost rises to $11,580 at 10%. Then the gap grows to about -$17,055 (-$5,475 − $11,580). Maya and Chris's rule has five tests. The worksheet in OP.11.7 adds market and systems rows.

Here's how I read any pass rule. Check the No rule first. No means property one can't cover its own debt service even in its underwritten base case, or the household can't carry property two's weak case. Then hold one property. Not yet means any test fails and the No rule doesn't apply. Fix it and recheck on a set date. A failure in the first three tests always means at least not yet. Those rows describe property one and your cash, and a new listing can't fix either. Proceed means every test passes.

Result: not yet. Cedar Ridge's base case covers its loan with $5,572 to spare, and the household test is still open. So the No rule doesn't apply. Maya and Chris don't need a new property. They need a better year from the one they have. Their next steps are specific. They find the cause of the rate and occupancy gap with Sam's OP.5 tools. They confirm the water heater was a one-off, and they hire a backup cleaner. Then they rerun this worksheet after the next 90-day review.

Notice what did not drive the answer. The refinance and HELOC were available. Borrowing capacity is not the same as a reason to borrow.

### OP.11.8 Who confirms what

**Confirm with:** your bookkeeper or CPA that the trailing-12 matches the property's books and bank records, and that repairs vs improvements are classified correctly.

**Confirm with:** the refinance lender, in writing: appraised value, maximum LTV for a short-term rental, and the coverage ratio definition. Also get rate, points (upfront fees paid to lower the rate), closing costs, reserves, and any prepayment penalty on the existing loan.

**Confirm with:** the HELOC lender, in the plan disclosures: maximum CLTV, draw and repayment periods, index and margin, and any rate caps. Also get the minimum payment formula, fees, and the conditions that let the lender freeze or reduce the line.

**Confirm with:** your CPA how HELOC or refinance interest will be treated, given IRS Publication 936 and how you use the proceeds. Ask how a second property affects your depreciation and loss position (resource SR.3).

**Confirm with:** the local licensing office or an STR attorney, for property two's exact address, that the planned use is permitted (resource SR.1).

**Confirm with:** your insurer that property two can be covered for short-term rental use, and whether a combined policy changes deductibles or terms.
