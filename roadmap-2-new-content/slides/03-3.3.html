3.3 · Occupancy taxes and registration§ 3.3.01 / 19
Slide 1 · A platform that collects some taxes still leaves you owing the rest

A lot of new hosts believe Airbnb handles the taxes. Sometimes it handles some of them. The rest are yours, and the agency expects them whether or not you knew.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to build a channel-by-channel tax worksheet for one address. It shows which lodging taxes apply, who collects and pays each one on Airbnb, Vrbo, and direct bookings, and what you still owe. You'll know how to enter and keep up a permit or registration number on a listing, and what happens when it lapses. And you'll be able to reconcile a quarter of bookings against your tax filings before the agency finds the gap.

We'll use Alder Street again, in a made-up tax jurisdiction. Every rate in the example is an assumption for teaching. This is education, not tax or legal advice.

3.3 · Occupancy taxes and registration§ 3.3.12 / 19
Slide 2 · Your permit, your tax accounts, and income tax are three separate registrations

Let's separate three things students blur together. Each one has its own agency, its own number, and its own deadline.

The operating permit or registration is permission to run a short-term rental at the address. The city issues it, and it comes with a number you show on listings. Lesson 3.1 covered that.

The tax registration is an account with each agency that collects lodging tax from you. It has its own account number and filing schedule. Having a permit doesn't open a tax account, and having a tax account doesn't give you a permit. Los Angeles, for example, lists Transient Occupancy Tax registration as a separate eligibility step for home-sharing hosts.

Federal income tax is different again. Occupancy tax is money the guest pays. Income tax is money you pay on what's left. IRS Publication 527 says rental income normally goes on Schedule E, and may go on Schedule C if you provide substantial services like regular cleaning. Your CPA decides that. Lesson 9.9 covers it.

3.3 · Occupancy taxes and registration§ 3.3.23 / 19
Slide 3 · One stay can carry four tax layers, and each layer has its own rules

Lodging taxes stack. One stay can carry a state tax, a county tax, a city transient occupancy tax, and a special district charge like a tourism improvement district. Some districts charge a percentage. Some charge a flat amount per occupied night. Sometimes one agency collects several layers on one return. Sometimes you file with each one separately.

For every layer, check four things.

First, which stays are taxable. Texas, for example, defines a short-term rental for hotel tax as 29 days or less. Your city's line may differ.

Second, what's in the base. Nightly rate, cleaning fee, other fees. Airbnb says cleaning fees are included where it collects. On bookings you collect yourself, the city's rules decide.

Third, exemptions. Only apply one if the agency's own document says it applies, and keep the paperwork.

Fourth, filing frequency. Many agencies expect a return every period once you're registered, even at zero. Texas requires No Tax Due reports to be filed electronically. A missed zero return can bring a penalty in some places.

  • Your payout is the same either way: your price minus Airbnb's service fee
3.3 · Occupancy taxes and registration§ 3.3.34 / 19
Slide 4 · Airbnb collects some taxes in some places, and you may still owe the others

Airbnb collects and remits some occupancy taxes automatically in some places, and it lists those places in its help center. The system works out which taxes apply from the listing address you entered. So if the address is wrong, the taxes can be wrong too.

Here's the part people miss. Airbnb says that even where it collects some taxes, you may still have to collect others. Its own example is collecting regional taxes but not local ones. Hosts in those areas are still responsible for everything else.

For the taxes Airbnb doesn't collect, you have two options. Collect them yourself outside the platform. Or, if you're eligible, use Airbnb's custom tax tool. You can set a tax as a percentage per booking, or as a fee per guest, per night, or per guest per night, and choose what it applies to. Airbnb charges it to the guest and pays it to you separately from your payout. You still own the filing and the payment.

And your payout doesn't change either way. The tax sits on top.

Slide 5 · Vrbo remits where it's liable and gives you three ways to handle the rest

Vrbo works on a similar split. Where Vrbo is liable for a lodging tax, it collects and remits that tax on a return filed under HomeAway.com, Inc.'s taxpayer account. Where it isn't liable, the owner or manager is responsible. Vrbo publishes, state by state, the jurisdictions where it collects.

For the taxes you owe, Vrbo describes three ways to handle them.

One, you collect, file, and pay them yourself outside the platform.

Two, Vrbo collects them from travelers and sends them to you with your payout. You still file and pay.

Three, Vrbo collects them and Avalara MyLodgeTax prepares, files, and pays. Vrbo describes that as available at no extra cost.

Vrbo says it always collects and remits where the law requires it, whichever option you choose. The one to watch is option two. The tax money arrives in your bank account mixed with your payout. It isn't yours. It belongs to the agency, and it has to be there when you file.

Slide 6 · On a direct booking you quote, charge, hold, report, and pay every layer

Direct bookings are the simplest to explain and the easiest to get wrong. Nobody collects for you. You quote the tax, charge it, hold it, report it, and pay it, for every layer.

That means your booking engine or PMS has to carry the correct rate and the correct base for each layer. Before your first direct booking, check those settings against your worksheet.

The Texas Comptroller spells this split out plainly. Owners must collect and remit state hotel tax on rentals through their own website, or through a platform that doesn't collect. And when a collecting platform does handle state tax, the owner should still contact the county and city about local hotel tax.

That's one state's rule. But the pattern is common. Platform collection often covers some layers, not all of them.

Slide 7 · The registration number goes on the listing, and some cities make the platform verify it

Where a city requires a permit or registration number, the platform usually has a field for it. On Airbnb, you go to the listing, find the laws and regulations section, and add the permit number there.

Some cities let a listing show "City registration pending" while an application is in process. Others allow "Exempt" for categories the city exempts, like a hotel. Only use those statuses when the city's rules allow them for your listing.

In some cities, the platform checks the number against the city's records. New York City is the clearest case. A booking service can't collect a fee for a short-term rental until it has checked the city's electronic verification system. That check confirms the number is valid and that the host's legal name and address match. Airbnb's New York page says hosts waiting for approval may set a 30-night minimum in the meantime.

So keep the name and address identical everywhere, down to the unit number. A mismatch can stop bookings.

Slide 8 · Calendar every expiration 60 days out and file the renewal when the window opens

Numbers expire. Airbnb's Los Angeles page says the Home-Sharing Registration is valid for one year and has to be renewed every year. The renewal window opens 30 days before expiration. If the registration lapses, you have to apply again and enter the new number on the listing. Failing to renew may affect your ability to host.

What happens when a number fails verification or expires depends on the city and the platform's arrangement with it. In a city like New York, a platform may not be able to take a booking at all without a verified number. So treat a lapse as lost revenue plus a compliance problem.

Here's the routine I suggest. Put every expiration date on your calendar with a reminder 60 days out. That 60 days is my suggested buffer, not a rule. File the renewal as soon as the window opens. Then check the number on the listing and save a dated screenshot after every renewal.

Reconcile tax owed against tax collected every period, before you file

You need three records: the PMS reservation report, each platform's earnings report, and your tax worksheet.

  1. Start from the reservation report. Remove fully refunded cancellations; adjust partial refunds.
  2. Split taxable receipts by channel.
  3. For each layer, separate platform-remitted receipts from the ones you owe on.
  4. Compute tax owed by layer on your share.
  5. Add up tax actually collected by layer: platform tax payouts, Vrbo pass-throughs, direct charges.
  6. Compare owed with collected, and explain every difference.
3.3 · Occupancy taxes and registration§ 3.3.79 / 19
Slide 9 · Reconcile tax owed against tax collected every period, before you file

Reconciliation means proving that the tax you filed matches the stays you hosted. Do it every filing period, before you file.

It catches four common errors. A direct booking charged the wrong rate. A tax layer left off. A cancellation left in taxable receipts. Or platform-remitted receipts reported as if you collected them.

You need three records. The reservation report from your PMS, with every stay, channel, nights, check-in date, revenue, cleaning fee, and status. Each platform's earnings report. And your tax worksheet.

Then six steps. Start from the reservations and remove full refunds. Split receipts by channel. Separate what the platforms remitted from what you owe on. Compute what you owe by layer. Add up what you actually collected. Compare, and explain every difference.

A shortfall usually means you forgot to charge something, and you owe it anyway, out of your own pocket. An overage usually means you charged tax that didn't apply. Ask the agency how to handle it. And follow each return's own instructions.
