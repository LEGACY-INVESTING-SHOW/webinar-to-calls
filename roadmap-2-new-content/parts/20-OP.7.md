### OP.7.0 Learning outcome

After this lesson you can list every job a stay requires and name who does each one, plus a backup. You'll decide what access each person gets on the platform, with money, with guest data, and with keys. You'll compare a set of hired vendors against a co-host or manager using your own numbers. And you'll spot the places where you'd pay two people for the same task.

### OP.7.1 Start with the jobs, not the job titles

"Should I hire a co-host or a property manager?" is the wrong first question. Those titles cover very different scopes, and the scopes overlap. A co-host, for example, is anyone you pay to help run your listing, with the access you give them. One does messaging only. Another does everything but cleaning. Start with the list of jobs every stay needs. Then decide who does each one.

Here are the jobs for a single owned house, grouped by when they happen. A turnover is the clean and reset between one guest's checkout and the next check-in.

- Every stay: turnover cleaning, laundry, restocking consumables, a damage and condition check, and guest messaging from booking to checkout.
- On demand: maintenance and repairs, lockouts, supply runs, and access for vendors.
- Around the clock: emergencies and anything that can't wait until morning.
- On a schedule: inspections (filters, alarms, grill, deck, water heater), bookkeeping, tax filings, and permit renewals.
- Owner-only: pricing approval, compliance decisions, bank and platform ownership, and reviewing how the team is performing.

Notice what's in the last group. Some jobs you can delegate, but you can't hand off responsibility for them. The owner keeps title to the house, the loan, and the platform account. The owner answers to the city, the lender, and the insurer.

### OP.7.2 The role map and the backup rule

Once you have the job list, write one name beside each job. Then write a second name. The backup rule is simple: every role that touches a guest stay needs a backup who has already been trained and given access. Access for a backup has to be ready before the emergency, not handed over during it.

The roles for a small owned short-term rental (STR) usually look like this. Par levels are the minimum stock of each item you keep on hand.

| Role | Covers | Typical payment |
|---|---|---|
| Turnover cleaner | Cleaning, laundry, restock, condition photos | Per turnover |
| Backup cleaner | Same, when the primary can't | Per turnover, often at a premium |
| Handyman / on-call vendor | Repairs, inspections, vendor access | Retainer plus hourly, or hourly |
| Emergency contact | After-hours first response within a set drive time | Retainer, or part of a co-host scope |
| Guest messaging | Inquiries, check-in details, questions, issues | Owner time, co-host fee, or a messaging service |
| Inspections | Scheduled walk-throughs and safety checks | Handyman retainer or co-host scope |
| Bookkeeping | Categorizing income and expenses, reconciling payouts | Owner, bookkeeper, or CPA |
| Supply replenishment | Keeping par levels of consumables and linens | Cleaner, co-host, or owner by delivery order |

The retained Roadmap lessons 6.1 Cleaners and 6.2 Handyman cover finding and training those vendors. This lesson is about fitting them together for a house you own.

### OP.7.3 The access matrix: five kinds of access

Every person on the team needs some access. Give each one the least access that still lets them do the job. There are five kinds of access to decide, and they aren't the same thing.

1. Platform access. Can they see the calendar? Message guests? Change prices or the listing?
2. Spending authority. How much can they spend without asking, and on what?
3. Guest data. Do they see names, phone numbers, and messages? Do they store them anywhere?
4. Keys and codes. Physical keys, a lock code, the code to the owner's locked storage closet, the Wi-Fi admin login.
5. After-hours responsibility. Who answers the phone at 2 a.m., and how fast must they get there?

A common mistake is giving full platform access to someone who only needed a calendar. Access given for convenience is hard to take back later without friction.

### OP.7.4 Airbnb's co-host permission levels

Airbnb lets a listing owner add co-hosts with different permission levels. The [co-host permissions page](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/1534) describes three levels. The descriptions below summarize Airbnb's help pages as of 2026-09-25. Airbnb changes these screens, so check the labels in your own account before you rely on them.

| Level | What it allows (per Airbnb's help pages) | Fits |
|---|---|---|
| Full access | Manage the listing and calendar, message guests, view payouts and transaction history, manage other co-hosts and their permissions, and submit reimbursement requests on the host's behalf | A full-service co-host or manager you have vetted and contracted |
| Calendar and messaging access | View (not edit) the calendar and message guests | A messaging co-host or a cleaner-coordinator who talks to guests |
| Calendar access | View the calendar and check-in and checkout details. These co-hosts don't appear as co-hosts on the listing | A cleaner or handyman who only needs the turnover schedule |

A few more points from Airbnb's help pages matter for owners:

- The listing owner is responsible for co-hosts, including co-hosts added by a full-access co-host. Airbnb's [co-host page](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/1534) tells owners to choose them with care.
- Only the listing owner can set up co-host payouts, per Airbnb's [co-host payouts article](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/3389). Payouts can be set up for co-hosts at any permission level. When the host's earnings exceed the co-host payouts, co-hosts are paid first. So a percentage co-host's share leaves before yours on each booking. Check the payout math monthly, because a wrong percentage goes straight out the door.
- According to Airbnb's co-host help pages ([payouts](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/3389), [permissions](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/1534)), co-hosts can't view or change the owner's payout method or taxpayer information.
- The [Co-Host Additional Terms](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/3264) say the host stays responsible for a co-host's actions and obligations from before the co-host was removed.

Least-needed access for Cedar Ridge (the course's example house, owned by Maya and Chris, which we price in OP.7.7) looks like this. The cleaner gets calendar access. If they ever add a messaging co-host, that person gets calendar and messaging access. Nobody gets full access unless they're managing the listing under a written agreement.

### OP.7.5 Vetting and the written agreement

A handshake works until the first damaged sofa. Every role that touches the house or guests gets a short written agreement. It doesn't need to be long. It needs to answer the questions you'd otherwise argue about later.

Agreement terms to cover:

| Term | What the agreement should say |
|---|---|
| Scope | Which jobs from the list in OP.7.1 are included, by name. Anything not listed is extra or not included. |
| Fees and fee base | Per turnover, hourly, flat monthly, or percentage. If a percentage, of what: nightly revenue only, nightly plus cleaning fees, or payout after platform fees. |
| Quality standard | The OP.8 checklist is the standard, with photo proof of each room after every turnover. |
| Response time | How fast they reply to a message and how fast they can be on site, by hour of day. |
| Spending authority | A dollar limit per item and per month without approval, and what counts as an emergency exception. |
| Insurance | General liability for any vendor who works in the house. Workers' compensation and auto coverage may apply depending on the state and the vendor's setup. |
| Data handling | Guest information stays in the platform or the PMS (property management software). No screenshots of guest IDs, no personal copies of phone lists, deletion on termination. |
| Keys and codes | Individual lock codes, changed when someone leaves. No shared master code. |
| Backup coverage | Who covers when they're sick or booked, and who pays the difference. |
| Termination and handover | Notice period, return of keys, removal from the platform, and handover of future bookings and records. |

Some states and cities require a real estate or property management license for anyone who manages a rental for someone else for pay. That can apply to a co-host or manager. Ask before you sign. OP.7.9 lists who confirms this, and what to ask the insurer about vendor coverage.

Vetting runs before the agreement. Ask for two references from other STR owners, not from friends. Ask for proof of insurance. Ask the cleaner to do one paid trial turnover to the OP.8 standard before the first guest. Ask the co-host how many properties they handle and who covers when two emergencies happen at once.

### OP.7.6 Vendor set vs. co-host vs. manager, and how to avoid paying twice

There are three broad setups for an owned house.

A **vendor set** means the owner hires each role separately and coordinates them. The owner does guest messaging and approvals. This costs the least in fees and the most in owner time.

A **co-host** usually takes on some or all of messaging, coordination, restocking, and local response. Cleaning and repairs are often still billed separately. Scope varies widely, which is why the agreement matters.

A **full manager** usually takes on the listing, pricing, messaging, coordination, and owner statements. Many managers use their own cleaners and vendors, and some add markups.

Paying twice happens when two people are paid for the same job. It usually happens quietly. The owner keeps a handyman inspection retainer after the co-host's scope already includes monthly inspections. Or the owner pays a messaging service while the co-host is also answering guests. Or the manager charges a supply markup while the cleaner also bills for supplies.

The fix is a task ledger. Write every job from OP.7.1 in one column. Beside each job, name exactly one payer. If two names appear, cut one or rewrite a scope.

### OP.7.7 Worked example: Cedar Ridge

Cedar Ridge is the hypothetical house this course follows. Maya and Chris own it. They live about two hours away, and both work full time.

A few terms before the numbers. The base case is the expected year. The weak case is a slower year: $265 a night at 50% occupancy. The base case expects 61 stays a year: 212 booked nights at $300 a night, with a 3.5-night average stay. Gross booking revenue is $74,275. That's nightly revenue plus the cleaning fees guests pay.

NOI is net operating income: gross booking revenue minus all operating costs, before the loan payment. Cedar Ridge's NOI of $29,209 already subtracts platform fees, the $150 cleaner, and the $150/month handyman retainer. It also subtracts utilities, supplies, software, repairs, the capital reserve, property tax, and insurance.

Maya and Chris have two loan quotes. One is a DSCR loan, sized mostly on the rental's income (PA.6 and PA.7 cover it). The other is a conventional loan. They chose conventional first, with DSCR as the backup. We test on the DSCR quote because it costs more. Its annual loan payments (principal and interest) are $23,637.

**Option A: vendor set (original underwriting).** A local cleaner at $150 per turnover ($9,150 a year). An on-call handyman and inspection retainer at $150 a month ($1,800 a year). Maya handles messaging; Chris handles approvals and bookkeeping.

- NOI: $29,209.
- Cash flow on the DSCR quote: $29,209 - $23,637 = **$5,572**.

**Option B: full-service local co-host at 18%.** Same cleaner and handyman retainer, plus 18% of gross booking revenue.

- Co-host fee: 18% × $74,275 = $13,370.
- NOI: $29,209 - $13,370 = $15,839.
- Cash flow on the DSCR quote: $15,839 - $23,637 = **-$7,798**.

That is a $13,370 swing from positive to negative. The house doesn't carry an 18% co-host at the base case.

The next two variants each change one thing from Option B. They don't stack.

**Where Option B pays twice.** Suppose the co-host's scope includes monthly inspections and after-hours response. Then the $150 retainer duplicates part of that scope. Drop the retainer (all figures are assumptions):

- NOI: $15,839 + $1,800 = $17,639.
- Cash flow: $17,639 - $23,637 = **-$5,998**.

Still negative, but $1,800 better, just from removing a duplicate.

**The fee base matters.** The same 18% charged on nightly revenue only, not on cleaning fees:

- Nightly revenue: 212 nights × $300 = $63,600. Cleaning fees collected: $74,275 - $63,600 = $10,675.
- Fee: 18% × $63,600 = $11,448 (assumption about the fee base), which is $1,922 less than the fee on full gross.
- NOI with retainer kept: $29,209 - $11,448 = $17,761. Cash flow: **-$5,876**.

**Option C: messaging-only co-host at 10% (assumption).** The co-host gets calendar and messaging access and answers guests. Maya and Chris keep the cleaner and the handyman retainer and handle approvals.

- Fee: 10% × $74,275 = $7,428.
- NOI: $29,209 - $7,428 = $21,781.
- Cash flow on the DSCR quote: $21,781 - $23,637 = **-$1,856**.
- On the conventional quote ($22,763 of annual loan payments): $21,781 - $22,763 = -$982.
- Weak case (gross $57,330, NOI $18,162): fee $5,733, NOI $12,429, cash flow -$11,208.

For comparison, even the vendor set is negative in the weak case: $18,162 - $23,637 = -$5,475. A co-host makes a weak year worse. The gap is there before any co-host.

**A flat-fee alternative (assumption).** A messaging service at $400 a month costs $4,800 a year, about 6.5% of gross. NOI: $29,209 - $4,800 = $24,409. Cash flow: $24,409 - $23,637 = **$772**. Flat fees don't grow when revenue grows, and they don't shrink in a weak season either.

**Break-even fee.** How much percentage fee can the base case carry before cash flow hits zero on the DSCR quote?

- Room before any co-host: $29,209 - $23,637 = $5,572.
- $5,572 / $74,275 = **7.5%** of gross booking revenue. On the conventional quote it's 8.7%.

For Cedar Ridge, any percentage co-host above 7.5% of gross turns the base case negative on the DSCR loan. Your number will be different.

This break-even assumes the fee is a percentage of gross. If a quote is a percentage of nightly revenue only, divide your vendor-set cash flow by nightly revenue instead. For Cedar Ridge that's $5,572 ÷ $63,600 = 8.8% of nightly revenue. Do the same for any other base, such as payout after platform fees.

**Pricing owner time.** Use the time estimate for Maya and Chris from lesson 0.3: about 1.5 hours per stay on messages, scheduling, and turnover checks (assumption). That's 1.5 × 61 = 91.5 hours a year.

This is the most a co-host could replace. A messaging-only co-host (Option C) takes the messages but not the scheduling or turnover checks. So the hours it really replaces are fewer, and its real cost per hour is higher than the figure below.

Their other owner work is about 4 hours a month of bookkeeping, pricing, and vendor calls (also from 0.3). We leave it out to keep the comparison simple.

- Option C costs $7,428 / 91.5 = at least $81 for each hour of owner time it replaces.
- Option B costs $13,370 / 91.5 = about $146 per hour.
- The flat service costs $4,800 / 91.5 = about $52 per hour replaced (same caveat about hours).

That's the question behind a percentage co-host: is an hour of their time worth at least $81, or $146, when cash flow goes negative?

Summary, all on the DSCR quote:

| Setup | Added cost | NOI | Annual cash flow |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A: vendor set (original underwriting) | $0 | $29,209 | $5,572 |
| B: 18% co-host plus retainer | $13,370 | $15,839 | -$7,798 |
| B without duplicate retainer | $11,570 | $17,639 | -$5,998 |
| B at 18% of nightly only | $11,448 | $17,761 | -$5,876 |
| C: 10% messaging-only co-host | $7,428 | $21,781 | -$1,856 |
| Flat messaging service, $400/month | $4,800 | $24,409 | $772 |

Rows after Option B are assumptions built from the course's Cedar Ridge figures.

**Adjustment for the permit renewal.** These comparisons use the original underwriting. A later diligence step (PA.9) found a $400/year permit renewal. Subtract $400 from every cash-flow figure above. Option A becomes $5,172, the flat fee $372, and the break-even fee about 7.0%. The vendor set's weak case becomes -$5,875. The ranking doesn't change, and every percentage co-host still turns the base case negative.

**What about a full manager?** We don't price a full manager for Cedar Ridge. Run any manager quote through the same test. Take the fee in dollars, minus the vendors it replaces. Compare that with the $5,572 of room ($5,172 after the permit renewal).

**The Cedar Ridge team.** Given these numbers, Maya and Chris choose the vendor set and do the messaging themselves, with Chris as Maya's backup. The $400/month flat service also stays positive, but only by $772 (about $372 after the permit renewal), against $5,572 for the vendor set. They keep the flat service as the first thing to try if messaging becomes too much.

| Role | Primary | Backup | Platform access | Spending authority | Guest data | Keys / codes | After hours |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Turnover cleaner | Local cleaner | Second cleaner, trained on one paid trial | Calendar access | Emergency top-ups only, up to $75 per turnover (assumption), receipts required; routine supplies are ordered by Maya | Guest first name and party size only, from the calendar | Own lock code; code to the owner's storage closet | No |
| Handyman | On-call handyman (retainer) | Second handyman or HVAC company | None; tasks sent by owner | Up to $200 per job without approval (assumption) | None | Own lock code, active during job windows and emergency calls | Yes, within 60 minutes (assumption) |
| Guest messaging | Maya | Chris | Listing owner | Goodwill gestures up to $100 (assumption) | Full, in platform only | n/a | Yes, phone on until 10 p.m.; after that, calls forward to the handyman for urgent issues |
| Emergency contact | Handyman | Chris by phone; 911 for any fire, CO, or injury | None | Emergency exception, owner informed within 1 hour | Name and phone of the guest during the incident | Own lock code | Yes |
| Inspections | Handyman, monthly | Chris, on personal-use days | None | Within retainer | None | Own lock code | No |

**Decision rules.**

- If the proposed fee is above your break-even fee, cash flow goes negative in the base case. Proceed only if you've decided to fund that from other income, and write that down.
- If the weak-case cash flow goes negative by more than a year of reserves can cover, reduce the scope or change the fee base.
- If any role has no trained backup, don't open the calendar until it does.
- If anyone has more platform access than their row requires, lower it now.

### OP.7.9 Who confirms what

**Confirm with:** an attorney or the state real estate licensing board on whether a paid co-host or manager needs a license in your state.

**Confirm with:** the insurer on required vendor coverage, and whether a co-host or manager must be named or listed on the policy.

**Confirm with:** the CPA on how co-host payouts through Airbnb are reported. Ask whether any contractor needs a year-end information return (a tax form reporting what you paid them).

**Confirm with:** Airbnb's current co-host help pages and your account screens on permission level names and what each allows, before you give anyone access.
