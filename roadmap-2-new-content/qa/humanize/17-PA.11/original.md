### PA.11.0 Learning outcome

After this lesson you can check your final closing figures line by line against your accepted offer and your latest loan terms. You'll know which document does what and how to move your closing funds without handing them to a scammer. You'll also know what to collect when the keys to your short-term rental (STR) change hands. You'll leave with a filled closing-figures comparison, a closing-day checklist, and an unresolved items log that carries into PA.12.

### PA.11.1 Three things change hands

Closing looks like a pile of signatures. Underneath, three things move.

Money moves from you and your lender to the seller, the seller's lender, the title company, and a list of service providers. Documents move ownership to you and give your lender a claim on the house. Control moves too: keys, codes, accounts, records, and the right to occupy.

A title company or a closing attorney runs the closing. I'll call the person handling your file there the settlement agent.

Most closing problems come from one of those three getting ahead of the others. You wire money before you've checked the figures. You sign before the walkthrough. You get the deed but not the lock codes. This lesson puts them in order: check the figures, walk the house, review the documents, verify the wire by voice, and sign what matches. Then collect control before you leave. Ask your settlement agent early whether your funds must arrive before or after you sign.

### PA.11.2 Which closing document will you get?

For most consumer mortgages, the lender must give you a Closing Disclosure. That's the lender's final form listing your loan terms and closing costs. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) says you must receive it at least three business days before closing. The window lets you compare final terms and costs with your Loan Estimate, the lender's early estimate. It also gives you time to ask questions first ([CFPB, Review documents before closing](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/close/review-documents-before-closing/)). The CFPB also says not to close until you've received and reviewed it ([CFPB, What should I do if I don't get a Closing Disclosure three days before closing](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-should-i-do-if-i-do-not-get-a-closing-disclosure-three-days-before-my-mortgage-closing-en-1911/)).

Investment property adds a wrinkle. The CFPB has official commentary on its lending rules (Regulation Z). It treats credit to buy a non-owner-occupied rental as business-purpose credit ([CFPB, Comment for 1026.3](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/interp-3/)). Business-purpose credit falls outside many of those consumer disclosure rules. A debt service coverage ratio (DSCR) loan is sized mostly on the property's income, as PA.7 explained. So a DSCR lender may give you a settlement statement from the title company instead of a Closing Disclosure.

There's one exception to watch. The same commentary covers a vacation home the owner will occupy for more than 14 days in the coming year. That home doesn't get the non-owner-occupied treatment. So give your lender an honest personal-use number, because it affects which rules apply to your loan.

Our running example is Cedar Ridge, a hypothetical three-bedroom lake house. Buyers Maya and Chris are buying it for $385,000 as a short-term rental. They plan about 10 personal days a year, under that 14-day line.

What does that mean for you? Ask your lender, in writing, two questions early. Which closing document will I receive? When will I receive final figures? If your loan doesn't come with the three-day rule, ask for the final figures at least three business days ahead anyway. Put that request in writing to both the lender and the title company. You're asking for time to check. On your loan it may not be a legal right.

Whatever the form is called, the review is the same. Compare it with the accepted offer, any amendments from PA.9, and your latest loan terms.

### PA.11.3 What to compare, line by line

The CFPB says to check that cash to close matches your latest Loan Estimate, and to ask the lender why if it doesn't ([CFPB, Closing Disclosure explainer](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/closing-disclosure/)). If your loan comes without a Loan Estimate, use your lender's written quote. The CFPB also notes some costs can change, and there are legal rules about what can change and by how much. Those limits depend on the loan type and the fee. Your lender and settlement agent explain which ones apply to you.

Work through four groups.

The first group is the deal terms from your contract. Check the purchase price, the earnest money credit, any seller credit, and any repair escrow (money held back until a repair is done). Also check any items the seller agreed to leave. For Cedar Ridge, the PA.9 amendment has the seller fix the septic and treat the well before closing. The seller also pays a $3,100 contractor's lien from their proceeds. PA.9 found that lien in the title search. The lien payoff should show on the seller's side, not yours.

The second group is the loan terms. Check the loan amount, rate, points, monthly principal and interest (P&I), and loan term. Also check for any prepayment penalty, a fee for paying the loan off early. Compare each against the written quote you accepted in PA.7. A rate or point change needs an explanation. The CFPB notes a rate can change if something important changes, such as an appraisal coming in low ([CFPB, rate or fees changed](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/my-rate-or-the-fees-changed-between-my-loan-estimate-and-my-closing-disclosure-what-do-i-do-en-184/)).

The third group is closing costs. Check each fee against the estimate. On a Closing Disclosure, services you couldn't shop for, services you did shop for, and other costs sit in separate sections. Your title charges should match the title company's quote. The owner's title policy should be there if you chose it in PA.9.

The fourth group is prepaids and escrow. This is where the insurance premium, prepaid interest, and the starting balance of your escrow account appear. The CFPB explains that an escrow account lets you pay insurance and property taxes monthly with your loan payment ([CFPB, Closing Disclosure explainer](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/closing-disclosure/)). Check that the insurance premium is for the STR policy from PA.9, not a generic homeowners estimate. Check the escrow lines against your lender's escrow estimate.

Finally, check the names. The buyer, borrower, and insured names must match your PA.10 worksheet exactly.

### PA.11.4 Worked example: Cedar Ridge closing figures

In PA.7, Maya and Chris kept the DSCR loan as their backup. For this example, assume they closed on it (assumption). The terms come from the example's figures: $385,000 price, $288,750 loan (75%) at 7.25% with 1 point, 30-year fixed. One point means 1% of the loan amount, paid up front. Down payment is $96,250. Total closing costs including points are $14,438, and cash to close is $110,688.

Monthly P&I is $1,970, and monthly PITIA is $2,557. PITIA means principal, interest, taxes, insurance, and association dues. Cedar Ridge has no homeowners association, so it has no dues.

The example's assumptions give only the total: closing costs of 3% of price, plus 1 point. Below is one plausible way that $14,438 breaks down. Every line item is an assumption. Real fees vary by state, lender, and title company. Transfer taxes and property tax prorations are left out to keep the example simple. In this hypothetical, the seller pays any transfer tax.

I've laid the figures out in Closing Disclosure sections so you can see the structure. On a DSCR loan, your lender may not send a Loan Estimate or Closing Disclosure. Compare its written fee quote with the title company's settlement statement instead. The "Estimate / quote" column below stands for whichever early estimate you got.

The letters follow Closing Disclosure sections. A is origination, B is services you couldn't shop for, and C is services you shopped for. E is government fees, F is prepaids, G is the initial escrow deposit, and H is other costs. D is left out here.

The final column adds up to the example's totals. Points of $2,888 plus $11,550 of other costs equals $14,438. The $11,550 is 3% of $385,000. Adding the $96,250 down payment gives $110,688.

The prepaid interest line uses the daily rate. $288,750 × 7.25% ÷ 365 = about $57.35 a day. Ten days is about $574. Fifteen days is about $860. The amount depends on the day of the month you close, which is why it moved.

The escrow lines use the example's property tax of $3,850 a year and STR insurance of $3,200 a year. Three months of tax is $3,850 ÷ 4 = about $962. Two months of insurance is $3,200 ÷ 6 = about $533. Your lender sets how many months of tax and insurance go into the starting escrow balance. If the months look high, ask your lender how it calculated them.

Now read the changes the way you would at your kitchen table.

1. Insurance up $600, and its escrow line up $100. The estimate used a generic homeowners premium of $2,600 (assumption). The final figures use the actual STR quote of $3,200 from PA.9. That's expected, and it's the right policy. This $3,200 is the same first-year premium already in the Cedar Ridge operating budget. If you add up year-one cash, include it once, either here or in operating costs, not both.
2. Appraisal up $100. The STR rent analysis add-on cost more than estimated. Ask the lender whether that increase is allowed under the rules that apply to your loan.
3. Lender's title policy and settlement fee up $95 combined, and survey up $50. These are services they shopped for. Ask the title company and surveyor to explain each change.
4. Prepaid interest down $286. The closing date moved later in the month.

Net effect: $600 + $100 + $100 + $50 + $45 + $50 - $286 = $659. That matches the difference between the two totals. The two insurance lines alone add $700, more than the whole net change. The other fees add $245, and the later closing date takes back $286.

Next, the wire amount. Suppose Maya and Chris paid $7,700 of earnest money at contract, which is 2% of price (assumption). That deposit is credited at closing. The funds they still owe are $110,688 - $7,700 = $102,988. That's the number they'll wire, and it's the number a scammer would love to redirect.

Your statement may show this figure under a different label. Its cash-to-close line may also already subtract the deposit. Before wiring, confirm with the settlement agent which figure is the wire amount. Do it by voice, at the saved number.

Here are the checks that aren't in the fee table. The seller's septic and well invoices should be in the file, per PA.9. The seller's side should show the $3,100 lien payoff. The borrower names should match the PA.10 decision. The monthly payment should read $1,970 P&I, and about $2,557 with escrow. Escrow adds $587.50 a month: $3,850 of tax plus $3,200 of insurance, divided by 12.

### PA.11.5 The final walkthrough

The CFPB says to use the final walkthrough to confirm agreed repairs were made. It also says to check that items the seller agreed to leave are still there. If something's wrong, contact the seller right away ([CFPB, What should I do before, during, and after closing](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-should-i-do-before-during-and-after-the-mortgage-closing-process-en-1907/)).

For an STR, the walkthrough is also your first look at the house as an operator. Bring the PA.9 tracker and walk it row by row.

1. Seller repairs. Look at the septic work and the new well UV (ultraviolet) treatment system, and get both invoices. Check anything else the seller agreed to fix.
2. Included items. Check furniture, appliances, the dock equipment, and anything else in the contract.
3. New damage. Look for anything that wasn't there at inspection: water stains, broken windows, storm damage.
4. Systems. Run water at every tap, flush toilets, test the HVAC in both modes, and check the water heater.
5. Photos and meter readings. Photograph each room and write down the utility meter readings with the time. You'll use them for final bills and for the PA.12 handoff.

If the walkthrough turns up a real problem, don't sign and hope. Call your agent, and your attorney if you have one. Common fixes include a repair escrow, a credit, or a short delay. The CFPB notes you can walk away at closing, but you might lose your deposit and face other consequences under your contract ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-should-i-do-before-during-and-after-the-mortgage-closing-process-en-1907/)). Let your attorney or agent use that lever. Don't try it alone at the table.

### PA.11.6 What you sign and what each document does

You'll sign a stack. Know what the main pieces do, and read the names on each one against your PA.10 worksheet.

The deed transfers ownership from the seller to the title owner you chose. The seller signs it, and it gets recorded in the county records. Ask the title company when you'll receive the recorded copy.

The promissory note is the borrower's promise to repay the loan on its terms. Read the rate, payment, maturity, and any prepayment section.

The mortgage or deed of trust gives the lender a claim on the house if the loan isn't repaid. It also holds clauses you should read. One is the due-on-sale clause from PA.10. It lets the lender demand full payment if the property is transferred without its consent.

An entity loan is a loan made to your LLC rather than to you personally. Your lender may require a personal guaranty on one. That's your personal promise to repay if the entity doesn't. Read it with your attorney before closing day, not at the table.

The final closing figures and settlement statement show every dollar in and out. Initial every page only after it matches your comparison.

The insurance binder or declarations page isn't signed at closing, but the lender needs it. The effective date must be the closing date or earlier. The named insured must match the loan documents. So must the mortgagee clause, the line that names your lender on the policy.

The CFPB says to request the rest of your closing documents in advance. If documents at the table differ from what you reviewed, don't sign until you're comfortable ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-should-i-do-before-during-and-after-the-mortgage-closing-process-en-1907/)).

### PA.11.7 Wire fraud: the protocol

Closing-day wire fraud targets exactly your situation: a known closing date, a large transfer, and several parties emailing each other. The CFPB warns that scammers send emails posing as your agent or settlement agent with last-minute changes to wiring instructions ([CFPB, Buying a home? Beware of mortgage closing scams](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_mortgages-scams_one-pager.pdf)). Its guidance comes down to a handful of steps. Here's how I'd turn them into a protocol. Where a step says "the CFPB says" or "suggests", that's their guidance. The rest is how I'd put it into practice.

1. Set it up early. At contract, meet or call your agent and settlement agent. The CFPB suggests you identify two trusted people who can confirm payment instructions. Your agent and your settlement agent are the natural pair. Write down their phone numbers from a source you trust. Use a card handed to you in person, or the company website you typed in yourself.
2. Agree on a code phrase. The CFPB suggests a code phrase only your trusted contacts will know ([CFPB, Beware of mortgage closing scams](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/beware-mortgage-closing-scams/)). Ask for it on every call about money. If the caller can't give it, hang up and call the saved number.
3. Verify every instruction by voice. Before you send any money, call the settlement agent at the saved number. Read back the bank name, account name, account number, and amount. The CFPB says to verify in person or by the phone number you previously agreed to. It says to avoid phone numbers or links in an email ([CFPB one-pager](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_mortgages-scams_one-pager.pdf)).
4. Treat any change as fraud until proven otherwise. A "new account" email is the classic move. Stop and call the saved number.
5. Don't email financial information. The CFPB says email is never a secure way to send it.
6. Don't trust caller ID. The CFPB notes scammers can spoof phone numbers. If a call feels wrong, hang up and call the saved number.
7. Confirm receipt. After the wire, call the settlement agent at the saved number and confirm they received the exact amount.
8. If something goes wrong, act in minutes. The CFPB says to contact your bank or wire service immediately to try to stop or reverse the transfer. It also says to file a complaint with the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center ([CFPB, Mortgage closing scams](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/archive/blog/mortgage-closing-scams-how-protect-yourself-and-your-closing-funds/)). Ask your bank this week who to call and what its cutoff times are, so you aren't looking it up in a panic.

For Cedar Ridge, the wire is $102,988. Maya and Chris saved the settlement agent's number from the title company's website at contract. They agreed on a code phrase with their agent. Their bank's wire form gets filled in only after the voice readback.

### PA.11.8 Possession: keys, codes, records, and proof

The deed gets recorded in the county records after closing. Control transfers only when you have every way into the house and every record about it. Your contract says when possession passes. Collect these before you leave the closing or the house that day.

1. Physical keys, garage remotes, mailbox keys, and gate or boat-launch passes.
2. Door codes, alarm codes, and the admin login for any smart lock, camera, thermostat, or Wi-Fi router. Ask the seller to remove their accounts from each device, then reset every code yourself.
3. Warranties and manuals: roof, HVAC, water heater, appliances, septic components, and the well UV system the seller installed.
4. Vendor records: septic pump and repair invoices, well test results, pest treatment, HVAC service, and the names of the seller's plow and trash services.
5. Permits and approvals: the county's STR permit status for your new application, septic permit record, and any building permits for past work.
6. Proof of payment: final figures, wire confirmation, title documents, and the insurance declarations. Ask the title company when you'll receive the final owner's and lender's title policies.

Anything you don't get goes on the unresolved items log. That log is the bridge to PA.12, and every row needs an owner and a date.

### PA.11.10 Who confirms what

**Confirm with:** your lender. It confirms which closing document you'll get and when, and why any rate, point, or fee changed. It also confirms the loan figures behind the wire amount.

**Confirm with:** the title company or closing attorney. They confirm the settlement figures, lien payoffs, recording, and title policies. They also confirm the exact wire amount and the wire instructions, verified by voice at a saved number.

**Confirm with:** your real estate attorney or agent. They confirm possession terms, walkthrough remedies, and anything in the documents that differs from your contract. Your attorney confirms the guaranty wording and when ownership legally transfers in your state.

**Confirm with:** your insurer. It confirms the policy effective date, named insured, and mortgagee clause.

**Confirm with:** your bank. It confirms its wire process, cutoff times, and who to call immediately if a wire needs to be stopped.
