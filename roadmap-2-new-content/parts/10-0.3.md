### 0.3.0 Learning outcome

By the end of this lesson you can add up the full cash an ownership deal requires, not just the down payment. You can compare it with the money you actually have, after keeping your own emergency savings. You can test whether you'd survive a delayed opening or a weak first season. And you finish with a one-page readiness scorecard. It sends you to one of four places. The first is PA.1, the next lesson, where you write your buy box: a written spec of what you'll buy. The second is better financing or liquidity first. The third is the Part One lease path: renting a place from a landlord and hosting it instead of buying, sometimes called arbitrage. The fourth is wait.

### 0.3.1 The down payment is the smallest question

**Meet the example.** I'll use one hypothetical couple all the way through. Maya and Chris are a two-earner household with $185,000 of combined income and credit scores around 740. They have $210,000 in liquid savings outside retirement accounts. They own their home, worth $520,000, with a $260,000 mortgage.

They're buying Cedar Ridge, a hypothetical three-bedroom house in a lake market about two hours from a metro area. The contract price is $385,000, with 25% down. It's a short-term rental (STR) they'll also use about 10 days a year. It isn't their main home.

They have two hypothetical loan quotes. The first is a **DSCR loan**. DSCR stands for debt service coverage ratio: the lender qualifies the loan mainly on the property's income compared with its payment. That quote is 7.25% with 1 point. Its monthly payment, with taxes, insurance, and dues (PITIA, explained in 0.3.4), is $2,557. The second is a **conventional loan**, qualified mainly on their household income and debts. It's 6.875% with no points, and its PITIA is $2,484.

A *point* is an upfront fee equal to 1% of the loan, paid to get a lower rate. In the course example, the house books about 212 nights a year across 61 stays. Its average nightly rate (ADR) is $300. Every Cedar Ridge figure is an assumption for teaching.

Most buyers start with one number: the down payment. For Cedar Ridge, 25% of $385,000 is $96,250. Maya and Chris have $210,000 in liquid savings outside retirement accounts. On that math alone, they look ready twice over.

That math leaves out most of the bill. An owned short-term rental needs money for closing, furnishing, repairs, and permits. It needs money for the months before the first guest. And it needs reserves, for the lender and for you. It also needs your own household to stay safe if the house has a bad year.

This lesson lays out every layer, then runs Maya and Chris through it. Learning "not yet" here is much cheaper than at a closing table.

### 0.3.2 The full cash stack, layer by layer

Think of the cash requirement as a stack of eight layers. The order doesn't change the total. You need all of them funded, or you are relying on the house to perform from day one.

| Layer | What it covers | Spent or held? |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Down payment | Your share of the price | Spent (becomes equity) |
| 2. Closing costs and points | Lender fees, title, escrow, prepaid items, discount points | Spent |
| 3. Furnishing and setup | Furniture, linens, kitchen, safety equipment, photos | Spent |
| 4. Initial repairs and safety punch list | Inspection findings you'll fix yourself after closing, plus work needed before guests | Spent |
| 5. Permit, registration, inspections | Local fees and specialist inspections | Spent |
| 6. Launch carrying costs | Loan payment, utilities, software, and vendor retainers before the first booking | Spent |
| 7. Property reserves | The lender's required reserves, which after closing become your operating cushion for the house | Held |
| 8. Personal emergency savings | Your household's own safety net, separate from the house | Held |

Layers 1 to 6 are money you will spend. Layers 7 and 8 are money you must still have after spending it.

A common failure is merging layers 7 and 8. A buyer counts the same dollars as the house's reserve and the family's emergency fund. Then the water heater fails in the same month a paycheck is late, and there's only one pile of cash for two problems.

### 0.3.3 Where the money can come from, and the limits on each source

Money for the stack can come from several places. Each has limits, and your lender may treat them differently. For this lesson, we count only liquid savings.

**Liquid savings.** Checking, savings, and taxable brokerage accounts. This is the cleanest source. Lenders usually want to see where it came from and how long it has been there. Ask your lender what documentation they need for large recent deposits.

**Retirement accounts.** Maya and Chris's $210,000 excludes retirement accounts. Some lenders may count part of a retirement balance toward reserves, and withdrawals can carry taxes and penalties. Treat this as a question for your lender and CPA, not as spendable cash.

**Home equity.** Maya and Chris own their primary home. It's worth $520,000 with a $260,000 mortgage balance, so they have $260,000 of equity on paper. A home equity line of credit (HELOC) lets you borrow against it. The CFPB notes that HELOCs usually carry a variable rate, so payments can change, and your home is the collateral. If you can't repay, you could lose your home ([CFPB, What is a HELOC?](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-home-equity-line-of-credit-heloc-en-107/)). A HELOC is borrowed money, not savings. It adds a payment to your household budget. Ask the lender whether HELOC funds can go toward a down payment or count as reserves. Also ask how the new payment affects your qualification. OP.11 covers HELOCs for scaling in more depth.

**Gifts or partners.** Lenders have rules on gift funds and co-borrowers. If a partner puts in money, ownership and loan paperwork must match. PA.10 (entity and tax structure) covers that.

**Seller concessions.** A seller may pay part of your closing costs. That's a negotiation item for PA.8 (offers and negotiation), not something to count on now.

If the stack only works with borrowed money, record that as a finding.

### 0.3.4 Reserves: the lender's minimum is not your plan

Fannie Mae's Selling Guide measures reserves in months of the new loan's payment. That payment is principal, interest, taxes, insurance, and association dues (PITIA). The question is how many months your financial assets could cover ([Fannie Mae Selling Guide, B3-4.1-01 Minimum Reserve Requirements](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-4.1-01/minimum-reserve-requirements)).

A few points from that section, as they apply to a buyer like Maya and Chris:

- There is no minimum reserve requirement for a one-unit principal residence. Second homes and investment properties do have requirements. Cedar Ridge isn't Maya and Chris's principal residence, so the no-minimum rule doesn't apply to it. Their lender decides which category it falls in.
- If you already own other financed properties besides your home, lenders can require extra reserves. The add-on leaves out the house you're buying and your principal residence. For Maya and Chris, that leaves nothing, so the add-on is likely zero. Their lender confirms.

The DSCR quote in the course example is not a Fannie Mae loan. Each DSCR lender publishes or quotes its own reserve rule. For teaching, we assume the DSCR lender requires **6 months of PITIA (assumption)**. On the DSCR quote that is 6 x $2,557 = $15,342. We use the same 6 months (assumption) on the conventional quote so the two are comparable: 6 x $2,484 = $14,904. Get the real number from each lender in writing. PA.6 and PA.7 go deeper.

After closing, the lender stops watching. The reserve becomes your operating cushion for the house. The lender's number is a floor to get the loan. It is not a plan for a bad season. Maya and Chris will set their own reserve target in the worked example.

### 0.3.5 Credit, eligibility, and debt capacity

Besides cash, three things shape whether a lender will lend: credit, loan type, and debt capacity. You can check all three before you choose a house.

**Credit.** Maya and Chris have scores around 740. Each lender sets its own minimum and prices its rate by score. Kiavi's DSCR guide, for example, says the lender looks at credit score and down payment, not only the property's coverage ratio ([Kiavi DSCR guide](https://www.kiavi.com/the-complete-guide-to-dscr-rental-property-loans)). That is one lender's approach, not a market standard.

**Eligibility of the loan type.** A DSCR loan qualifies mainly on the property's income compared with its payment. A conventional loan qualifies mainly on your household income and debts. Fannie Mae's Selling Guide has a section on short-term rental income from the property being bought ([Fannie Mae Selling Guide, B3-3.8-03](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-3.8-03/rental-income-subject-property-short-term-rental)). Confirm the current version with your lender. Whether a particular lender will count projected or actual STR income for your loan is their call. PA.7 (DSCR versus conventional) compares the two paths.

**Debt capacity.** A conventional lender compares your monthly debts with your income (the debt-to-income ratio, or DTI). Here is a rough illustration for Maya and Chris. **All figures are assumptions** except their income and the Cedar Ridge PITIA.

| Line | Monthly |
|---|---:|
| Gross income ($185,000 / 12) | $15,417 |
| Primary home payment (assumption) | $2,400 |
| Other debts: cars, cards (assumption) | $600 |
| Cedar Ridge PITIA, conventional quote | $2,484 |
| **Total debts** | **$5,484** |
| **DTI with no STR income counted** | **35.6%** |

The lender sets the maximum ratio and decides how much rental income, if any, to credit. So 35.6% isn't good or bad on its own. Ask each lender for its maximum DTI in writing and write it next to yours. This rough check only tells you whether a conventional path is worth asking about. (For your own household budget, the DSCR payment of $2,557 gives 36.0%.)

A DSCR lender looks at a different number: the property's income divided by its PITIA, called the coverage ratio. In the course example, Cedar Ridge's ratio on the DSCR quote runs from 1.18 to 2.42. It depends on how the lender defines income. The lender's written definition decides which one counts, and PA.6 (DSCR loans) walks through the math. Ask each DSCR lender for its minimum ratio in writing.

### 0.3.6 A delayed opening or a weak first season

Two things go wrong often enough that you should budget for both.

**Delayed opening.** Permits take longer than expected. Furniture arrives late. A contractor misses a week. Every month between closing and the first guest costs the full carrying cost with no revenue. For Cedar Ridge on the DSCR quote, that is $3,257 a month. It's the $2,557 PITIA plus the course example's $450 of utilities, $100 of software, and $150 handyman retainer.

The base stack includes two months of carrying costs (assumption), or $6,514. A three-month delay beyond that adds another 3 x $3,257 = $9,771. Paid from the 6-month property reserve of $15,342, that would leave $5,571.

**Weak first season.** A new listing has no reviews and may book below its long-run potential. The course example's weak case has an ADR of $265 at 50% occupancy (the share of nights booked). On the DSCR quote it loses $5,475 a year, after the loan payment and all operating costs. That is about $456 a month. This is the pre-diligence figure, and PA.9 revisits it.

Now put the two together. A 3-month delay takes $9,771 of the reserve, leaving $5,571. One weak year then takes $5,475. Together they use $15,246 of the $15,342 reserve, leaving $96. Plan for that pairing. It's the kind of pairing that can force a sale at a bad time.

### 0.3.7 Time, involvement, and risk tolerance

Cash isn't the only resource. Owning also takes time and a stomach for variance.

**Time.** In the course example's base case, Maya and Chris self-manage with a local cleaner and an on-call handyman. Assume they spend 1.5 hours per stay on messages, scheduling, and turnover checks (assumption). Add 4 hours a month on bookkeeping, pricing, and vendor calls (assumption). With the example's 61 stays a year, that is 91.5 + 48 = about 140 hours a year, or 2.7 hours a week (assumption). Some weeks will take much more: a lockout at 11 p.m., a broken hot water heater on a Saturday.

That time estimate also matters for taxes. A rental can show a loss on paper for tax purposes. Whether that loss is usable against your other income can depend on *material participation*. That's a set of IRS tests about how involved you are, and lesson 0.2 covers it. Publication 925's tests look at hours, including how your hours compare with those of other individuals, such as a cleaner ([IRS Publication 925](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p925)). If the cleaner spends 4 hours on each of 61 turnovers (assumption), that is 244 hours, more than the owners' 140. Your CPA confirms how your hours count.

**Involvement choice.** If you don't want to spend those hours, the alternative is a co-host or manager. In 0.2, a full-service co-host charging 18% of booking revenue turned Cedar Ridge's pre-diligence base-case cash flow from $5,572 to -$7,798 a year. Decide now which kind of owner you'll be, because it changes whether the deal works.

**Personal use.** Maya and Chris plan about 10 personal days a year. IRS Publication 527 treats a dwelling as used as a home if personal use passes a threshold. The threshold is the greater of 14 days or 10% of fair-rental days. It also requires dividing expenses by days ([IRS Publication 527](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p527)). Here I count the example's 212 rented nights as fair-rental days, and your CPA confirms the count. On that basis the threshold is 21.2 days, so 10 days is below it. There is also a revenue cost. If those 10 nights would have booked at the $300 base ADR, they're worth up to $3,000 of nightly revenue (assumption). Plan your own stays for slower weeks.

**Risk tolerance.** Lesson 0.2 ran a five-year test. It added five years of cash flow to the sale proceeds, then compared the total with the $142,688 they put in. In the base case they came out $27,732 ahead before tax. In the weak case they came out $27,503 behind. Both of you should look at the second number and say out loud whether you could live with it. If the honest answer is no, that's a readiness result, not a failure.

### 0.3.8 Worked example: Maya and Chris's full cash stack

Maya and Chris have $210,000 in liquid savings. Their household's essential spending is $8,500 a month (assumption), and they want to keep 6 months of it as personal emergency savings (assumption): $51,000.

They also set their own property-reserve target (assumption). It has to survive the pairing from 0.3.6: a 3-month delay plus one weak year. That's $9,771 + $5,475 = $15,246. The 6-month lender figure of $15,342 just covers it, with $96 to spare. So they keep $15,342 as both the lender floor and their own target.

Here is the stack on each quote. Figures without a tag come from the course's example house file. New figures are marked (assumption).

| Layer | DSCR quote | Conventional quote |
|---|---:|---:|
| Down payment (25%) | $96,250 | $96,250 |
| Closing costs incl. points | $14,438 | $11,550 |
| **Cash to close** | **$110,688** | **$107,800** |
| Furnishing and setup | $32,000 | $32,000 |
| Initial repairs and safety punch list (assumption) | $4,000 | $4,000 |
| Inspections: general, septic, specialist (assumption) | $1,500 | $1,500 |
| Permit and registration fees (assumption) | $600 | $600 |
| Launch carrying costs, 2 months (assumption) | $6,514 | $6,368 |
| **Total spent (layers 1 to 6)** | **$155,302** | **$152,268** |
| Property reserve, 6 months PITIA (assumption) | $15,342 | $14,904 |
| Personal emergency savings, 6 months (assumption) | $51,000 | $51,000 |
| **Full stack** | **$221,644** | **$218,172** |
| Liquid savings | $210,000 | $210,000 |
| **Surplus / gap** | **-$11,644** | **-$8,172** |

Step by step for the DSCR column. Spent layers: $110,688 + $32,000 + $4,000 + $1,500 + $600 + $6,514 = $155,302. Add the $15,342 property reserve and the $51,000 emergency fund: $221,644. Subtract from $210,000: a gap of $11,644.

Two readings of the same table:

- **The lender's view.** After spending $155,302, Maya and Chris would still hold $54,698. That's about 21 months of the DSCR PITIA, well above a 6-month reserve rule. On paper, a lender may say yes.
- **Their own plan.** They can't fund the house's reserve and their own emergency savings at the same time. They are $11,644 short on the DSCR quote and $8,172 short on the conventional quote.

This is the gap the down payment check hides.

Options to close the gap, in the order I'd look at them:

1. **Time.** If they save $3,500 a month (assumption), the DSCR gap closes in about 3.3 months, the conventional gap in about 2.3 months. Searching, underwriting, and closing take a while anyway, so time is often the cheapest fix.
2. **Financing.** The conventional quote needs $2,888 less at closing (it has no point) and has a lower payment, if they qualify. That shrinks the gap to $8,172; it doesn't close it. PA.7 compares the two loans.
3. **Price.** A lower price reduces the down payment and closing costs. PA.1 turns this into a hard cap.
4. **Furnishing plan.** A phased furnishing plan may cut the up-front budget. OP.6 (furnishing) covers this without cutting safety or listing accuracy.

What they should **not** do: shrink their emergency fund to make the table balance, or count a HELOC as savings.

### 0.3.9 The one-page readiness scorecard

Fill this in before you shop. Four tasks get you there.

1. Total your liquid savings, leaving out retirement accounts and home equity.
2. Write your essential monthly spending and multiply it by the months of emergency savings you'll keep.
3. Fill in the eight-layer stack for one realistic price, and write down the surplus or gap. Where you don't have a number yet, use Maya and Chris's line as a placeholder and mark it (assumption). Scale it to your price where it's a percentage of price, like the down payment and closing costs. Then replace each assumption using the "Who confirms what" list in 0.3.11.
4. Score all 13 lines below, with evidence, and read off your outcome.

The first two tasks take minutes. The stack takes longer until you have lender numbers.

Score each line Green, Yellow, or Red. Green clears the line with room. Yellow clears it only with a written condition and a date. Red doesn't clear it now. Some scores rest on a lender figure you don't have in writing yet. Mark those "lender to confirm" and put a confirm-by date in the Evidence column. The outcome rules follow the table.

**Readiness scorecard** (one page)

Name(s): ____________ Date: ____________ Quote used: DSCR / Conventional / Other ____________

| # | Check | Green | Yellow | Red | Your score | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Full cash stack (0.3.8) from liquid savings | Surplus of $0 or more | Gap you can close with normal saving within 6 months | Larger gap, or funded only with borrowed money | | |
| 2 | Personal emergency savings kept intact after closing | 6+ months of essential spending | 3 to 6 months | Under 3 months | | |
| 3 | Property reserve after closing | At or above the lender's rule and your own target | At lender rule only | Below lender rule | | |
| 4 | Credit | At or above the lender's written minimum, with room | At the minimum | Below the minimum | | |
| 5 | Lender fit | Written pre-qualification or term sheet for at least one loan path | Verbal quote or pre-qualification only, with written quotes scheduled in PA.6 and PA.7 | None, or declined | | |
| 6 | Debt capacity (conventional) or property coverage (DSCR) | Inside the lender's stated limit (ask the lender for its maximum DTI or minimum coverage ratio, in writing) | Near the limit | Outside it | | |
| 7 | Delayed opening: 3 extra months absorbed | From property reserve, emergency fund untouched | Needs part of emergency fund | Cannot be absorbed | | |
| 8 | Weak first season | Weak-case loss covered 2+ years by property reserve | 1 to 2 years | Under 1 year | | |
| 9 | Time available | Weekly hours available exceed the plan's estimate, plus a local backup | Just enough | Not enough, and no budget for a co-host | | |
| 10 | Involvement choice tested | Deal still works with your chosen management model (0.2) | Works only if you self-manage and you're unsure you will | Doesn't work with the model you'll actually use | | |
| 11 | Risk tolerance | Both owners accept the five-year weak-case result in writing | One owner unsure | Either owner can't accept it | | |
| 12 | Personal use plan | Below the Pub 527 threshold, or reviewed by CPA | Plan unclear | Heavy personal use, not reviewed | | |
| 13 | Lease path considered | You compared owning with leasing (lesson 0.2) and chose owning for a stated reason | Not compared | Leasing clearly fits better and you'd still buy | | |

The cut-offs in this scorecard are teaching defaults (assumption), not lender or industry rules. That covers the 6-month saving window, the 3- and 6-month emergency bands, and the 1- and 2-year weak-season bands. It also covers the rescore timing in the outcome rules below. Set your own if your situation calls for it.

**Outcome rules**

Check the rows from top to bottom. The first row that matches your scores decides where you go.

| Result | Where you go next |
|---|---|
| Two or more Reds anywhere | **Wait.** Rescore in six months. |
| One Red, on line 9, 10, 11, or 12 | **Wait.** Fix the time, risk, or personal-use question before money is at stake. If time is the only issue, check whether a co-host still leaves the deal positive. |
| One Red, on line 1, 2, or 13 | **Use the lease path (Part One).** A lease needs far less cash. Alder Street, the course's example leased apartment, needed $20,370 on day one. Start with lesson 1.3. |
| One Red, on any of lines 3 to 8 | **Improve financing or liquidity first.** Get written quotes (PA.6, PA.7), pay down debt, or save. Rescore in 60 to 90 days. |
| No Reds, all Green | **Proceed to PA.1.** |
| No Reds, and every Yellow has a written condition and a date | **Proceed to PA.1 with conditions.** Write each condition into your buy box: no offer until it's met. |
| No Reds, but a Yellow has no condition or date | **Wait.** Write the condition and the date, then rescore. |

### 0.3.11 Who confirms what

**Confirm with:** each lender, in writing, the reserve months and how they're calculated, the minimum credit score, and the down payment. Also confirm acceptable sources of funds (HELOC funds, gifts, retirement assets) and how rental income is counted.

**Confirm with:** your CPA the tax effect of retirement withdrawals, how your hours count for material participation, and how personal-use days are treated.

**Confirm with:** your insurance agent the premium and when it's due, so layer 2 or layer 6 of the stack is accurate.

**Confirm with:** the local permit office the fees and processing time, so the launch carrying-cost assumption is realistic.
