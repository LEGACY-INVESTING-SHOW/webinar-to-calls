Slide 2 · Write down who books the house before you open a single catalog

Most furnishing mistakes start in a store. Someone falls for a sofa, then makes the house fit the sofa. I want you to reverse that.

Write down who books this house and what they'll do there. Then buy for that person. The profile has four parts. The fourth one, complaints, comes from reading reviews of the comparable set in Sam's PA.4 package. Those reviews tell you what guests in this market already dislike.

Then write the other list: what this house is not for. If you won't allow parties, you don't need a sound system. If the lower level only has stairs, you can't imply step-free access.

Every accessibility claim has to be true on the day a guest arrives. Airbnb's ground rules for hosts say the listing should describe the home and the amenities available from check-in to checkout. So the guest profile also protects you from promising things the house can't deliver.

OP.6 · Furnishing an Owned Property§ OP.6.23 / 20
Slide 3 · Set the sleeping layout before you buy, and count it by bed, not by room

Guests check the sleeping layout twice. It's the one number they read closely, because it decides whether their group fits. It also sets your linen count and how long every turnover takes.

So I set it first, before any purchase. And I count sleeping spots by bed. A three-bedroom house can sleep six or twelve depending on what's in the rooms.

Airbnb's sleeping arrangements article says a bunk bed counts as 2 beds in the bed count on the listing. Keep that in mind when you read the Cedar Ridge numbers in a minute.

Before you lock the layout, run three checks. Occupancy limits and permit conditions should already be in your PA.9 notes. Egress means every sleeping room has a fire exit your building or fire official accepts. And insurance: if the policy says one guest count and the listing says another, that gap can become a claim problem. Settle all three before the bunk beds get ordered.

OP.6 · Furnishing an Owned Property§ OP.6.34 / 20
Slide 4 · Work space, storage, and outdoor gear get skipped in budgets, then show up in reviews

Three areas get left out of most furnishing budgets. Then they appear in reviews.

The first is the work space. If you claim a dedicated workspace, a guest expects a desk, a chair, a lamp, and outlets they can reach. And the internet has to hold a video call. Test the speed at the desk itself, not in the kitchen, and write the result in the handoff packet.

The second is storage, and it means two things. Guests need somewhere to put a suitcase and hang clothes for a four-night stay. The owner needs a locked place for personal items, spare inventory, and cleaning stock. Mix those two and you get the classic review: closets full of the owner's stuff.

The third is outdoor use. A lake house earns its rate on the deck and the water. That's also where many injuries happen. Every outdoor item is a maintenance job and an insurance question. If you can't keep it right every turnover, leave it out of the listing.

Safety equipment is the first line in the budget, and CPSC guidance sets the floor

CPSC vacation rental safety tips. Consumer guidance, not your local code. Treat it as a planning floor and ask the fire marshal.

  • Smoke alarms on every level, outside each sleeping area, and inside every bedroom
  • CO alarms on every level, outside sleeping areas
  • At least one fire extinguisher
  • TVs and furniture anchored; no dangling window-covering cords
  • Pools and spas: compliant drain covers, a 4-foot fence, self-closing gates
  • Cribs that meet CPSC standards, with a bare sleep space
  • Cleaning supplies locked or out of children's reach
  • Recall check on child products at SaferProducts.gov

Slide 5 · Safety equipment is the first line in the budget, and CPSC guidance sets the floor

Safety goes first in the budget, not last. Nothing gets cut from this layer later.

Airbnb's safety requirements say hosts must state whether smoke and carbon monoxide alarms are present. If they are, they must work and you must test them regularly. The same page says you must deal with fire and electrical hazards. That covers blocked exits, dead smoke detectors, exposed wiring, and poorly kept hot tubs, gas appliances, and furnaces. You're responsible for any area guests can use.

The list on this slide comes from the Consumer Product Safety Commission's vacation rental safety tips. It covers alarms, extinguishers, anchoring, pools, cribs, and cleaning supplies.

This is consumer guidance, and your local code may ask for more. Your city, county, or state may require more, such as alarms that are wired to each other. So I treat this list as a planning floor, and I ask the fire marshal what this address needs.

Slide 6 · Cameras stay outside and disclosed, and nothing that records goes indoors

Security devices come next, because they touch privacy and listing accuracy at the same time.

Airbnb's device rules ban security cameras and recording devices that monitor indoor spaces. That holds even if the device is turned off. Hidden cameras are prohibited everywhere.

Exterior cameras are allowed, with conditions. The listing has to disclose where they are. They can't point into the house. And they can't cover areas where people expect more privacy, like an enclosed outdoor shower or a sauna.

Noise monitors that only measure sound level are allowed in common spaces. They have to be disclosed, and they stay out of bedrooms, bathrooms, and sleeping areas.

So at Cedar Ridge the plan is simple. If Maya and Chris want a camera, they buy a doorbell camera. It goes outside, and its location goes into the listing. The noise monitor sits in a common area and gets disclosed. Check Airbnb's current page before you publish, since these rules change.

Every item has three prices: purchase, turnover minutes, and replacement

  • Buy performance or washable fabrics, with removable covers, in the living room
  • Put waterproof encasements on every mattress on day one
  • Buy white or one-color bedding in one line, so sets mix and match
  • Choose dishware you can rebuy by the piece, and keep spares on a shelf
  • Skip glass tabletops and lamps that need an app to turn on
  • Cut decorative clutter: twelve pillows add time to every turnover
Why minutes cost money

The cleaner is paid per turnover. Extra minutes turn into rushed work or a higher rate later.

OP.6 · Furnishing an Owned Property§ OP.6.57 / 20
Slide 7 · Every item has three prices: purchase, turnover minutes, and replacement

Every item you buy has three prices. There's the purchase price. There's the minutes it adds to each turnover. And there's the cost of replacing it. The cheapest sofa is often the most expensive one by year three.

These buying rules hold up. Washable fabrics and removable covers let the cleaner handle a spill without a service call. Encasements go on every mattress on the first day. A stained mattress is a replacement. A stained encasement is a wash.

One line of white or one-color bedding means any sheet fits any bed of that size. Dishware you can rebuy by the piece means one broken plate doesn't mean a new set.

Then think in cleaning minutes. Twelve decorative pillows get fluffed and placed after every stay. The cleaner is paid per turnover, not per hour. So extra minutes either get rushed or show up later as a higher rate. Buy for the turnover, not the photo.

A furnishing plan looks like shopping. But five parts of it are someone else's decision.

The licensing office or building department confirms maximum occupancy, bedroom egress, and any permit condition tied to beds or guest count. If they say six, the bunk room plan changes.

The fire marshal confirms alarm type, placement, interconnection, and extinguishers. The CPSC list we used is a floor. The marshal sets the actual count for this address.

The insurer confirms the listed guest count, the bunks, the grill, fire features, water access, and any pool or hot tub. Bring the room inventory to that call.

The CPA confirms how furnishings, linens, supplies, delivery, and assembly are classified. Ask whether B6's 5-year treatment of the full $32,000 holds for your purchases.

And Airbnb's help pages change. Check the device rules and listing accuracy pages again right before you publish.

Four actions this week. Each one takes under an hour.

Start with the guest. Pull three comparable listings from your PA.4 package and read their reviews. Write down who books, how many sleep over, what they do, and what they complain about. Then write what your house is not for.

Next, call the licensing office. Ask for the maximum occupancy at your address and any permit condition on guest count. Then write your bed list by room and type.

Third, open the five-layer budget and put in your underwriting number. Rough in each layer and check that contingency is at least 3%.

Fourth, ask the fire marshal's office about alarm placement for your address.

The house is furnished and the listing facts are ready. Now someone has to keep it that way after every stay. That's OP.7. It decides who cleans, restocks, inspects, answers guests, and holds a key. It also decides who can approve spending, like the $200 replacement limit we set here. Any single replacement over $200 needs owner approval, and OP.8 writes that into the SOP.
