Price all four exits before you pick one

Slide 1 · Price all four exits before you pick one

This is the last lesson in the STR Concierge track. It's about what to do next with a property you own.

You have four options. You can hold it, refinance it, sell it, or sell it through a 1031 exchange. By the end of this lesson you'll put those four side by side on after-tax cash, time, future debt, ongoing work, and opportunity cost.

I'm not going to calculate your tax. I'm going to show you how the gain is built, so you can collect the right records and read your CPA's answer. We'll use Cedar Ridge at the end of year 5.

Slide 2 · Every exit asks where your equity does the most

Every exit decision comes down to one question. Is the equity in this property doing more for you here than it would somewhere else?

Equity is market value minus the loan balance, minus the costs of getting it out. Holding keeps it in the house. Refinancing pulls some out as a new loan. Selling turns it into cash, minus taxes. A 1031 exchange moves it into another investment property and may defer the tax.

No option wins on after-tax cash, time, debt, work, and opportunity cost all at once. You're choosing a trade-off you can live with. Rerun this whenever your 90-day or annual review from Sam's OP.10 shows a real change.

Slide 3 · Hold, refinance, and sell fit different situations

Holding is the default. It needs no transaction, but it has a cost you don't see on a statement. That's opportunity cost: what the same equity could earn somewhere safer.

Hold when the property still covers its costs in a weak season, the capital repairs are funded, and the work is manageable. It also makes sense when a sale would trigger a big tax bill you'd rather defer.

Refinance when you have a specific, better use for the cash and the property can carry the bigger loan in a weak year. It adds debt, but nothing is sold, so there's no tax on a gain.

Sell when the property no longer fits your PA.1 goals, local rules have tightened, a big repair won't pay back, or the equity has a clearly better use.

Slide 4 · Depreciation shrinks your basis, and that grows your gain

You don't need to calculate your own tax. You need to know how it's built.

Gain is the amount realized minus your adjusted basis. Amount realized is roughly the sale price minus selling costs. Adjusted basis starts with what you paid, goes up with improvements, and goes down with depreciation.

If you didn't claim all the depreciation you were entitled to, the IRS still reduces your basis by the full amount. The phrase is allowed or allowable. Skipping it only loses you the deduction.

Then your CPA splits the gain. Furniture and many fast-depreciated parts are generally section 1245 property. Gain on them is taxed as ordinary income, up to the amount of depreciation. Gain from straight-line depreciation on the building is unrecaptured section 1250 gain, taxed at a maximum 25%. The rest is section 1231 gain.

Slide 5 · Four gates decide whether an exchange can work

A 1031 exchange lets you sell one investment property, buy another, and defer the gain. Deferred means the tax waits.

Gate one: both properties are real property held for business or investment. Furniture generally doesn't qualify, so it sits outside the exchange.

Gate two: personal use. If you stay there too, Rev. Proc. 2008-16 has a safe harbor. I couldn't confirm its exact day counts from the IRS text, so your CPA checks them.

Gate three: the qualified intermediary, who holds your sale money. Your own agent, broker, accountant, attorney, or employee can't serve. Neither can anyone who was in those roles for you in the past two years. Ask how funds are held and who signs withdrawals.

Gate four is the clock. Identify in writing by day 45. Receive the replacement by day 180 or your return due date, whichever comes first.

Slide 7 · A refinance pulls out $53,853 but cuts cash flow to $2,085

Now suppose they refinance at year 5 instead. Same terms as earlier in the course: 75% loan-to-value, 2.5% costs, and a 7.0% rate. All assumptions.

The new loan is $334,741. Pay off the $272,519 balance and $8,369 of costs, and they walk away with $53,853. They keep the house, and nothing is sold, so there's no tax on a gain.

The new payment is $26,724 a year. Base cash flow drops from $5,172 to $2,085. A weak year now costs them $8,962 instead of $5,875.

So the refinance trades a thinner margin for cash now. If they don't have a specific, better use for the $53,853, they've just taken on more debt.

Slide 8 · A cost-seg study nearly doubles Cedar Ridge's gain

Now the sale, built two ways. The amount realized is the same in both: $446,321 minus $31,242 of selling costs, or $415,079. What changes is the adjusted basis.

In the first case there's no study. They took bonus depreciation on the $32,000 of furniture and straight-line on the building. Total depreciation is $72,105, and the gain is $64,184.

In the second, a cost-segregation study moved $68,816 of building parts into faster property, deducted in year one. Total depreciation is $132,096, and the gain is $124,175.

Same house, same sale price. The depreciation choice nearly doubles the gain. I simplified the building to a 39-year life. Your CPA decides the real life and timing.

Slide 10 · An October 20, 2031 sale starts a 180-day clock

Now the 1031 path. Suppose they sell on October 20, 2031. The dates are assumptions.

Day 45 is December 4, 2031. That's the written identification deadline. Day 180 is April 17, 2032. If their 2031 return is due before that, the earlier date is the real deadline unless they file an extension. Their CPA confirms which applies.

If it qualifies, the gain on the real property is deferred. The furniture sits outside the exchange, so the CPA handles that $6,000 separately. Their 10 personal-use days a year go to the CPA too.

The replacement needs its own SR.1 permission check, underwriting, financing, and team, all inside 180 days. Have the shortlist ready before the sale closes.

Slide 11 · The numbers show the trade-offs but don't pick for you

Here's how the four options line up for Maya and Chris.

Hold gives them no cash now, keeps $272,519 of debt, and keeps the $5,172 base and minus $5,875 weak case. Refinance gives them $53,853 now on $334,741 of debt, with $2,085 base and minus $8,962 weak.

Notice that both lose money in a weak year. Holding only works with reserves for about $5,875 of losses. A refinance needs about $8,962.

Sell gives them $127,902 or $114,686 after tax, depending on how they depreciated. No debt, no work. The exchange rolls about $142,560 into a new property with a new loan, and the work restarts.

Which is right depends on their PA.1 goals and their worksheet.

Slide 12 · Fill one column per option, and date every figure

Your worksheet has three parts. Part A is your starting facts: value, loan balance or payoff quote, repairs due, selling costs, trailing-12 NOI, personal-use days, and depreciation to date from your returns.

Part B is this slide. One column for each option: hold, refinance, sell, and exchange. Fill in each line, and write the source and date next to every figure. Then divide annual base cash flow by the Sell column's cash after tax. That's your return on the equity you're choosing to leave in.

Part C is the 1031 checklist, only if you pick the exchange. Start it before the sale closes, because it runs to day 180.

End with one decision, a one-sentence reason, a recheck date, and who approved it.

Slide 13 · An exit is only as good as four records

This is the last lesson in STR Concierge, so look at where the worksheet's inputs came from. Every one comes from work you've already done. That's your buy box, your weak-season test, your depreciation choices with your CPA, your maintenance log, and your monthly and quarterly reviews.

So keep four things from day one. Closing documents. Every depreciation schedule your CPA files. Receipts for improvements and repairs. And a calendar of rental and personal-use days.

With those, any of the four options is a short conversation. Without them, even a good sale turns into a reconstruction project.
