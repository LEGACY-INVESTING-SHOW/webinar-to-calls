Work on what Airbnb publishes, not on rumors

Slide 1 · Work on what Airbnb publishes, not on rumors

After this lesson you can tell what Airbnb actually says affects search apart from claims about "the algorithm." You'll map each factor to something you control, set up guest checkpoints, write a review request that follows the rules, and start a monthly tracker.

Airbnb doesn't publish whether or how AI changes its ranking. So I stick to what it does publish, and to the guest experience that shows up in reviews.

The example house is Cedar Ridge, a hypothetical lake rental owned by Maya and Chris. Maya answers guest messages, so she's the message owner. Chris is her backup and gets the repair quotes. Sam, the other instructor, covers listing setup in OP.1 and OP.2 and pricing in OP.5. This lesson covers the parts guests experience and then write about.

Airbnb names four main search factors

  • Quality: content, reviews, communication
  • Popularity: saves, bookings, messages
  • Price: total price against nearby listings
  • Location: places guests like to stay
Slide 2 · Airbnb names four main search factors

This is what Airbnb's search page says. I checked it in September 2026, and it changes, so reread it before you rely on it.

Four factors heavily influence how a listing appears. Quality covers photos and videos, ratings and reviews, host-guest communication, and listing details. Popularity is how often guests save the listing to a wishlist, book it, and message the host. Price is the total price against nearby listings. Listings priced below comparable ones tend to rank higher. And listings in places guests like to stay tend to rank higher.

The page adds two points. The system encourages variety, so guests see different hosts and prices. And availability matters. More open dates make a listing more likely to show up, and flexible stay lengths help it fit more trips.

Notice what's missing. No weights, and no special field for AI. If someone sells you a trick, ask for the Airbnb page that supports it.

Airbnb's ground rules are what guests write reviews about

  1. Accuracy: the listing matches the home
  2. Commitment: honor bookings, reliable check-in
  3. Communication: reachable before and during stays
  4. Cleanliness: clean well between every stay
Slide 3 · Airbnb's ground rules are what guests write reviews about

Airbnb's ground rules for home hosts cover four things.

Accuracy: the listing describes the home and its amenities, and you change a booking only with the guest's consent. Commitment: honor accepted reservations, give a reliable check-in, and cancel only for valid reasons outside your control. Communication: the host or co-host, someone who helps run the listing, is available before and during stays. Cleanliness: clean between every stay to a high standard.

Airbnb enforces these. That can mean education, warnings, or removing content that misrepresents the listing.

Now look at the list again. Those are the same things guests tend to write about in reviews. So the ground rules are the floor, and reviews measure how far above it you land. OP.6 and OP.8 were built to clear this floor.

A guest journey is the run of moments where a guest decides whether the stay is going well. I plan a checkpoint at each one, so the team checks something instead of waiting for a complaint. Maya runs most of them.

The arrival-evening message matters most. Problems caught that night get fixed during the stay. Problems found after checkout go straight into a review.

A question guests ask twice means the house needs a fix.

Slide 6 · Log every guest question and fix the repeats

The friction log is the simplest tool in this lesson. Every time a guest asks a question, mark it.

"Where's the water shutoff?" asked once is harmless. Asked twice, the house manual needs a line. "How do the bunk lights work?" asked twice means the lights need a label.

Many bad reviews start as a repeated question nobody fixed. A later guest may skip the question and put it in the review instead.

So the owners read the log every week and look for repeats. A repeat gets a fix, with a name and a date. That twice rule shows up again in the tracker and in the Cedar Ridge bunk room decision.

Keep review requests free of trades, rewards, and pressure

  • No rewards or discounts for reviews
  • No trading reviews for refunds or deposits
  • No pressure and no threats
  • Never name a star rating
Slide 7 · Keep review requests free of trades, rewards, and pressure

After checkout, Airbnb prompts guest and host to review each other. Each side has 14 days.

Airbnb's reviews policy sets the limits on how you ask. Nobody may coordinate with, manipulate, extort, incentivize, or pressure another person to shape a review. Reviews can't be given or withheld for something of value: a discount, a refund, a reciprocal review, or a promise not to take negative action. And neither side may threaten a bad review to get a refund or other compensation.

The last bullet is my rule, a step past the policy text. Don't ask for five stars at all. Naming the rating reads as pressure, and the policy prohibits pressure.

The same rules apply to your review of the guest. Write it on the facts.

"Hi [first name], thanks for staying at Cedar Ridge. If you have a few minutes, we'd appreciate an honest review on Airbnb. If anything wasn't right, please tell us directly too, so we can fix it for the next guests."

Slide 8 · Send this two or three days after checkout

Here's a request that works within the rules. Send it two or three days after checkout. That gap lets the cleaner's notes come in, so you know about any problem before you ask.

It thanks the guest, invites an honest review, and asks them to tell you directly if anything was wrong. That gives an unhappy guest a place to say it, and it gives you something to fix. Private feedback comes in addition to the review, never instead of it.

Keep any goodwill gesture away from the request. In OP.8, Maya's $100 internet credit went out during the stay, with no mention of reviews. Keep it that way.

Slide 9 · Answer criticism for future guests, after the fix is done

A public response shows on the listing next to the review. So you're writing for future guests, not arguing with the past one.

Wait a day. That's my house rule, not Airbnb's. Check the facts: turnover photos, the message thread, the friction log. Then sort it. Fair and fixable, like a hot bunk room: say what you've fixed. Fair but a known trade-off, like being far from town: point to where the listing says so. Factually wrong: state the fact calmly.

Never claim a fix that isn't done. Save disputes for reviews that break the policy, and know a dispute doesn't guarantee removal. A fair three-star about a hot bunk room doesn't qualify.

Slide 10 · Track what you can act on, every month

Track what you can act on, monthly. Each metric gets a definition, a source, a target, and an owner. These targets are Cedar Ridge house rules, not Airbnb thresholds. Set your own.

First response time aims for a median under 15 minutes from 7 a.m. to 10 p.m., with every reply in those hours under 60. After 10 p.m., an auto-reply gives the after-hours number.

Any complaint reason seen twice in a quarter gets a fix, an owner, and a date. Read every review below five stars. Anything below four also gets a check of the photos and thread within 48 hours.

Review rate has no target, because pushing it risks pressuring guests. Record repeat-stay signals too, and thank people.

Slide 11 · A 4.6 average can hide a real problem

Here's the Cedar Ridge tracker at day 90. These are teaching numbers.

The base case expects 61 stays a year. In 90 days that's about 15 stays. If about 70% of guests leave a review, that's about 10 reviews. So each review is a tenth of what a new guest reads.

Seven are five-star, two are four-star, and one is three-star. The average is 4.6. That looks fine.

So look past the 4.6 to what the complaints tell you to fix.

Run your review request through five checks

  • Offers nothing of value
  • Doesn't mention a specific rating
  • No link to a refund, deposit, discount, or future stay
  • Invites private feedback about problems
  • Sent after checkout, not tied to a gesture
Slide 13 · Run your review request through five checks

The exercise has three parts in the written lesson. Part A maps each search factor to one monthly check and a name. Part C is the monthly tracker, with a fix list.

Part B is your review request. Write it, then run it through these five checks. Every answer should be yes. If any answer is no, rewrite it.

Watch the third check. Even a friendly "hope to see you next summer, here's a code" in the same message reads as a trade.

The fifth keeps any goodwill gesture separate from the request.
