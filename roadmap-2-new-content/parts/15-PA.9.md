### PA.9.0 Learning outcome

By the end of this lesson you can run a diligence sequence on a house you have under contract. You'll test the building and the proposed rental business before your contingency deadlines expire. You'll know which professional verifies each risk, what written evidence to save, and how each finding changes the underwriting you brought from Sam's PA.4. The output is a filled diligence tracker and a dated proceed, renegotiate, or walk decision.

### PA.9.1 The clock starts when the contract is signed

Your accepted offer from PA.8 bought you time, and that time is the real asset in this phase. Most purchase contracts give the buyer a window to inspect, a window to secure financing, and sometimes a separate window for the appraisal. The exact names and lengths come from your contract and your state's standard forms. Read your own contract for the dates. Put every deadline in a calendar the day you sign.

Here is the rule I want you to hold onto. A problem you find after a contingency expires is still your problem, but you've lost most of your leverage to fix it. The CFPB puts it plainly: if the contract is contingent on a satisfactory inspection, you should be able to cancel without penalty when the inspection turns up serious flaws ([CFPB, Schedule a home inspection](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/close/schedule-a-home-inspection/)). That protection only works while the window is open and only in the form your contract states.

So order the work by what can kill the deal fastest. Legal use of the address and the insurance quote come first, because no repair can fix a ban or an uninsurable house. The general inspection and specialist inspections come next, because they need scheduling time. Title and survey review run in parallel, since the title company works on its own timeline. The appraisal is ordered by the lender, so your job is to know when it's due and what happens if it comes in low.

Open one folder for the property on day one. Every report, quote, email, and photo goes in it with a date in the file name. You'll use the same folder at closing in PA.11 and in the operations handoff in PA.12.

### PA.9.2 Two questions, not one

A regular homebuyer asks one question during diligence: is this house sound? You have to ask a second one: can this house run the specific rental business in Sam's underwriting, at the guest count and price he modeled?

Those two questions need different people. An inspector can tell you the roof has four years left. An inspector can't tell you whether the county will issue you a short-term rental permit. The county can tell you about the permit. The county won't tell you whether your insurer will write a policy for eight guests on a lake. Each question has an owner, and your tracker should name that owner in writing.

Treat the underwriting from PA.4 as a hypothesis. Every finding in diligence either confirms a line in it or changes it. At the end you rerun the numbers and ask whether the deal you're about to close is still the deal you meant to buy.

### PA.9.3 Property condition: the STR inspection list

Start with a general home inspection by an inspector you've checked out yourself. The CFPB notes that inspectors may not be required to be licensed in some areas. It suggests checking references and your state or county licensing authority before you hire ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/close/schedule-a-home-inspection/)). Then add specialists for the systems a general inspector only looks at from the surface.

Walk the inspector through the plan: eight guests, turnovers every few days, people who don't know the house. Ask them to inspect with that load in mind. Here is the list I'd hand over for a house like Cedar Ridge.

1. Roof. Age, remaining life, flashing, gutters, signs of past leaks in the attic. A roof near the end of its life is a capital expense you'll pay while guests are booked.
2. HVAC. Age, service history, capacity for the full house, and whether the system can be set and monitored remotely.
3. Water supply. If the house is on a private well, the water test is on you. The EPA says its public drinking water rules don't apply to private wells, and it recommends testing annually for total coliform bacteria, nitrates, total dissolved solids, and pH ([EPA, Protect your home's water](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells/protect-your-homes-water)). The CDC also recommends using certified labs ([CDC, Guidelines for testing well water](https://www.cdc.gov/drinking-water/safety/guidelines-for-testing-well-water.html)). Test during the contingency, not after closing.
4. Sewer or septic. Lake houses are often on septic. The EPA notes that many states require a septic inspection when real estate transfers. It suggests an inspection every 1 to 3 years and pumping every 3 to 5 years for a typical household ([EPA, Frequent questions on septic systems](https://www.epa.gov/septic/frequent-questions-septic-systems)). An STR is not a typical household. More guests and more laundry mean more load. Ask the septic inspector to pump the tank, inspect the baffles and drain field, and tell you what bedroom count the system was permitted for. The county health department usually holds that record.
5. Pests. The CFPB says a lender may require a pest certificate, and you may want one even when it isn't required ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/close/schedule-a-home-inspection/)). Wood near water gets checked for rot and insects.
6. Electrical. Panel condition, capacity for a hot tub or EV charger you may add later, GFCI protection near water, and any exposed wiring.
7. Access and parking. Can a cleaner reach the house in winter? Is the road public or private, and who plows it? Is there parking for the guest count without blocking a neighbor?
8. Pool, hot tub, dock, and decks. Anything guests will stand on or swim in gets a close look. The CPSC points out that pools and spas should have drain covers that meet federal standards ([CPSC vacation rental safety tips](https://www.cpsc.gov/s3fs-public/VacationRentalSafetytips002_0.pdf?VersionId=MKcrFCYmuih6xmpoX59wcdmexPwzAXrO)).
9. Fire and life safety. Smoke and carbon monoxide alarms, exits from every sleeping room, and extinguishers. Airbnb's own safety rules list blocked exits, inoperable smoke detectors, and exposed wiring as hazards hosts must address ([Airbnb, Safety requirements for homes](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/2904)).
10. Flood exposure. Look up the address on the [FEMA Flood Map Service Center](https://msc.fema.gov/portal/search). FEMA says most homeowners insurance does not cover flood damage and flood coverage is a separate policy ([FEMA, Flood insurance](https://www.fema.gov/flood-insurance)).

Every finding gets a cost. "Needs attention soon" is not a number. Ask the inspector or a contractor for a written range, and record the midpoint in your tracker.

### PA.9.4 Business suitability: can this address run this business?

This half of diligence is where STR buyers get hurt, because a normal home inspection never touches it. You're testing five things.

The first is legal use of this exact parcel. You and Sam checked the market in PA.2, but a market answer is not an address answer. Use the SR.1 checklist to confirm zoning, the STR permit category, any cap or waitlist, and whether the permit is tied to the owner. Airbnb's own guidance says hosts are responsible for checking permitting, zoning, safety, and health rules ([Airbnb, Responsible hosting in the United States](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/1376)). Get the licensing office's answer in writing if you can.

The second is whether anything transfers. Many sellers will say "it's already permitted." Ask whether the permit transfers to a new owner or whether you must apply fresh. Ask how long a new application takes. If the house has bookings on the calendar after your closing date, your contract needs to say who honors them and who keeps the money. Don't rely on a verbal answer from the listing agent.

The third is private restrictions. Cedar Ridge has no HOA. That doesn't mean there are no rules. Recorded covenants on the lot can limit rentals or commercial use, and those show up in the title work covered in PA.9.6.

The fourth is the insurance quote. Get a bindable quote for a short-term rental policy before the inspection window closes. Ask what conditions the insurer attaches, such as railing repairs or added alarms, and when those must be done. Airbnb says AirCover for Hosts is not a substitute for your own insurance ([Airbnb, AirCover for hosts](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/3733)). PA.12 covers the policy details after closing.

The fifth is guest capacity. Sam's underwriting assumes the house sleeps 8. Check that number against three limits: the septic permit's bedroom count, any occupancy cap in the local permit, and what the insurer will write. The lowest of the three is your real capacity. If it's lower than 8, your revenue line changes.

### PA.9.5 Inspection versus appraisal

Buyers mix these up, and the confusion costs money. The CFPB draws the line clearly. A home inspector works for you and looks for flaws. An appraiser is an independent professional whose job is to give the lender an estimate of market value ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/close/schedule-a-home-inspection/)). A clean appraisal does not mean the septic works. A clean inspection does not mean the house is worth your price.

The CFPB also notes you are entitled to a copy of the appraisal before closing when the loan is secured by a first lien on a dwelling ([CFPB, 12 CFR 1002.14](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1002/14/)). Ask for it as soon as it's done. Read the comparable sales it used. For a DSCR loan, ask whether the appraisal includes a rent or income analysis and which figure the lender will use.

Here's why the appraised value matters on a loan sized as a percentage of value. Most lenders size the loan off the lower of price or appraised value. Confirm your lender's rule in writing. Say Cedar Ridge appraised at $375,000 (assumption) against the $385,000 contract price. At 75% of $375,000, the loan drops to $281,250. The buyers would need $103,750 down instead of $96,250. Points fall slightly to $2,812, so closing costs become $14,362. Cash to close rises to $118,112, which is $7,424 more than the $110,688 in the canonical DSCR column. That's before any renegotiation. PA.8 covered the appraisal gap decision. In diligence, your job is to know the number the day the appraisal lands.

### PA.9.6 Title review: who else has a claim on this land?

The title company searches public records and issues a title commitment. Read it yourself. Your attorney or closing agent should walk you through it, but you're the one who knows how you plan to use the land.

A commitment usually has two parts that matter to you. The requirements list what must happen before the title company will insure, such as paying off the seller's mortgage or releasing a lien. The exceptions list what the policy won't cover, such as recorded easements and covenants. Anything in the exceptions stays with the land after you buy it.

Look for four things in that list.

1. Liens. A lien from an unpaid contractor or unpaid tax should be paid from the seller's proceeds at closing. The CFPB gives exactly these examples of claims owner's title insurance can protect against ([CFPB, What is owner's title insurance](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-owners-title-insurance-en-164/)).
2. Easements. A neighbor's right to walk to the lake across your lot changes privacy and guest experience. A utility easement may limit where you build a hot tub pad.
3. Covenants and restrictions. Read every recorded restriction for rental or business-use language.
4. Survey matters. A survey shows where the lot lines, structures, dock, and driveway actually sit. The CFPB lists survey fees among the closing services you can often shop for ([CFPB, Shop for title insurance and other closing services](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/close/shop-for-title-insurance-and-other-closing-services/)). A lender may require one. Even if it doesn't, a lake lot with a dock and shared access is a good reason to order one.

Now the two title policies. Lender's title insurance protects the lender, and the CFPB says most lenders require it and it covers only claims that affect the lender's loan ([CFPB, What is lender's title insurance](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-lenders-title-insurance-en-163/)). Owner's title insurance protects you. The CFPB describes it as optional coverage you may want to protect your equity ([CFPB, What is owner's title insurance](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-owners-title-insurance-en-164/)). On an investment property with about $96,000 of your own cash in it, I'd want the owner's policy priced and decided on purpose. PA.11 shows where it appears on the closing figures.

### PA.9.7 Evidence, owners, and deadlines

A finding without an owner and a date doesn't get resolved. Every row in your tracker needs five fields: who verifies it, the deadline, what they found, what it costs, and your decision.

The decision column only has four answers. Accept means you'll live with it and budget for it. Seller fixes means the seller repairs before closing, with proof. Credit or price means you negotiate money. Walk means you terminate inside the contingency window. Write the decision the day you make it, and write why.

Save evidence in the property folder: the inspection report, lab results, septic report, the county's permit answer, the insurance quote with its conditions, the title commitment, the survey, and the appraisal. You'll need several of these again for the insurer, the lender, and the permit office.

### PA.9.8 Worked example: Cedar Ridge diligence

Maya and Chris have Cedar Ridge under contract at $385,000 with a DSCR loan quote. Their contract gives them a 10-day inspection window and a 21-day financing and appraisal window (assumption). Sam's PA.4 baseline is the canonical base case: gross booking revenue $74,275, operating costs $45,066, NOI $29,209, and DSCR debt service of $23,637. That leaves $5,572 of annual cash flow, or about $464 a month. The weak case is -$5,475.

All findings below are assumptions for teaching.

**Cedar Ridge diligence tracker (all figures are assumptions)**

| # | Item | Verified by | Deadline | Finding | Cost | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|---|---:|---|
| 1 | STR permit for this parcel | County licensing office, in writing | Day 5 | Permitted use. Seller's permit does not transfer. New owner applies. Occupancy cap 2 per bedroom plus 2 (8 guests). | $400 / year | Accept. Apply right after closing. |
| 2 | Insurance quote | STR insurance agent | Day 6 | Quote at $3,200 / year. Conditions: repair dock stair railing and add alarms on lower level before binding. | See rows 7 and 8 | Accept with conditions. |
| 3 | Roof | General inspector, roofer estimate | Day 8 | Asphalt shingles with 4 to 6 years of life left. Replacement estimate $14,000. | $2,800 / year sinking fund | Asked for a roof credit. Seller declined. Accept and fund it. |
| 4 | Septic | Licensed septic inspector | Day 8 | Tank pumped. Broken outlet baffle, missing effluent filter. County record shows a 3-bedroom design. | $1,850 repair | Seller fixes before closing, with invoice. |
| 5 | Septic, ongoing | Septic inspector | Day 8 | Guest load warrants yearly inspection and more frequent pumping. | $400 / year | Accept. Add to operating budget. |
| 6 | Well water | Certified lab | Day 7 | Total coliform present. Nitrates within limits. | $350 shock and retest; $1,650 UV system; $220 / year lamp and test | Accept. Buyer treats after closing. Retest before first guest. |
| 7 | Dock stair railing | General inspector | Day 8 | Loose posts on stairs to the dock. | $2,300 | Accept. Repair before insurer binds. |
| 8 | Smoke and CO alarms | General inspector | Day 8 | No alarms on lower sleeping level. | $380 | Accept. Install before first guest. |
| 9 | HVAC | General inspector | Day 8 | 14 years old, working, no service record. | $275 service | Accept. Service after closing. |
| 10 | Title commitment | Title company, attorney | Day 15 | Mechanic's lien of $3,100 from a 2024 contractor. Recorded footpath easement for a neighbor to reach the lake. | $0 to buyers | Seller pays the lien from proceeds. Accept easement and disclose it in the listing. |
| 11 | Survey | Licensed surveyor | Day 18 | Dock and driveway inside lot lines. Footpath matches the easement. | Included in closing costs | Accept. |
| 12 | Appraisal | Lender's appraiser | Day 21 | Value supports the contract price. | None | Proceed. |
| 13 | Future bookings | Seller, per contract | Day 10 | Seller has no bookings after closing date. | None | Confirm in writing. |

Now rerun the numbers. Four findings add recurring annual costs that PA.4's generic lines don't cover.

| Added annual cost (assumption) | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Roof sinking fund ($14,000 / 5 years) | $2,800 |
| Septic inspection and pumping program | $400 |
| Well UV lamp and annual water test | $220 |
| County STR permit renewal | $400 |
| **Total added annual cost** | **$3,820** |

You might ask why the roof isn't already covered by the 5% capital reserve of $3,714. It could be, if nothing else ever broke. That reserve was a generic percentage. Now you have a known $14,000 bill in about five years, and it would eat almost four years of the whole reserve. Fund the known item on its own line and let the 5% cover the surprises.

The revised base case works out like this.

- Revised NOI: $29,209 - $3,820 = $25,389.
- Revised annual cash flow on the DSCR loan: $25,389 - $23,637 = $1,752, about $146 a month.
- Revised weak-case cash flow: $18,162 - $3,820 - $23,637 = -$9,295.

The buyers also pay for one-time repairs after closing: $350 + $1,650 + $2,300 + $380 + $275 = $4,955. Total cash in rises from $142,688 to $147,643. Cash-on-cash in the base case falls from 3.9% to 1.2% ($1,752 / $147,643).

The seller's items don't touch the buyers' cash. The $1,850 septic repair and the $3,100 lien payoff come out of the seller's side, so the buyers' cash to close stays at $110,688. You'll see that figure again in PA.11.

Now the decision. The house is sound enough to buy. The business still works in the base case, barely. The weak case got worse by $3,820 a year. Maya and Chris asked for a roof credit and were refused. They decided to proceed because they have $210,000 in liquid savings and the $147,643 of total cash in leaves room for a reserve. They wrote down a new rule: if the first 12 months run closer to the weak case, they revisit the co-host and pricing plan at the OP.10 review.

That's a documented decision, not a feeling. A different buyer with less cash could look at the same file and walk inside the 10-day window. Both answers are defensible if they're written before the deadline.

Decision rules to apply before the window closes:

1. If legal STR use at this address is prohibited or unresolved, walk or extend the window in writing. Don't close on "probably."
2. If no insurer will write a policy at a price your numbers can carry, walk.
3. If real guest capacity is lower than the underwriting assumed, rerun revenue before anything else.

6. Record the decision, the date, and the reason in one sentence.

### PA.9.10 Who confirms what

**Confirm with:** your real estate attorney or agent. They confirm the exact contingency deadlines, how to extend them in writing, and how to terminate without losing earnest money.

**Confirm with:** the local licensing or zoning office. They confirm STR use for the parcel, whether a permit transfers, occupancy caps, and new-application timing.

**Confirm with:** the county health department or septic inspector. They confirm the permitted bedroom count and design capacity of the septic system.

**Confirm with:** your insurance agent. They confirm the policy can be bound, its conditions, and its price for your guest count, plus any flood coverage.

**Confirm with:** your lender. It confirms how the loan is sized after the appraisal, whether the appraisal includes an income analysis, and what happens to the loan if value comes in low.

**Confirm with:** the title company and your attorney. They confirm which liens will be paid at closing, what each exception means, and whether a survey is required.
