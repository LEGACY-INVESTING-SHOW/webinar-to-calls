### PA.8.0 Learning outcome

After this lesson you can set a walk-away price before you talk to the seller. You'll build it from conservative net operating income, your loan terms, and the cash you actually have. You'll know which contract terms protect that number: earnest money, contingencies, appraisal gap, seller concessions, closing date, and furniture. And you'll record every negotiation decision as proceed, renegotiate, or walk, with the reason.

### PA.8.1 Set the ceiling before you see the seller's number

The asking price is the seller's opening position. It tells you what the seller hopes to get. It says nothing about what the house can carry as a rental.

A common way STR offers go wrong is working backward from the asking price. The buyer sees $399,000, then nudges the nightly rate up until the spreadsheet works. Now the offer depends on the most optimistic line in the model.

Reverse the order. Decide the most you'll pay first, from numbers you'd defend to a lender. Then negotiate below that number. If the seller won't come under it, you walk, and you already know why.

Sam's PA.4 lesson builds the underwriting package: revenue, expenses, cap rate, and cash-on-cash return. This lesson takes two lines from that package, base-case NOI and weak-case NOI, and turns them into a price. Don't redo PA.4's work here. Bring his outputs.

### PA.8.2 Three tests that set a walk-away price

Your walk-away price is the lowest of three ceilings. Test 2 comes in two versions, strict and funded, and you pick one before you run the numbers.

**Test 1: base-case cash flow.** At your expected revenue, the house must produce at least a minimum annual cash flow after the loan payment. You set the minimum. It should cover more than zero, because base-case years still bring surprises.

**Test 2: weak-case survival.** In a weak year, the house should break even. If it can't, you need a written, funded plan for the shortfall. A buyer who can't fund a weak year should hold the strict breakeven line.

**Test 3: cash.** Everything you put in, plus the reserves you promised yourself, must fit inside the cash you have. Count the down payment, closing costs, points, furnishing, lender reserves, a weak-year reserve, and your personal emergency fund.

Use the more expensive financing quote for all three tests. Maya and Chris chose conventional first in PA.7, with DSCR as the backup. Until the conventional approval is in writing, they underwrite on the DSCR quote: 7.25%, 1 point, 25% down.

Why cash flow and not cap rate? The tracker asks for an offer ceiling from conservative NOI and cash required. Cap rate uses NOI but ignores your loan. Cash flow shows what the loan does to that NOI. Both belong in Sam's PA.4 package. This lesson uses the one that decides whether you can hold the house.

### PA.8.3 Worked example: Cedar Ridge's walk-away price

Canonical inputs (all assumptions from Example B): base-case NOI $29,209; weak-case NOI $18,162; property tax $3,850 and insurance $3,200, already inside NOI; furnishing $32,000; closing costs 3% of price plus points.

Maya and Chris now have $231,000 in liquid savings. In 0.3 they had $210,000 and a funding condition: no offer until the full cash stack was funded. They saved $3,500 a month (assumption), and six months later $210,000 + 6 x $3,500 = $231,000 (assumption). The condition is met.

Three new rules are theirs to set. I've marked them as assumptions:

- Minimum base-case cash flow: $5,000 a year (assumption).
- Weak-case rule: a shortfall no larger than $6,000 a year (assumption), with two years of that shortfall held in a separate reserve.
- Personal emergency fund untouched: $51,000, the six months of essential spending they set in 0.3 (assumption). Lender reserves: 6 months of PITIA (assumption).

Here's the arithmetic, step by step.

**Test 1.** NOI already covers tax and insurance, so cash flow is NOI minus principal and interest.

Maximum annual P&I = $29,209 - $5,000 = $24,209, or $2,017 a month.

At 7.25% over 30 years, $2,017 a month supports a loan of about $295,732.

With 25% down, the loan is 75% of price. Price ceiling = $295,732 / 0.75 = **$394,310**.

**Test 2, strict version.** Breakeven in the weak case means P&I can't exceed weak NOI.

Maximum annual P&I = $18,162. That supports a loan of about $221,863.

Price ceiling = $221,863 / 0.75 = **$295,818**.

That's $103,182 under the asking price. Could more cash close the gap instead? At $385,000, a weak-case breakeven loan of $221,863 means a down payment of $163,137, which is 42.4% of the price. Add closing costs and the $32,000 furnishing budget, and the total is about $208,906. That's far more than the $180,000 they can put into a property once the $51,000 emergency fund is set aside. Under the strict rule, Cedar Ridge doesn't work anywhere near the asking price.

**Test 2, funded-shortfall version.** Maya and Chris have two W-2 incomes and savings. They decide in writing that they can carry a weak-year shortfall of up to $6,000 a year, and they set aside two years of it.

Maximum annual P&I = $18,162 + $6,000 = $24,162. Price ceiling = **$393,544**.

That's a real choice, and they're making it on purpose. A buyer without their income or savings should use the strict test and pass on this house.

**Test 3.** Total cash needed at a price P has seven parts. They are 25% down, 3% closing costs, 1 point, $32,000 furnishing, 6 months of PITIA, two years of weak-case shortfall, and the $51,000 emergency fund. The cash ceiling is the highest price where that total stays at or under $231,000. Take away the emergency fund and $180,000 is left for everything else. The ceiling works out to about **$409,984**.

**The walk-away price.** Maya and Chris chose the funded version of Test 2, so the strict $295,818 line doesn't apply to them. Take the lowest ceiling: $393,544 from the funded weak-case test, just under Test 1's $394,310. Round down: **$393,000**. A buyer who can't fund a weak year uses the strict line, and at $295,818 this house is a pass.

The asking price of $399,000 fails. Their eventual contract price of $385,000 passes.

Here's the same check at four prices. All figures are assumptions, DSCR quote.

| Price | Loan (75%) | Total cash in | Annual P&I | Base cash flow | Weak cash flow |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| $399,000 | $299,250 | $146,712 | $24,497 | $4,712 | -$6,335 |
| $392,000 | $294,000 | $144,700 | $24,067 | $5,142 | -$5,905 |
| $385,000 | $288,750 | $142,688 | $23,637 | $5,572 | -$5,475 |
| $372,000 | $279,000 | $138,950 | $22,839 | $6,370 | -$4,677 |

At $399,000, both tests fail: base cash flow is under $5,000 and the weak shortfall is over $6,000.

At $385,000, the cash plan looks like this:

| Use of cash at $385,000 | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Total cash in (cash to close + furnishing) | $142,688 |
| Lender reserves, 6 months PITIA (6 x $2,557) | $15,342 |
| Weak-year reserve, 2 x $5,475 | $10,950 |
| Personal emergency fund | $51,000 |
| **Total committed** | **$219,980** |

One more caution. The model holds property tax at $3,850. Some counties reassess after a sale, and the tax could rise. Ask your agent or the county assessor what the tax would be at your price before you finalize the ceiling.

### PA.8.4 The terms that protect your ceiling

Price is one term. The others decide how much risk you carry between signing and closing. Purchase contracts are state-specific, often on a standard form your agent uses. Treat everything below as questions for your agent and attorney, not as rules.

**Earnest money.** This is the deposit you put down when the seller accepts, often held by an escrow or title company. It shows the seller you're serious. It goes toward your cash to close. The CFPB's [Loan Estimate explainer](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/loan-estimate/) notes that your estimated cash to close is reduced by any deposit you've already paid. Whether you get it back if the deal fails depends on the contract and which contingencies are still open. A larger deposit can make an offer stronger. It also raises what you could lose if you miss a deadline.

**Inspection contingency.** This gives you a set number of days to inspect and then cancel or renegotiate. For an STR, you're also testing the business: permits, HOA or deed restrictions, insurance quotes, septic capacity for your guest count. PA.9 builds that list. Make sure the period is long enough to get STR insurance quotes and a written answer from the licensing office.

**Financing contingency.** This lets you cancel if your loan isn't approved by a date. Match the date to your lender's realistic timeline, not the seller's preference. If you're on the conventional route with a DSCR backup, the date needs room for a switch.

**Appraisal contingency and appraisal gap.** Lenders generally size the loan on the lower of the price or the appraised value; ask yours to confirm. If the appraisal comes in low, the loan shrinks and you need more cash. An appraisal contingency lets you renegotiate or cancel. An appraisal gap clause promises the seller you'll cover some or all of a shortfall with cash. Only offer gap coverage you've already counted in Test 3.

**Seller concessions.** The seller pays some of your closing costs, usually as a credit. The CFPB's Loan Estimate explainer notes that seller credits reduce your estimated cash to close. Lenders can limit how much the seller contributes and what the money can pay for, and the limit may depend on the occupancy type. Ask your lender for its limit in dollars before you ask the seller.

**Closing date.** Two things drive it. Your rate lock has an expiration date, and extensions cost money. Your launch season matters too: closing in time to furnish, photograph, and list before the lake season is worth more than a small price cut. Work backward from the date you want the first guest.

**Furniture and equipment.** A seller may offer to include furniture, a golf cart, a boat lift, or a hot tub. Ask three questions. Does it fit your design and guest plan (OP.6)? Is it in usable condition? How does your lender treat personal property included in the price? Ask before you agree to pay for it. If you want an item, list it clearly in the contract or on a separate bill of sale. If you don't, say it's excluded so the seller removes it.

**Existing bookings.** If the house already takes guests, ask whether future reservations transfer, who keeps those payments, and whether the platform even allows a transfer. PA.9 picks this up.

### PA.8.5 Before you waive or shorten a contingency

Sellers sometimes ask for a shorter inspection period, no financing contingency, or appraisal gap coverage. Get written answers to these before you agree.

Ask your agent:

1. If I shorten the inspection period, can we still fit the inspection, the septic or sewer check, and the STR insurance quote?
2. What happens to my earnest money if I cancel after this contingency expires?
3. What's normal in this market for this type of house? Is the seller's request unusual?
4. Can the permit or licensing answer arrive before the inspection period ends?

Ask your lender:

1. How many days do you realistically need to approve this loan with this property type?
2. What happens to my loan if the appraisal comes in $10,000 low?
3. Does your approval depend on proof of STR permit eligibility? When do you need it?
4. What's your limit on seller credits for this occupancy type?
5. When does my rate lock expire, and what does an extension cost?

If any answer puts your earnest money at risk for a problem you can't test in time, keep the contingency.

### PA.8.6 What changes after a low appraisal or an inspection finding

Two practice scenarios, both what-ifs on the accepted $385,000 contract. All figures are assumptions. I set one more rule for Maya and Chris: keep at least $10,000 of the $231,000 unallocated after every commitment (assumption).

**Scenario 1: the appraisal comes in at $375,000.**

The lender lends 75% of the lower value: $375,000 x 0.75 = $281,250. That's $7,500 less than planned.

| Option | Price | Cash to close | Total committed | Unallocated | Rule met? |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Pay the full gap | $385,000 | $118,112 | $225,870 | $5,130 | No |
| Split the gap | $380,000 | $112,962 | $220,720 | $10,280 | Yes |
| Seller drops to value | $375,000 | $107,812 | $215,570 | $15,430 | Yes |

The smaller loan lowers PITIA to $2,506, so reserves and the weak-year shortfall shrink a little. Base cash flow rises to $6,186 and the weak case improves to -$4,861 in every option, because the loan is the same $281,250.

Decision rule: renegotiate. Ask the seller to drop to $375,000. Accept anything up to $380,000. If the seller holds at $385,000 and the appraisal contingency is still open, walk. They'd have to spend their margin to pay a price the appraiser didn't support.

**Scenario 2: the inspection finds $4,500 of work (assumption).**

Say the water heater is at the end of its life and several deck boards are soft. If Maya and Chris absorb the cost, total committed rises to $219,980 + $4,500 = $224,480. Unallocated cash falls to $6,520, under their $10,000 rule.

Decision rule: renegotiate. Ask the seller to repair before closing, or to reduce the price. Ask the lender first whether a seller credit can go toward repairs or only toward closing costs. If the finding is a safety issue for guests, it gets fixed before launch either way. PA.12 covers that sign-off.

A different finding changes the answer. A failing septic system sized for fewer guests than you planned isn't a price problem. It can break the business plan. That's a walk unless the fix and the permit both work on paper.

Here's how the Cedar Ridge negotiation could run. The terms and amounts are assumptions for teaching, except the canonical asking and contract prices.

**Round 1: Maya and Chris offer.**

Why $372,000? It's $21,000 under their $393,000 walk-away price. That leaves room for two counters without crossing the line.

**Round 2: the seller counters.**

| Term | Counter |
|---|---|
| Price | $392,000 |
| Earnest money | 2% |
| Inspection contingency | 7 days |
| Closing | 30 days |
| Furniture | Included for an extra $8,000 |

Maya and Chris check the counter against their tests. $392,000 is under $393,000, but only by $1,000. Base cash flow is $5,142, barely above the $5,000 minimum. The weak shortfall is $5,905, just inside $6,000. It passes on paper with almost no margin.

The terms matter too. Seven days isn't enough to get the septic inspection and an STR insurance quote, according to their agent. A 30-day close is tight for their lender. The furniture doesn't fit the design plan in OP.6, and their lender told them it won't count it in the loan (assumption for this mock).

**Round 3: Maya and Chris counter.**

| Term | Counter |
|---|---|
| Price | $385,000 |
| Earnest money | $7,700 (2%), accepting the seller's request |

**Part 3: your decision log.** For every offer, counter, appraisal, and inspection result, record the date, the numbers, and one of three decisions: proceed, renegotiate, or walk. Write the reason in one line, tied to a test or a rule.

### PA.8.9 Who confirms what

**Confirm with:** your real estate agent, the local purchase contract form, contingency periods, earnest money handling, and what's normal in this market.

**Confirm with:** your attorney, what each contingency lets you do, when your earnest money is at risk, and how any appraisal gap clause is worded.

**Confirm with:** your lender, the maximum seller credit for this occupancy type, whether it can cover repairs, how personal property in the price is treated, the approval timeline, and the rate lock expiration.

**Confirm with:** the county assessor or your agent, the likely property tax at your purchase price.

**Confirm with:** your insurer, an STR quote inside the inspection period.

**Confirm with:** the local licensing office, that STR use is allowed at this address before the inspection contingency expires (SR.1).
