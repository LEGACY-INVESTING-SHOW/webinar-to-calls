Ownership wins only on specific facts, so test it on each deal

Slide 1 · Ownership wins only on specific facts, so test it on each deal

The title of this lesson makes a claim: ownership wins. Sometimes it does. Sometimes it loses badly. So I'm going to treat that title as a question. You answer it for one property, one loan, and one household.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to split an ownership return into four streams. You'll know which of the four you can actually spend this year. You'll compare owning a house with leasing a similar one, using the same assumptions. And you'll have a simple test that tells you whether "ownership wins" is true for a specific deal, or only true on a slide.

We'll use Cedar Ridge the whole way through. That's the hypothetical three-bedroom lake house Maya and Chris are buying at $385,000 on a DSCR quote. DSCR stands for debt service coverage ratio: how many dollars of property income the lender counts for every dollar of loan payment. Every figure is an assumption from the course example file. None of this is tax advice. Where the answer depends on your household, I'll tell you, and your CPA makes the call.

The mistake to avoid: adding all four together and calling the total income.

Slide 2 · An ownership return has four streams, and only one lands in your bank account

The usual pitch for owning lists three benefits: cash flow, tax savings, and equity. I split equity into two parts, because they behave differently. That gives four possible streams. They run side by side, not one after another.

Stream one is operating cash flow. It's what's left after operating costs and the loan payment.

Stream two is principal paydown. It's the part of each loan payment that reduces what you owe.

Stream three is change in value. It's what the house is worth later compared with what you paid.

Stream four is tax timing. That's deductions, mainly depreciation, that may lower your tax now. Part of that benefit may be taxed back when you sell. That's why I call it timing rather than savings.

Every stream has a cost or a risk attached. And only one of them lands in your bank account in a normal month. The other three are either locked inside the house or depend on facts you won't know for years.

So when someone adds all four and calls the total "income", stop them. It isn't income.

Slide 4 · Year-one paydown of $2,795 is equity you can't spend until you sell or refinance

Stream two is principal paydown. Each month, part of the loan payment reduces the balance. That part isn't money gone. It becomes equity: the share of the house you own outright.

On the Cedar Ridge DSCR quote, the balance falls by $2,795 in year one. That grows a little each year, because more of each payment goes to principal as the balance shrinks.

Here's the catch. You already paid that $2,795 out of your cash flow. It's inside the $23,637 of debt service. You can't spend it. You only get it back when you sell or refinance.

Refinancing can turn some of it into cash. The example file has a refinance at the end of year two. It frees $15,725 after costs. But the new loan is larger, so base-case cash flow drops from $5,572 to $4,752 a year.

And the equity it builds depends on the house holding its value. If the value falls, you can pay the loan down every month and still end up with less equity than you started with.

Stream three is change in value. People talk about it most and control it least.

For teaching, the example assumes Cedar Ridge grows 3% a year. In year one that's 3% of $385,000, or $11,550. That's an assumption. Values can fall. Lake markets can see new supply, rule changes, or a slow year.

Three rules for this stream. First, never add modeled appreciation to spendable monthly income. $11,550 of assumed growth isn't $960 a month you can use. Depreciation starts when the house is ready for guests, and your CPA sets the schedule

  • You depreciate the building, never the land (IRS Pub 527). The land split comes first.
  • It starts when the house is ready for rent. Ready in July, first rented in September: it starts in July.
  • Per full year: $11,375 (27.5-year) or $8,021 (39-year). Short average stays can change which applies.
  • 100% bonus depreciation is permanent for eligible property acquired after January 19, 2025 (IRS, Notice 2026-11). Not land, not the whole building.
Slide 7 · Depreciation starts when the house is ready for guests, and your CPA sets the schedule

Each input here is a question for your CPA, not a number to assume.

Basis and land come first. IRS Publication 527 says you can never depreciate land. So how much of the price belongs to the land matters a lot.

Then the placed-in-service date. Depreciation starts when the property is ready and available for rent, not when the first guest arrives. Publication 527's example: a house ready in July and first rented in September starts depreciating in July. So a late-year launch means a small first-year deduction. The example uses a full year only to keep the math simple.

Then the recovery period. The example file shows two schedules: $11,375 a year at 27.5 years, labeled residential rental, or $8,021 at 39 years, labeled nonresidential or transient use. Short average stays can push a property onto the 39-year schedule. Your CPA decides which applies.

Finally, bonus depreciation. IRS guidance describes a permanent 100% first-year deduction for eligible property acquired after January 19, 2025. It doesn't cover land or the whole building.

Slide 8 · Three tax tests decide whether the loss can offset your other income this year

Here's what has to be true before that deduction lowers your tax this year.

IRS Publication 925 covers passive activity rules. Losses from passive activities generally offset only other passive activity income.

Test one. If the average stay is 7 days or less, Publication 925 doesn't count it as a "rental activity" under these rules. You divide total rental days by the number of rentals. Cedar Ridge is 212 nights over 61 stays, 3.5 nights. Yes is the answer you want. But it only opens the door.

Test two. The loss can offset other income, like W-2 wages, only if you also materially participate. Three tests matter most. More than 500 hours. Or substantially all the participation. Or more than 100 hours and at least as much as any other individual. If you fail, the loss stays passive and may be suspended. The example file notes it carries forward to later years.

Test three is personal use, from Publication 527. The house counts as a home if personal use exceeds the greater of 14 days or 10% of fair-rental days. At 212 rented nights, that's 21.2 days. Maya and Chris plan 10.

  • At a short average stay it may not apply at all. Either way, it doesn't help at their income.
Slide 9 · A busy cleaner can out-log you, and the $25,000 allowance won't help at $185,000

Two traps that catch high-earning households.

First, the "more than 100 hours and not less than any other individual" test. That means other people's hours matter, owner or not. A cleaner who turns Cedar Ridge over 61 times a year may log more hours than Maya and Chris. If they hire a full-service co-host, the co-host's hours may beat theirs by a wide margin. Keep a real log of your hours. Your CPA will ask.

Second, the $25,000 special allowance. Publication 925 describes it for rental real estate with active participation. It's reduced by 50% of modified adjusted gross income above $100,000, and it's gone at $150,000. Maya and Chris have $185,000 of combined W-2 income. If their modified AGI is near that, the allowance gives them nothing. And at a short average stay it may not apply at all.

So for this household, the $25,000 allowance isn't a backup plan. Participation is the test that matters.

Slide 16 · Ownership wins for your deal only if all three of these tests pass

Here's the decision rule. Ownership wins for this deal only if all three are true.

One. The Part B base case is ahead after five years. If owning doesn't come out ahead when things go as planned, it isn't going to win when they don't. Cedar Ridge passes: $27,732 ahead.

Two. The weak case is behind by no more than you could accept losing. Put a real number on that before you look at listings. For Cedar Ridge, the weak case with 3% growth ends $27,503 behind. Some households can live with that. Some can't. Neither answer is wrong.

Three. In Part C, the extra cash for owning buys something you actually want. That could be a long hold, personal use, or equity. If a lease gets you the same cash flow for a third of the money, you should be able to say why you're buying anyway.

If any one fails, write down which one. Don't argue with it. Bring it to lesson 0.3, where we check whether your household can fund the full stack and get through a bad first year.
