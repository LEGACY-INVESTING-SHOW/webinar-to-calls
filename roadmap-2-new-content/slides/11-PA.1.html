Slide 1 · Write the buy box before you see listings, so you can walk away without debate

This is the first lesson of the acquisition phase. By the end of it, you'll have a written, one-page buy box.

That page ranks your goals. It states your constraints. It names your hard requirements. And it describes the property you want in terms someone else could search with. It ends with a pass rule, a sentence that tells you when to walk away from a listing without arguing about it.

Why does this come before any market research? Because Sam's PA.2 and PA.3 lessons use this page. PA.2 picks a market with it. PA.3 builds your property pipeline with it. Without the page, those lessons have nothing to filter on.

We'll build Maya and Chris's buy box from what they learned in 0.3. Then we'll test it on Cedar Ridge at two prices, and on a house they should never look at twice. Every figure is an assumption. The canonical ones come from the course example file, and the new ones are marked.

PA.1 · Defining Your Buy Box§ PA.1.12 / 19
Slide 2 · A buy box protects you from budget drift and from underwriting the wrong houses

A buy box is a search specification. It says what you'll buy, where, at what price, and under what conditions. Anything outside the box, you don't look at twice.

It protects you from two things.

The first is drift. You start looking for a three-bedroom house at $380,000. A month later you're touring a $450,000 house with a pool. The photos were great and the agent said it "books great". Nobody decided to raise the budget by $70,000. It just happened, one listing at a time.

The second is wasted work. Every property you seriously consider costs hours. Reading rules, pulling comparable listings, underwriting, calling lenders. Sam's PA.3 builds a pipeline of 5 to 10 qualified properties. Without a buy box, "qualified" means nothing.

A good buy box is short and specific. You write it before you see listings. And it's hard to change on purpose. If you want to change it, you rewrite it, with numbers. You don't change it in a driveway during a showing.

Slide 3 · Your goals pull against each other, so rank them and let the top two or three decide

Before you describe a property, rank your goals. People want several things from a short-term rental purchase, and they conflict. A ranking tells you which one wins when they do.

The common six are cash flow, long-term ownership, personal use, tax planning, portfolio growth, and how involved you want to be.

Here's how they fight. Personal use pulls against cash flow. Your own peak-season week is a week you didn't rent.

Tax planning can pull against everything else. A deal bought "for the depreciation" can lose money every month. And the deduction may not even be usable, depending on your household. Lesson 0.2 covered why.

Low involvement pulls against cash flow. In 0.2, a full-service co-host at 18% turned Cedar Ridge from $5,572 a year to minus $7,798.

Portfolio growth pulls against safety. If you stretch now to buy again sooner, this house gets a thinner reserve.

Rank your goals 1 to 6. Only the top two or three shape the search. The rest are tie-breakers. And if two owners rank them differently, settle it now. A skipped disagreement tends to come back at the offer.

Slide 4 · Write down the limits you can't wish away; most of them come from your 0.3 scorecard

Constraints are limits you can't wish away. Most of them come straight from lesson 0.3.

Money comes as two caps. The first is the maximum cash to close: down payment, closing costs, and points. The second is the maximum total cash in, which adds furnishing. Both caps have to leave your property reserve and your personal emergency savings untouched. If your 0.3 scorecard carried a funding condition, write it here too.

Time. The hours per week you'll really give it.

And minimum reserves. These are floors. You don't spend below them to make a deal work.

Describe the property

Describe the property in lines concrete enough for Sam's PA.2 and PA.3 filters

  • Market type and its demand driver: drive-to leisure, urban, event-driven, business travel.
  • Property type: detached, townhouse, condo, small multi-unit. It changes legality, lending, and insurance.
  • Location inside the market. Zoning, HOA boundaries, and permit caps can change street by street.
  • Price range. The ceiling comes from your money and downside rules, not from what the market charges.
  • Capacity and amenities. More beds can raise ADR and wear. Water or height brings insurance and safety questions.
  • Operating complexity: septic, wells, long private roads, snow, older systems. Each adds cost or response time.

Slide 5 · Describe the property in lines concrete enough for Sam's PA.2 and PA.3 filters

Now the property itself. Keep each line concrete enough that Sam's filters can apply it.

Market type: name it and its demand driver. Sam's PA.2 picks the actual market.

Property type matters for three reasons: legality, because of condo and HOA rules; lending, because some lenders restrict condos or certain property types; and insurance.

Location inside the market. Rules can change street by street. Zoning districts, HOA boundaries, and permit caps can all differ within one town. Write where you'll look, and where you won't.

Price range. You need a floor and a ceiling. The ceiling comes from your money constraints and your downside rule. It doesn't come from what sellers are asking. I'll show you how to calculate it shortly.

Guest capacity and amenities. More capacity can mean a higher nightly rate. It also means more wear, more cleaning, and more noise risk. Separate what guests expect from what adds complexity. Any amenity with water or height brings insurance and safety questions.

Operating complexity. None of these kills a deal alone. Decide how much you'll accept.

Slide 6 · A property must clear every hard requirement before it gets any more of your time

Hard requirements are the lines a property must clear, every one, before you spend real time on it. They aren't preferences. Fail one, and you pass.

Five belong in almost every short-term rental buy box.

One: legal STR use at the exact address. Unhosted short-term rental of a house you don't live in has to be allowed there. Any HOA or deed restriction has to allow it too. Airbnb itself tells hosts to check local laws and HOA rules. Use the SR.1 checklist and record the official source.

Two: acceptable insurance. That means a written quote from an insurer willing to cover STR use of this house, at a premium your numbers can carry.

Three: lender fit, in writing.

Four: your two cash caps. No exceptions because the house is great.

Five: minimum downside performance. Set a floor on weak-case cash flow after debt service, and a minimum ratio of base-case NOI to principal and interest.

Add a sixth if 0.3 surfaced one. For Maya and Chris, it's that the deal must work with self-management.

Slide 7 · The pass rule names the trigger and the action, and it allows only written changes

The pass rule is one or two sentences you agree on in advance. It tells you what to do when a listing doesn't fit.

A good one has three parts. The trigger: any failed hard requirement, a price above the ceiling, or a preference score below a level you set. The action: pass, and log why. Don't "keep an eye on it" unless the price drops below your ceiling. And the change process: the buy box changes only on purpose. That means in writing, with the new numbers, signed by every owner. Never during a showing.

Why log every pass? Because after 20 passes you'll see a pattern. Maybe every house in your price range fails the insurance quote. Maybe every house near the water fails on permits. That's information. It goes back to Sam's PA.2 market choice. It isn't a reason to lower your standards.

The log also stops you from re-reading the same bad listing three times because it keeps showing up in your feed. You already decided. The log says why.

Slide 9 · Their two cash caps set a price ceiling of $393,000 on the DSCR terms

Now the money constraints become a price. Maya and Chris set two caps, both assumptions. Maximum cash to close is $115,000. Maximum total cash in, including $32,000 of furnishing, is $145,000.

On the DSCR terms, cash to close is 25% down, plus 3% closing costs, plus one point on a loan that's 75% of the price. One point on 75% is 0.75% of the price. So cash to close is 28.75% of the price.

Now divide. The cash-to-close cap: $115,000 divided by 28.75% is $400,000. The total-cash-in cap: $145,000 minus $32,000 of furnishing is $113,000. Divide by 28.75% and you get $393,043.

The tighter cap wins. Their ceiling is $393,000 contract price on these terms.

Their downside rule also gives a price ceiling on its own. For a house with Cedar Ridge's weak-case revenue, the weak case reaches minus $6,000 at about $392,000. That sits right next to the cash ceiling. When two different rules land in the same place, that's a good sign the box is consistent.

The city or county licensing office confirms that unhosted short-term rental use is allowed at each address that passes your other filters. Use the SR.1 checklist. If the answer is unclear, go to an STR attorney. Don't take a listing agent's word for it.

Your insurance agent confirms the premium and the exclusions for STR use on each finalist. Do that before the diligence window, not during it. If insurance fails, you want to know before you've paid for inspections.

Your lender confirms that the property type, location, and planned use fit their loan. They also confirm the terms you used in section C. If the real terms are different, your price ceiling changes. Recompute it.

And your CPA. Talk to them before you rank tax planning above fourth in your goal list. That way the ranking reflects your household's actual situation, not a hope about depreciation.

Every "confirm with" line is a phone call or an email. Keep the replies with your buy box.
