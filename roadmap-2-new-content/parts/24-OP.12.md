### OP.12.0 Learning outcome

After this lesson you can put four options side by side for a property you own: hold, refinance, sell, or sell through a 1031 exchange. You'll compare them on after-tax proceeds, future debt, time, and workload. You'll know how the gain on a sale is built, which parts depreciation touches, and which answers only your CPA can give. You'll leave with a decision worksheet you can hand to that CPA and to a qualified intermediary.

### OP.12.1 One question behind all four options

Every exit decision comes down to one question. Is the equity in this property doing more for you here than it would do somewhere else?

Equity is the value you could turn into cash: market value minus the loan balance, minus the costs of getting it out. Holding keeps that equity in the house. Refinancing pulls some of it out as a new loan. Selling turns it into cash, minus taxes. A 1031 exchange moves it into another investment property and may defer the tax.

Each option mixes five things differently. They are after-tax cash, time to complete, future debt, the work you keep doing, and what you give up (opportunity cost). No option wins on all five. You're choosing a trade-off you can live with.

You'll make this call more than once. Use it whenever your 90-day or annual review from Sam's OP.10 shows a real change. Examples: a big capital repair, a new local rule, a life change, or equity that has outgrown the cash flow.

### OP.12.2 Start with an honest equity number

Four inputs start every exit analysis.

The updated value. Get it from a broker's opinion of value, recent comparable sales, or an appraisal. Don't use the price you'd like. Short-term rental houses sometimes sell with furniture and bookings. Ask the broker whether buyers in your market pay for that.

The loan balance. Take it from the servicer's latest statement, not your amortization schedule. If you plan to sell, request a payoff quote; it can include items the statement doesn't show.

Capital repairs coming in the next few years. A roof, HVAC system, or deck replacement changes the hold case. It can also change the sale price if a buyer's inspector finds it.

The cost of selling: agent commissions, transfer taxes, title and closing fees, and any seller concessions. The canonical example uses 7% of value (assumption). Your agent can give you a line-by-line net sheet.

Value − loan balance − selling costs = net proceeds before income tax. That is not your spendable number yet.

### OP.12.3 Hold, refinance, or sell: the before-tax comparison

Holding is the default. It needs no transaction, but it has a cost you don't see on a statement. If the property earns $5,000 a year (assumption) on $140,000 of equity (assumption), the equity is earning about 3.6%. If you could sell and put the after-tax cash somewhere safer at a similar return, holding carries more risk for the same money. That comparison is your opportunity cost.

Holding makes sense when the property still covers its costs in a weak season, the capital repairs are funded, and the work is manageable. It also makes sense when a sale would trigger a large tax bill you'd rather defer and the property still meets your goals from PA.1.

Refinancing lets you keep the property and pull out cash. OP.11 showed the math: new loan, costs, new payment, and the weak-season test. A refinance doesn't trigger a sale, so it doesn't trigger tax on a gain. It does add debt and payments. Refinance when you have a specific, better use for the cash and the property can carry the bigger loan in a weak year.

Selling ends the risk and the work. It turns equity into cash you can redeploy or keep. It also brings the tax consequences in the next section. Sell when the property no longer fits your goals or the local rules have tightened. Also sell when a large repair doesn't pay back, or the equity has a clearly better use.

### OP.12.4 How the gain on a sale is built

You don't need to calculate your own tax. You do need to know how the calculation is built, so you can collect the right records and read your CPA's answer. Every rule below depends on your facts and the tax year. Treat it as the structure of the conversation, not a result.

**Gain = amount realized − adjusted basis.** Amount realized is roughly the sale price minus selling costs. Adjusted basis starts with what you paid plus certain acquisition costs, goes up by improvements, and goes down by depreciation. [IRS Publication 544](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p544) describes adjusted basis this way, including decreases for depreciation.

**Depreciation reduces basis whether or not you claimed it.** Suppose you didn't claim all the depreciation you were entitled to. [IRS Publication 527](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p527) says you still reduce basis by the full amount you could have deducted. The IRS phrase is depreciation "allowed or allowable." Skipping depreciation doesn't protect you at sale. It only means you lost the deduction. Publication 527 also says basis goes down by any special depreciation allowance (bonus depreciation) and any section 179 deduction you claimed.

**The gain then gets split into pieces that can be taxed differently.** Your CPA does this split. Here is the structure:

1. Personal property, including furniture and many building components a cost-segregation study moves to 5- or 7-year lives, is generally section 1245 property. Land improvements on 15-year lives can be treated differently, so your CPA classifies each asset. [Publication 544](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p544) says gain on section 1245 property is treated as ordinary income to the extent of depreciation allowed or allowable. The ordinary part is the lesser of that depreciation or the gain on those assets. So the recapture depends on how much of the sale price is allocated to those items. That allocation is a CPA and appraisal question.
2. The building itself is section 1250 property. Gain tied to straight-line depreciation on it is called unrecaptured section 1250 gain. [IRS Topic 409](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc409) says that portion is taxed at a maximum 25% rate.
3. Any remaining gain is section 1231 gain under [Publication 544](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p544). Your CPA nets it with your other gains and losses for the year.

Two more items belong on the CPA list. First, if depreciation losses from the property were suspended under the passive activity rules, ask how a sale affects them ([IRS Publication 925](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p925)). Second, ask which state income taxes and any other federal taxes apply to your gain. I have not modeled either here.

Cost segregation matters at this step. Sam's PA.5 lesson showed how a study can move part of the building into 5-, 7-, and 15-year property. If it's eligible, bonus depreciation can deduct it in year one. Those faster deductions reduce basis faster. At sale, more of the gain can land in the ordinary-income bucket. The study mostly changes timing. It can still be worth it; SR.3 is the tool for that conversation.

### OP.12.5 The 1031 exchange: what qualifies and the clock

A like-kind exchange under section 1031 lets you sell one investment property and buy another. You defer the gain instead of paying tax on it now. Deferral is not forgiveness. Ask your CPA how the deferred gain affects the replacement property's basis and future depreciation.

**What qualifies.** Since 2018, section 1031 applies only to real property held for use in a trade or business or for investment. Real property held primarily for sale doesn't qualify ([IRS like-kind exchange tips](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/like-kind-exchanges-real-estate-tax-tips)). Both the property you sell and the one you buy must be held that way. The IRS says property used primarily for personal use, like a primary residence, second home, or vacation home, does not qualify. Exchanges of machinery, equipment, vehicles, and other personal property generally no longer qualify after 2017. That means the furniture in an STR generally can't be part of the like-kind exchange.

**The two deadlines.** You have 45 days from the sale of your property to identify possible replacement properties. The identification must be in writing, signed by you, and delivered to a person involved in the exchange, such as the qualified intermediary. You must receive the replacement property by the earlier of two dates. One is the 180th day after the sale. The other is the due date, including extensions, of your tax return for the year of the sale ([IRS like-kind exchange tips](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/like-kind-exchanges-real-estate-tax-tips); [Form 8824 instructions](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8824)). The IRS treats these deadlines as strict.

**The qualified intermediary (QI).** In a deferred exchange you use a QI to hold the sale proceeds. Your own agent can't serve as the QI. The IRS list includes your real estate agent or broker, accountant, attorney, and employee. It also covers anyone who acted in those roles for you in the previous two years ([IRS like-kind exchange tips](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/like-kind-exchanges-real-estate-tax-tips)). The IRS also warns that some intermediaries have gone bankrupt or failed to perform, and that cost taxpayers their deadlines. Vet the QI: ask how funds are held, what bonding or insurance exists, and who signs withdrawals.

**Reporting.** You report the exchange on [Form 8824](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8824). Ask your CPA how any cash you receive, or a smaller replacement loan, would be treated on that form.

**The vacation-home caveat.** An STR owner who also stays at the property has a personal-use question. The IRS issued [Rev. Proc. 2008-16](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/rp-08-16.pdf), a safe harbor under which it will not challenge whether a dwelling unit with occasional personal use qualifies as property held for investment. The safe harbor has qualifying-use standards in its section 4.02. Summaries of that section describe three conditions. The owner holds the unit for 24 months around the exchange. The owner rents it at a fair rental for 14 days or more in each of the two 12-month periods. Personal use stays under a limit. Have your CPA confirm those specifics against the IRS text before you rely on them. Outside the safe harbor, qualification depends on your facts. Don't assume a property you use personally qualifies automatically.

### OP.12.6 Worked example: Cedar Ridge at the end of year 5

Maya and Chris kept the original DSCR loan (no refinance). This is canonical case B8. Every figure below is an assumption. Tax rates are placeholders for the CPA to replace.

**Step 1. Equity and before-tax proceeds (B8).**

- Value: $446,321 (3% a year growth, assumption).
- Loan balance: $272,519. They paid down $16,231 of principal in five years.
- Selling costs at 7%: $31,242.
- Net proceeds before income tax: $446,321 − $272,519 − $31,242 = $142,560.

**Step 2. Hold.** The canonical model keeps NOI flat. That gives base-case cash flow of $5,572 and weak-case cash flow of -$5,475 a year (B4). On $142,560 of net equity, the base case earns 3.9% ($5,572 / $142,560). That's before any appreciation, which is not spendable income.

**Step 3. Refinance at year 5 instead.** Using the B7 terms (75% LTV, 2.5% costs, 7.0% rate, all assumptions):

- New loan: $446,321 × 0.75 = $334,741.
- Costs: $8,369.
- Cash out: $334,741 − $272,519 − $8,369 = $53,853.
- New principal and interest: $26,724 a year.
- Cash flow after: base $2,485; weak -$8,562.

They'd get $53,853 now and keep the property. Their base-case margin would shrink to $2,485 a year, and a weak year would cost $8,562.

**Step 4. Sell: build the gain two ways.** The amount realized is $446,321 − $31,242 = $415,079. The adjusted basis depends on how they depreciated. I simplified: full-year straight-line for five years, and the remaining building on 39 years (B6). The real numbers depend on placed-in-service dates and conventions.

| | Scenario A: no cost-seg study | Scenario B: B6 cost seg + 100% bonus |
|---|---:|---:|
| Starting basis (price + $6,000 capitalized costs) | $391,000 | $391,000 |
| Furnishings basis | $32,000 | $32,000 |
| Depreciation on 5/7/15-year building parts (bonus) | $0 | -$68,816 |
| Depreciation on furnishings (bonus, if eligible) | -$32,000 | -$32,000 |
| Building straight-line, 5 years | -$40,105 ($8,021 × 5) | -$31,280 ($6,256 × 5) |
| **Total depreciation** | **-$72,105** | **-$132,096** |
| **Adjusted basis** | **$350,895** | **$290,904** |
| Amount realized | $415,079 | $415,079 |
| **Gain** | **$64,184** | **$124,175** |

Now the split. The recapture on section 1245 items depends on how much of the price is allocated to them. I assumed $6,000 for the used furniture in both cases, plus $30,000 for the reclassified building parts in Scenario B. These allocations are assumptions only. I also treated all $30,000 as section 1245 property for simplicity; the CPA may classify the 15-year parts differently.

| Piece of the gain | Scenario A | Scenario B | Placeholder rate |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Section 1245 recapture (ordinary) | $6,000 | $36,000 | 32% (assumption) |
| Unrecaptured section 1250 gain | $40,105 | $31,280 | 25% (maximum per Topic 409) |
| Remaining section 1231 gain | $18,079 | $56,895 | 15% (assumption) |
| **Illustrative federal tax** | **$14,658** | **$27,874** | |
| **Net proceeds after illustrative tax** | **$127,902** | **$114,686** | |

Check the logic in Scenario B. They took $59,991 more depreciation over five years than in Scenario A. At 32%, that's up to $19,197 of earlier tax savings, but only if the losses were usable when taken. At sale, the illustration shows $13,216 more tax. The study moved deductions forward. It didn't erase the tax.

On the after-tax figures, holding earns 4.4% on Scenario A's $127,902 and 4.9% on Scenario B's $114,686. The equity you'd actually walk away with is smaller than the equity on paper.

**Step 5. The 1031 path.** Suppose they sell on October 20, 2031, and want to exchange (dates are assumptions).

- Day 45, the written identification deadline: December 4, 2031.
- Day 180: April 17, 2032. That can be later than the due date of their 2031 return. If it is, the return due date comes first unless they extend. Their CPA confirms the exact date.

If the exchange qualifies, the gain on the real property is deferred. The furniture sits outside the like-kind exchange, so the CPA handles its $6,000 allocation separately. Their 10 personal-use days a year are the question for Rev. Proc. 2008-16. They need their calendar records for the two years before the sale, and a plan for the two years after, to show the CPA.

The exchange also carries next-property assumptions. The replacement property needs its own address-level permission check (SR.1), underwriting (Sam's PA.4), financing (PA.6 and PA.7), and team (OP.7). All of that must be done within 180 days. That clock argues for having the replacement shortlist ready before the sale closes.

**Step 6. The decision.** Here is how the options line up for Maya and Chris.

| Option | Cash now (illustrative) | Future debt | Annual cash flow, base / weak | Work | Main risk |
|---|---:|---:|---|---|---|
| Hold | $0 | $272,519 | $5,572 / -$5,475 | Same | Weak seasons; capital repairs |
| Refinance | $53,853 | $334,741 | $2,485 / -$8,562 | Same | Bigger loan in a weak year |
| Sell | $127,902 (Scenario A) or $114,686 (Scenario B) | $0 | n/a | Ends | Tax due; redeploying cash |
| 1031 exchange | Reinvested | New loan | Depends on replacement | Restarts | Deadlines; QI; qualification |

Which is right depends on their goals from PA.1 and their answers in the worksheet below. The numbers make the trade-offs visible. They don't pick for you.

### OP.12.8 Closing the loop on the track

This is the last lesson in STR Concierge. Look at where the worksheet's inputs came from.

The goals in your exit decision are the goals from your PA.1 buy box. The weak-case test is the same one you ran in 0.3, PA.6, and PA.7. The depreciation history comes from the choices you made with your CPA in PA.10 and Sam's PA.5, using SR.3. The capital repair list comes from your PA.9 inspection and your OP.8 maintenance records. The trailing-12 comes from the review habit Sam set up in OP.10 and that you extended in OP.11.

An exit is only as good as the records behind it. Keep four things from day one. They are the closing documents, every depreciation schedule your CPA files, receipts for improvements and repairs, and a calendar of rental and personal-use days. With those, any of the four options is a short conversation. Without them, even a good sale becomes a reconstruction project.

If you sell or exchange, the course path starts over. A new property goes back to the buy box in PA.1, then market and deal review, financing, diligence, closing, launch, and the 30/90-day review. If you hold, the next decision point is your next annual review.

### OP.12.9 Who confirms what

**Confirm with:** a broker or appraiser the current market value, and whether furniture or future bookings add anything a buyer will pay for.

**Confirm with:** the loan servicer a written payoff quote and any prepayment penalty.

**Confirm with:** your CPA the adjusted basis, depreciation allowed or allowable by asset, and the allocation of sale price to section 1245 items. Also confirm the split of gain, the rates, state tax, any suspended passive losses, and any other federal taxes on the gain.

**Confirm with:** your CPA whether the property is held for investment for section 1031 purposes, whether your personal use fits Rev. Proc. 2008-16, and how cash or debt changes would be reported on Form 8824.

**Confirm with:** a qualified intermediary, and an attorney if needed, the exchange agreement, how funds are held, and the day-45 and day-180 dates.

**Confirm with:** the refinance lender, in writing, value, LTV, rate, costs, and reserves if you choose to refinance.

**Confirm with:** the licensing office or an STR attorney that any replacement property's exact address permits your planned use.
