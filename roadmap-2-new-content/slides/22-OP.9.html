Quality, popularity, price, and location heavily influence how a listing appears. Quality looks at listing content like photos and videos, guest ratings and reviews, communication between hosts and guests, and listing characteristics.

Popularity looks at how guests engage: how often they save a listing to a wishlist, book it, and message the host.

Location matters a lot. The system also encourages variety, so guests see different hosts and prices.

And availability matters. The more dates a listing is open, the more likely it shows up. More flexibility on length of stay helps a listing fit more trips.

That's the published list. When someone tells you about a factor that isn't on it, ask them for the Airbnb page.

The friction log

A question guests ask three times means the house needs a fix, and most bad reviews start as a repeat nobody fixed.

Mark every guest question. "Where's the water shutoff?" three times: the house manual needs a line. "How do the bunk lights work?" four times: the lights need a label.
OP.9 · Guest Experience & Reviews§ OP.9.47 / 20
Slide 7 ·

The friction log is the most useful tool in this lesson, and it's also the simplest.

Every time a guest asks a question, mark it. One question is harmless. "Where's the water shutoff?" asked once is fine. By the third time, the house manual needs a line.

"How do the bunk lights work?" asked four times means the lights need a label. That's the house being unclear.

Most negative reviews start as a repeated question nobody fixed. Something confused the first guest, and they asked. It confused the second guest, and they asked too. The third guest didn't ask. They wrote it in the review.

So the log is a weekly habit. The owner reads it every week and looks for repeats. A repeat gets a fix, with a name and a date. That rule shows up again in the exercise. It's also what drives the Cedar Ridge bunk room decision in the worked example.

Airbnb's review policy bans any trade, incentive, or pressure around a review

Guest and host each have 14 days after checkout to submit a review.

What the policy forbids

  • Coordinating with, manipulating, extorting, incentivizing, or pressuring someone to influence a review
  • Giving or withholding a review for something of value: a discount, refund, reciprocal review, or promise
  • Threatening a negative review to get compensation, a refund, or another incentive

What that means for your request

  • It's neutral: it invites a review and asks for honest feedback
  • It offers nothing and hints at no trade
  • It doesn't tell the guest what rating to give
  • My rule, beyond the policy text: don't ask for "five stars" at all
OP.9 · Guest Experience & Reviews§ OP.9.58 / 20
Slide 8 · Airbnb's review policy bans any trade, incentive, or pressure around a review

After checkout, Airbnb prompts both guest and host to leave a review. According to Airbnb's review pages, each side has 14 days after checkout to submit one.

Airbnb's policy on authentic and trustworthy reviews sets the limits on how you ask. Hosts and guests may not coordinate with, manipulate, extort, incentivize, or pressure another person to influence a review. Reviews may not be given or withheld in exchange for something of value. That includes a discount, a refund, a reciprocal positive review, or a promise not to take negative action. And neither side may threaten a negative review to get compensation or a refund.

So the review request has to be neutral. It invites a review. It asks for honest feedback. It doesn't offer anything, hint at a trade, or tell the guest what rating to give.

I'd go a step further than the policy text. Don't ask for five stars at all. Naming the rating reads as pressure, and the policy prohibits pressure.

A compliant request invites an honest review and asks to hear about problems directly

Send this, 2 or 3 days after checkout

  • "Hi [first name], thanks for staying at Cedar Ridge. If you have a few minutes, we'd appreciate an honest review on Airbnb. If anything wasn't right, please tell us directly too, so we can fix it for the next guests."

Never write this

  • "Leave us 5 stars and we'll give you a discount next time." (Incentive)
  • "We'll return your deposit once you've left a review." (Trade)
  • "We gave you a great review; we hope you'll do the same." (Reciprocal pressure)
  • Any goodwill gesture tied to review timing
OP.9 · Guest Experience & Reviews§ OP.9.59 / 20
Slide 9 · A compliant request invites an honest review and asks to hear about problems directly

Here's a request that works within the rules. It goes out two or three days after checkout. It thanks them, invites an honest review, and asks them to tell you directly if anything wasn't right. That last line matters. It gives an unhappy guest a private place to say it, and it gives you something to fix.

The right column shows what never to write. The discount for five stars is an incentive. The deposit returned after a review is a trade. "We gave you a great review, we hope you'll do the same" is reciprocal pressure.

The fourth one is easy to miss. Don't tie any goodwill gesture to review timing. In OP.8, Maya gave a $100 credit when the internet went down. She sent it during the stay, through the platform, with no mention of reviews. Keep it that way.

Your own review of the guest follows the same rules. Write it on the facts of the stay. Never hold it back to influence what the guest writes.

Answer criticism for future guests: check the facts, fix it, then respond briefly

  1. Wait a day (assumption); don't reply while you're annoyed
  2. Check the facts: turnover photos, the message thread, the friction log for that stay
  3. Sort it: fair and fixable, fair but a known trade-off, or factually wrong
  4. Fix what's fixable before you respond, so you can say it's done
  5. Respond briefly: thank them, name the specific point, state the fix or fact; keep guest details private
Disputes are for policy violations

Hosts can dispute a review that breaks the policy; a dispute doesn't ensure removal. A retaliatory or extortion review may qualify. A fair three-star about a hot bunk room doesn't.

OP.9 · Guest Experience & Reviews§ OP.9.610 / 20
Slide 10 · Answer criticism for future guests: check the facts, fix it, then respond briefly

Airbnb lets hosts post a public response to a published review, as long as it follows the reviews policy. The response shows on the listing with the review. So you're writing for future guests, not arguing with the past one.

The process has five steps. Wait a day, so you don't reply while you're annoyed. Check the facts with the turnover photos, the message thread, and the friction log. Sort the complaint. Fix what you can before you respond, so you can say it's done. Then respond briefly. Never bring up something the guest did unless you need it to correct a factual claim.

Most criticism is fair and fixable. Treat it as free inspection data.

Save disputes for policy violations. Airbnb says a published review stays up unless its author asks for removal within 30 days, or it breaks the policy. Other circumstances can also affect it. You can dispute a review that violates the policy, but a request doesn't guarantee removal. Disputing fair criticism wastes time, and it doesn't fix the house.

Slide 17 · Four rules turn the tracker into decisions instead of a monthly report

These are the decision rules from the exercise. They're what keep the tracker from becoming a report nobody acts on.

First, a complaint reason that shows up twice in a quarter goes on the fix list, with an owner and a date. At Cedar Ridge, that's the bunk room. Two mentions, so it's on the list.

Second, a review below four stars gets a read of the turnover photos and message thread within 48 hours. The 48 hours is an assumption. Pick a window and keep to it.

Third, respond publicly only after the facts are checked and any promised fix is done. If the fix isn't done, you wait.

Fourth, dispute a review only if it breaks Airbnb's reviews policy. If you do dispute one, save the policy section you're relying on. Most reviews won't qualify, and that's fine. The fair ones are telling you what to fix.

Every month, look at whether the fix list changed. If the tracker never moves the fix list, it isn't doing its job.

Slide 20 · Every search and review rule here comes from Airbnb's own help pages

Every rule in this lesson about search and reviews comes from Airbnb's own pages. I checked them on 2026-09-25 through search summaries, because direct fetches were blocked.

The search page, article 39, is the source for the factors: quality, popularity, price, location, variety, availability, and stay flexibility. The ground rules page covers accuracy, reservation commitment, communication, and cleanliness, and how Airbnb enforces them.

The reviews policy, article 2673, is where the limits on incentives, pressure, and trades come from. The reviews-for-homes page gives the 14-day window. The response page covers public responses. The dispute page covers the 30-day author removal and the fact that a dispute doesn't guarantee removal.

The resource center article supports the point that guests filter by amenities.

The only canonical Cedar Ridge number here is 61 stays from B1. Everything in the 90-day tracker, including the 70% review rate, is a teaching assumption. Your own tracker replaces it.
