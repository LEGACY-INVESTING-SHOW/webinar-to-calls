After this lesson you'll have a short operating playbook for your house. A backup cleaner or a new co-host can follow it without calling you.

It has three pieces. The first is a turnover checklist with photo proof, par levels for supplies, and an escalation ladder for a missed clean. The second is a message sequence, with a named owner for every message. The third is an emergency matrix for seven kinds of incident.

Then we'll run all of it at Cedar Ridge three times. There's a normal Sunday turnover. There's a guest problem on a Tuesday, when the internet goes down during a work stay. And there's a real emergency at 1:40 in the morning, when the CO alarm sounds.

The people in these SOPs come from OP.7. The cleaner has calendar access. The handyman takes the after-hours calls. Maya owns guest messaging, and Chris backs her up. If you haven't built your role map yet, do that first. An SOP without a named owner isn't finished.

Slide 2 · A usable SOP names a trigger, one owner, a standard, proof, escalation, and a record

A standard operating procedure, or SOP, is a written routine that gives the same result no matter who follows it. Most short-term rental checklists fail that test. They say "clean kitchen" and leave the standard to the cleaner's mood that day.

Every SOP in this lesson has seven parts. I've put the last two on one line to fit the slide, but they're separate: escalation, then record.

The trigger is what starts it. The owner is one named role, taken from your OP.7 role map. One role, not "the team." The standard says what done looks like, specifically enough that two people would agree. The proof is a photo, a timestamp, or a reading. Escalation says who to call, and by when, if a step fails. The record says where the result gets written down.

Keep each one to a page. The person using it is standing in a driveway with a phone, often on the day they're covering for someone else.

Slide 3 · The turnover starts with a walk-through before anything is touched, so damage is on camera

The turnover runs more than any other SOP. At Cedar Ridge's base case, it runs 61 times a year. Airbnb's ground rules for hosts set cleanliness as an expectation. Hosts should clean between every stay: laundry, trash, vacuuming or sweeping, and wiping surfaces. Your SOP turns that into a standard.

The trigger is the checkout in the calendar. The owner is the turnover cleaner, who has calendar access only, per OP.7. The window is six hours, from a 10 a.m. checkout to a 4 p.m. check-in.

Step two is the one I care about most. The cleaner walks the house before touching anything. They note damage, missing items, a smoke smell, signs of extra guests, and anything left behind. Damage gets photographed before cleaning, because after cleaning, the evidence is gone.

Step three strips all six beds, whether they were used or not. That keeps the linen cycle simple and nobody has to guess which beds were slept in.

Slide 6 · The missed-clean ladder catches a no-show by 11 a.m., not at 3:30 p.m.

The worst operational failure is a guest arriving to a dirty house. It usually starts with a cleaner who doesn't show up and a host who finds out at 3:30 in the afternoon.

The ladder catches it by late morning. The night before, the cleaner confirms in the task app by 8 p.m. If not, Maya calls. If there's still no confirmation by 9, the backup cleaner gets asked to hold the slot.

On the day, the arrival photo should be in by 11. If it isn't, Maya calls the cleaner. No answer in 15 minutes, and the backup goes.

At 2:30 she counts photos. If the turnover isn't half done, she decides whether to delay check-in or send a second cleaner. At 3:45, she reviews the final photos before the door code goes out.

The backup is paid a same-day premium, $200 instead of $150. If that happens four times a year, it costs $200 more. That's cheap next to a refund and a one-star cleanliness review.

Slide 7 · Every guest gets the same seven messages, and a named person checks each one

Every guest gets the same core messages at the same moments. Automation sends most of them. A named person owns each one and checks it. At Cedar Ridge, that's Maya for all seven.

Airbnb lets hosts send scheduled quick replies automatically. They're triggered by events like a new booking, check-in, or checkout. The same help page says you can skip, send early, or edit an upcoming message without changing the template. When you host with a team, members with guest management permissions share the scheduled replies, and messages go out from the team owner.

Look at the day-before message. It carries the door code, so it's held for review. A wrong code in a scheduled message means a family standing on the porch at 4 p.m.

The check-in evening message is manual on purpose. It has to come from someone who can act on the answer.

And human review is a written weekly step. Every Monday, Maya opens the list of scheduled messages for the week and checks each one against the booking.

Slide 8 · No bot answers a message about smoke, gas, leaks, injury, lockouts, or refunds

Automation needs limits. Write these into the SOP and into the settings of any AI messaging tool you use.

The first limit is the important one. No automated or AI reply goes to a message about smoke, fire, gas, alarms, leaks, injury, police, lockouts, or refunds. A person answers those, immediately. A friendly auto-reply to "the CO alarm is going off" is the worst message you could send.

Second, no automated message sends a changed door code. A person checks the lock first, then sends it.

Third, an AI suggestion is a draft. Airbnb offers suggested quick replies that use AI to pick one of your existing replies for common questions. A person approves the suggestion before it goes, unless it's an approved template for a routine question.

Fourth, any exception you grant goes in the booking notes. If Maya allows a late checkout, the cleaner needs to see it.

From Sam's OP.4 lesson, bring back where your messages and tasks actually run, so you can set these limits in the right place.

Slide 9 · The emergency matrix says what to do first, who responds, and who can stop work

An emergency matrix tells the person on the spot what to do first and who to call. It also says who can stop work and what to record. Write it for the guest and the team together. The guest's part goes in the house manual.

Airbnb's help center says to contact local emergency services first if an emergency is in progress. Airbnb also describes an Urgent Support Line in its app for hosts and guests during active reservations. Neither one replaces 911. Neither one replaces your own on-call person.

Read the first action column. For fire and CO, it's the same shape: get out first, then call. Nobody investigates a CO alarm from inside the house.

For a leak, the first action depends on the guest knowing where the water main is. So the shutoff location gets marked in the manual.

Every incident gets a record: times, photos, report numbers, and an insurer notice when there's damage or injury. The fire department and the insurer should confirm these steps for your house.

Slide 10 · Anyone can stop work for safety, but only an owner reopens the calendar

Stop-work authority means the right to halt a turnover, a repair, or the next booking because something isn't safe.

At Cedar Ridge, anyone on the team can stop work for safety. The cleaner can. The handyman can. Nobody needs permission to say "this isn't safe, we stop."

Reopening works the other way. Only an owner can reopen the calendar, and only after the right professional signs off. After a CO alarm, that's the fire department, the HVAC technician, and the gas supplier. Stopping is easy on purpose. Restarting is hard on purpose.

The contact sheet is the other half. It's laminated and kept inside the owner closet door, with a copy in the house manual. It lists 911 and the non-emergency police and fire numbers. It lists the utilities and where the water shutoff is. It lists the on-call phone, the handyman, the plumber, the HVAC company, and the insurer's claim line.

Fill in the real local numbers before the first booking. A blank line on that sheet at 2 a.m. costs time.

Slide 11 · Privacy rules go in the SOPs, because well-meaning people are the ones who break them

Privacy rules belong in the SOPs, because the people who break them usually mean well. A cleaner might want to set up a "nanny cam" to watch for parties. That's not allowed.

Airbnb's device rules prohibit security cameras and recording devices that monitor indoor spaces, even if they're off. Exterior cameras have to be disclosed in the listing. They can't watch indoor spaces or areas like an enclosed outdoor shower. Noise decibel monitors that don't record sound are allowed in common spaces if they're disclosed. They're not allowed in bedrooms, bathrooms, or sleeping areas.

So I put three lines in the turnover SOP and two lines in the data SOP. They're on the slide. The cleaner checks the devices every turnover, the same way they check the alarms.

The data lines matter because footage and guest details are easy to copy. They stay in the platform or the device's own app.

Slide 16 · Four checks tell you whether your SOPs are finished or still drafts

These four checks come from the lesson's decision rules. Run them before your first booking, and again after any incident.

First, every step needs a named owner. If a step says "someone checks the alarms," it isn't finished. Put the role there.

Second, a backup has to have done the SOP once, for real or in a paid trial. A name on a list isn't a backup. A person who has already walked your house with the checklist is.

Third, look at your messaging settings. Can any automated message send a door code? Can one reply to a message about smoke or a leak? The safe answer there is no. If it's yes, change the setting before the next booking.

Fourth, review every SOP after any incident, and at least every six months. The six months is an assumption, so pick your own interval. At Cedar Ridge, the CO incident added a furnace check before heating season. That's what a review is for.

The emergency parts of these SOPs need outside confirmation.
