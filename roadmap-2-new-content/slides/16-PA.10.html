Slide 1 · Title, operator, and tax treatment are three separate choices, and your lender often makes the first

Somebody has already told you to put your rental in an LLC. Somebody else probably said to run it through an S-corp. This lesson sorts those answers out.

By the end you'll be able to separate three questions people blur together. Who holds title? Who operates the rental? How is the activity taxed? You'll know which professional answers each one, and where each name shows up in the paperwork.

You'll leave with a filled decision worksheet and a list of questions for your attorney, CPA, insurer, and lender.

I won't give you a universal recommendation, because there isn't one. The right structure depends on your state, your lender, your insurer, your partners, and your tax facts. Cedar Ridge is hypothetical, and nothing here is legal, tax, lending, or insurance advice.

Slide 2 · Title owner, operator, and tax treatment are three questions with three answers

Here are the three questions.

First, title owner. Whose name is on the deed? That person or entity owns the property and signs as the buyer.

Second, operator. Who runs the rental business? That's whoever holds the Airbnb account, signs the cleaner and co-host agreements, holds the permit, and collects the payouts.

Third, tax treatment. How does the IRS classify the entity and the activity? That decides which forms get filed and how income and losses flow to you.

'Put it in an LLC' answers the first question. 'Run it through an S-corp' answers the third. Neither one answers the question your lender asks first, which is who's borrowing.

These can all be the same, you and your spouse. Or a property LLC might hold title while an operating company runs the listing. Every layer costs money and paperwork. Add one only when it solves a problem you can name.

Legal form versus tax status

Limited liability company

Created under state law.
  • You file with the state and pay its fees
  • State law decides the liability protection
  • The SBA says ownership, liability, taxes, and filing rules vary by state

S corporation

A federal tax status (SBA).
  • An LLC can be taxed as a C corp or an S corp
  • The LLC files Form 2553 to elect it
  • Form 2553 also counts as electing corporate classification (Pub 3402)
  • Shareholder-employees must get reasonable pay before distributions
PA.10 · Entity & Tax Structure Setup§ PA.10.23 / 20
Slide 3 · An LLC is a legal form under state law, and an S-corp is a federal tax election

This is the distinction that clears up most of the confusion.

A limited liability company is created under state law. You file with the state and pay the state's fees. The state's law decides what liability protection you get. The SBA says an LLC separates business and personal liabilities, and that the rules vary by state.

An S corporation is a tax status. The SBA says designations like S corp aren't strictly business structures. An LLC can be taxed as a C corp or an S corp.

To become an S corp, the LLC files Form 2553. IRS Publication 3402 says that filing also counts as electing corporate classification, so you don't file Form 8832 first.

People usually want an S-corp to manage self-employment or payroll taxes on active business income. The IRS requires reasonable pay to a shareholder-employee before non-wage distributions. That means payroll and a second return. For a single STR, that's a CPA question.

Slide 4 · An LLC's default federal tax treatment depends on how many members it has

The IRS lays out the defaults in Publication 3402.

A one-member LLC is disregarded unless it elects otherwise. Its activity shows on the owner's own return, such as Schedule C or Schedule E.

A domestic LLC with two or more members is a partnership by default. It files Form 1065.

Married couples have one extra wrinkle. In community property states, the IRS will respect spouses treating a jointly owned entity as either disregarded or a partnership. Those states are on the slide. Outside them, a two-member LLC owned by spouses is generally a partnership. Your CPA decides which applies to you.

And one warning. If anyone suggests putting the real estate itself inside an S corporation, ask the CPA to walk you through moving the property out, refinancing, and selling. Get that answer before you choose it.

Slide 5 · Your loan type often settles who holds title before you pick an entity

Your financing choice from PA.7 often settles the title question before you pick an entity.

Conventional loans sold to Fannie Mae generally go to individuals. Fannie Mae's Selling Guide requires borrowers to be natural persons, with limited exceptions like certain land trusts. If Maya and Chris take the conventional quote, expect title and the note in their personal names.

DSCR lenders set their own rules. Kiavi says some DSCR lenders allow borrowing through an LLC. That's one lender's page, not a market standard.

An entity loan may also require a personal guarantee from the owners. I couldn't confirm a lender's published guarantee terms, so put that question to your lender in writing.

If a guarantee is required, the LLC doesn't separate you from the loan debt. It may still help with some other claims. Your attorney tells you what it does in your state.

Slide 6 · Decide the entity before closing, because retitling later can trip the due-on-sale clause

Now the caution about moving title after closing.

A mortgage or deed of trust usually has a due-on-sale clause. It lets the lender demand full payment if the property is transferred without consent.

Federal law, 12 U.S.C. 1701j-3, lists transfers a lender can't treat as a trigger on certain residential loans. Examples include some transfers to a spouse or into certain living trusts. A transfer to your own LLC is not on that list. Some lenders will consent to it, and some won't.

So get the lender's written consent before you deed the property anywhere. And ask your title company whether the transfer affects your owner's title policy.

The better path is on the slide. Decide before closing, so the lender, title company, and insurer all draw up documents with the same names. Retitling after closing costs money and can put the loan and the title coverage at risk.

Slide 7 · Insurance follows the named insured, so ask the insurer four direct questions

Your STR policy protects the named insured. Say title sits in an LLC and the policy names you personally. You may have a gap when you file a claim. The reverse is a problem too.

So ask the insurer these four questions, directly and in writing.

Who should be the named insured if title sits in an LLC? Can both the LLC and the owners be named? Does the policy cover the operating company, if that's a separate entity? Does the lender need to be listed as mortgagee, and in what exact name?

Airbnb tells hosts they're responsible for their own insurance and should review coverage and exclusions. It also says AirCover for Hosts doesn't replace it.

An umbrella liability policy is another way people handle liability. Whether an umbrella, an LLC, or both fits your situation is a joint call for your attorney and your insurer.

Slide 8 · Map every closing document to one name, because mismatches break claims later

Draw this map before closing. Every document should match the structure you chose. Mismatches are how a claim, a permit renewal, or a tax filing goes sideways.

This first half covers the purchase itself.

The purchase contract names the buyer. If you're still forming the LLC, your attorney may use an assignment clause. The deed names the title owner. The promissory note names whoever the lender approved as borrower. The mortgage or deed of trust is signed by the title owner, pledging the property.

If the lender requires a personal guaranty, the individual owners sign it. The title policies name the title owner and the lender. The STR insurance policy names the insured and the mortgagee.

Walk down this column with your PA.10 worksheet open. If two rows disagree, fix it before closing, not after.

The second half of the map is the operation.

The permit and tax registration go in whatever name the local office requires. Often that's the owner or the operator. The Airbnb and Vrbo accounts, the payout method, and the platform tax forms go to the operator. The bank account belongs to the entity that earns the income. A note on beneficial owners. The federal reporting rule used to apply to most small LLCs. FinCEN's March 2025 interim final rule exempts entities created in the United States and their owners. That status has changed before, so check FinCEN's page before you form an entity. Your bank will still ask who owns and controls the LLC.

FinCEN also reports that a court vacated its residential real estate reporting rule in March 2026. Your title company tracks what applies to your closing.

PA.10 · Entity & Tax Structure Setup§ PA.10.611 / 20
Slide 11 · A 3.5-night average stay changes which tests the CPA applies, and no entity does the hours

Next, average stay. Under IRS Publication 925, an activity isn't a rental activity for the passive rules if the average customer stay is 7 days or less. Cedar Ridge averages 3.5 nights.

That changes which participation tests the CPA applies. It's the reason people talk about 'the STR loophole.' It's really a set of tests with facts behind them.

Whether your losses can offset W-2 income depends on participation facts. The hours you work matter. So do the hours your cleaner or co-host works. The CPA applies the tests. Your entity doesn't do those hours for you.

One more note for the lender. The CFPB's Regulation Z commentary says a beach house the owner will occupy over 14 days next year isn't treated as non-owner-occupied under that rule. That's a lending rule, not a tax rule. Same personal-use days, different offices. Give both the same honest number.

Questions for the CPA

Send your CPA eight written questions before you choose an entity

  • Which participation tests apply, given our average stay and hours? How should we log time?
  • Does our personal-use plan require dividing expenses, and on what day count?
  • Schedule E or Schedule C, given the services we provide guests?
  • If title goes into a one- or two-member LLC, which return is filed, and what does it cost?
  • Is an S-corp election useful at all? If it holds real estate, what happens at refinance or sale?
  • How do cost segregation and bonus depreciation from PA.5 interact with our entity and hold period?
  • Does the entity limit a future sale or 1031 exchange? (OP.12 covers exits.)
  • What records do you want from day one: bank account, bookkeeping software, mileage, time log?
PA.10 · Entity & Tax Structure Setup§ PA.10.612 / 20
Slide 12 · Send your CPA eight written questions before you choose an entity

Take these questions to your CPA in writing. Written questions get written answers, and you'll want those answers later.

The first two are about facts: which participation tests apply and how to log your time, and whether your personal use means dividing expenses.

The third is about which schedule the activity reports on. That depends on the services you provide guests.

The fourth and fifth are about entities. Which return gets filed if title goes into an LLC, and what it costs to prepare. And whether an S-corp election helps at all.

The sixth connects to Sam's PA.5. Cost segregation and bonus depreciation interact with your entity and how long you'll hold.

The seventh looks at the exit. OP.12 covers that. The eighth is what records the CPA wants from day one. Set those up before your first booking.

PA.10 · Entity & Tax Structure Setup§ PA.10.713 / 20
Slide 13 · For a first property, a holding company often adds cost before it solves anything

You'll hear about holding companies next. A holding company structure usually means a parent LLC that owns several property LLCs. People use it to keep each property's liabilities separate while managing them together.

For a first property, it often adds cost before it solves anything. You'd be paying for filings and returns on entities that don't have a second property to protect yet.

So park it. Revisit it in OP.11 when you're buying property number two.

When you get there, ask your attorney whether your state offers a series LLC. Then ask your lender and your insurer how they treat one. Those two answers often matter more than the structure chart.
