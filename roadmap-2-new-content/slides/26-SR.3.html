A cost-segregation study splits the building into parts, so some of it can be deducted in year one instead of over 27.5 or 39 years.

Slide 2 · A study moves part of the building into short lives you can deduct in year one

In plain words: when you buy a rental, you can't deduct the building's cost all at once. You deduct it a slice at a time over 27.5 or 39 years. That paper write-off for wear on the building is depreciation. Land is never depreciated.

A cost-segregation study splits the building into parts. Some parts, such as land improvements, qualify for 5-, 7-, or 15-year lives. When bonus depreciation, an extra first-year deduction, applies, those short-life parts and your furniture can be deducted in full in year one.

Our running example is Cedar Ridge, a hypothetical three-bedroom lake house. Maya and Chris, a two-earner household with about $185,000 of W-2 income, bought it for $385,000 and furnished it for $32,000. In the base case, a study adds about $67,000 of year-one deduction. At a 32% rate, that's about $21,000 before a $4,500 fee. That only holds if the loss is usable this year.

First, the whole calculation in plain numbers. Then we'll build the workbook that does it.

Here's the whole calculation for Cedar Ridge's base case, worked by hand. The workbook we build next does exactly this.

Basis is $385,000 plus $6,000 of capitalized costs, or $391,000. Land at 20% is $78,200, and land never depreciates. The building is $312,800.

The study moves 22% of the building into short lives. That's $68,816, which leaves $243,984 of remaining building.

Accelerated depreciation is the reclassified parts plus the $32,000 of furnishings, at 100% bonus. That's $100,816, if eligible. Ordinary depreciation is $243,984 divided by 39, or $6,256. Add them and the year-one deduction is $107,072.

Two honest caveats. The base takes a full first year to keep the math simple. With a June start, the building line is closer to $3,389. And the $34,263 tax effect at 32% only holds if all of the deduction is usable this year. Every figure here is a Cedar Ridge assumption.

Slide 12 · Five built-in rules decide whether the deduction can count this year

The lesson lists eight rules. Five of them work like yes-or-no gates, and they're on this slide. The next slide covers the other three, plus a closer look at the loss limits.

Gate one is dates. The IRS says 100% bonus is now permanent for eligible property acquired after January 19, 2025. A binding contract signed on or before that date can change the answer, even if you closed later. That's why Inputs B6 exists. The screen only checks dates. Your CPA confirms eligibility.

Gate two: bonus needs a recovery period of 20 years or less. The building's 27.5 or 39 years is too long, and land isn't depreciable at all.

Gate three is the Publication 527 home-use test. Cedar Ridge's 10 personal days sit under 21.2.

Gate four is Publication 925's average-stay rule. At 3.48 days, Cedar Ridge isn't a rental activity under that rule. That's not bad news. It means the participation tests decide.

Gate five matters most. Your CPA sets the usable share in Inputs B32. If it's zero, the whole deduction carries forward and this year's tax effect is zero.

Three more rules set the building life, late-study filings, and basis

  • Building life is a CPA call: 39 years if short stays count as transient use, 27.5 if not. At 27.5, Cedar Ridge's line is $8,872, not $6,256.
  • Gate five, up close: the $25,000 allowance is gone by $150,000 of MAGI, so it doesn't help Maya and Chris at about $185,000.
  • A study on property placed in service in an earlier year can need Form 3115 (a change in accounting method) with a one-time catch-up adjustment.
  • Depreciation reduces basis whether or not you claim it. The Outputs tab carries both pools to OP.12.
Slide 13 · Three more rules set the building life, late-study filings, and basis

Here are the other three rules, plus a closer look at gate five.

The building life. Publication 946 defines residential rental property by where the income comes from. It excludes hotel, motel, or inn units used mostly on a transient basis. How that applies to a house rented for short stays is a question for your CPA. The Cedar Ridge base case uses 39 years, the cautious choice. At 27.5, its line would be $8,872 a year.

Now gate five. Publication 925 describes a special allowance of up to $25,000 for active participation. It phases out between $100,000 and $150,000 of MAGI. At Maya and Chris's assumed $185,000, it doesn't help them either way. The excess business loss limit was $313,000, or $626,000 joint, for 2025, far above their $107,072.

A late study can need Form 3115, a change in accounting method. Your CPA handles that filing.

And depreciation reduces basis whether or not you claim it. That's why the pools carry forward.

Six mistakes turn an honest estimate into a number that looks like cash

  • Adding the tax effect to operating cash flow or to a lender's math. It's a one-time estimate, not income.
  • Treating the estimated deduction as cash before the CPA sets the usable share.
  • Typing into the Calc tab. Only the shaded input cells take typing.
  • Guessing a CPA cell instead of leaving it blank and marking its status.
  • Applying bonus to land or to the whole building. Land never depreciates.
  • Counting furniture twice: once in Inputs B14 and again inside the study.
Slide 14 · Six mistakes turn an honest estimate into a number that looks like cash

Now that you've seen the rules, here are the mistakes this workbook is built to catch.

The biggest is mixing the tax effect into cash flow. Don't add it to operating cash flow in another model. It's a one-time estimate that depends on your CPA's rulings, not income the property earns. And keep it out of lender math. A lender underwrites cash flow, not deductions.

Second, treating the deduction as cash before your CPA sets the usable share. If the activity is passive, that share can be zero.

Third, typing into the Calc tab. Once someone overwrites a formula, nobody can trust the outputs.

Fourth, guessing. If a cell is your CPA's call, leave it blank and mark it CPA to supply.

Fifth, bonus on land or on the whole building. Bonus applies to the short-life pieces only.

Sixth, counting furniture twice. If your furnishings are already on your books in Inputs B14, ask the study provider how they treated them.

Slide 17 · Studies have no set standards, so get twelve pieces of evidence in writing

The IRS publishes a Cost Segregation Audit Techniques Guide for its own examiners. It's Publication 5653, and the current version is dated February 2025.

It says there are no standards for preparing these studies. It says they vary widely in method, documentation, depth, and preparer expertise. It contrasts a detailed engineering approach built on actual cost records with survey, letter, and rule-of-thumb approaches.

Its principal elements of a quality study include a preparer with expertise and experience, and a reconciliation of allocated costs to actual costs. Its information requests ask who prepared the study, what role each person played, and where the blueprints came from.

So use that guide to shape what you ask for. The left column comes straight from it. The right column is my recommendations for making the study fit this workbook. The asset schedule fills Inputs B17 to B19. The land method ties to B11. The placed-in-service date must match B8. Get all twelve in writing before you engage the provider.
