### PA.8.0 Learning outcome

After this lesson you can set a walk-away price before you talk to the seller. You'll build it from base-case and weak-case net operating income, your loan terms, and the cash you actually have. You'll know the eight contract terms that protect that number. They are earnest money, three contingencies (inspection, financing, and appraisal, with any appraisal gap), seller concessions, closing date, furniture, and existing bookings. And you'll record every negotiation decision as proceed, renegotiate, or walk, with the reason.

### PA.8.1 Set the ceiling before you see the seller's number

The asking price is the seller's opening position. It tells you what the seller hopes to get. It says nothing about what the house can carry as a rental.

A common way short-term rental (STR) offers go wrong is working backward from the asking price. The buyer sees $399,000, then nudges the nightly rate up until the spreadsheet works. Now the offer depends on the most optimistic line in the model.

Reverse the order. Decide the most you'll pay first, from numbers you'd defend to a lender. Then negotiate below that number. If the seller won't come under it, you walk, and you already know why.

Sam's PA.4 lesson is the live underwriting demo. It builds the package: revenue, expenses, cap rate, and cash-on-cash return. This lesson takes two lines from that package and turns them into a price. Don't redo PA.4's work here. Bring those two numbers back.

A few terms first, so the math reads cleanly:

- NOI (net operating income) is rental revenue minus operating costs, before the loan payment. Property tax and insurance already sit inside it.
- Base-case NOI is your expected year. Weak-case NOI is a bad year, with lower nightly rates and fewer booked nights. Both come from your PA.4 underwriting.
- P&I is principal and interest, the loan part of the payment. PITIA is principal, interest, taxes, insurance, and association dues: the full monthly payment.
- A DSCR loan is sized mostly on the property's income, not your personal income. DSCR stands for debt service coverage ratio. PA.6 and PA.7 cover it.
- A point is an upfront loan fee equal to 1% of the loan amount.

The example throughout is Cedar Ridge, the course's hypothetical lake house. Maya and Chris are the course's example buyers. Their numbers carry over from earlier lessons, and every one is an assumption for teaching.

### PA.8.2 Three tests that set a walk-away price

Your walk-away price is the lowest of three ceilings. Test 2 comes in two versions, strict and funded, and you pick one before you run the numbers.

**Test 1: base-case cash flow.** At your expected revenue, the house must produce at least a minimum annual cash flow after the loan payment. You set the minimum. Set it above zero, because base-case years still bring surprises.

**Test 2: weak-case survival.** In a weak year, the house should break even. If it can't, you need a written, funded plan for the shortfall. A buyer who can't fund a weak year should hold the strict breakeven line.

**Test 3: cash.** Everything you put in, plus the reserves you promised yourself, must fit inside the cash you have. Count the down payment, closing costs, points, furnishing, lender reserves, a weak-year reserve, and your personal emergency fund.

Use the more expensive financing quote for all three tests. Maya and Chris chose conventional first in PA.7, with DSCR as the backup. The DSCR quote costs more: a higher rate plus a point. Until the conventional approval is in writing, they run every test on the DSCR quote: 7.25%, 1 point, 25% down. On Cedar Ridge that point is about $2,888.

Why cash flow and not cap rate? Cap rate ignores your loan. Cash flow shows whether you can hold the house after the loan payment.

### PA.8.3 Worked example: Cedar Ridge's walk-away price

Inputs from the Cedar Ridge example (all assumptions): base-case NOI $29,209 and weak-case NOI $18,162. Property tax ($3,850) and insurance ($3,200) are already inside NOI. Furnishing is $32,000, and closing costs are 3% of price plus points.

Maya and Chris now have $231,000 in liquid savings (assumption). That's the $210,000 from lesson 0.3, their capital and readiness checklist, plus six months of saving $3,500 a month. It meets the condition they set in 0.3: no offer until the full cash stack is funded.

Before they run any numbers, they write down their rules. The rules are theirs to set, so I've marked them as assumptions:

- Minimum base-case cash flow: $5,000 a year (assumption).
- Weak-case rule: funded. They have two salaries (W-2 income) and savings. So they decide in writing to carry a weak-year shortfall of up to $6,000 a year (assumption). They hold two years of it in a separate reserve.
- Personal emergency fund untouched: $51,000, the six months of essential spending they set in 0.3 (assumption).
- Lender reserves: 6 months of PITIA (assumption). This money has to sit in their account. It isn't spent at closing.
- A buffer for after signing: keep at least $10,000 of the $231,000 unallocated whenever a surprise adds cost (assumption). This buffer isn't part of the ceiling. It's the rule they use for a low appraisal or an inspection finding (PA.8.6).

Here's the arithmetic, step by step.

**Test 1.** NOI already covers tax and insurance, so cash flow is NOI minus principal and interest.

Maximum annual P&I = $29,209 - $5,000 = $24,209, or $2,017 a month.

At 7.25% over 30 years, $2,017 a month supports a loan of about $295,732.

A loan calculator or a spreadsheet does that step. In a spreadsheet, PV(0.0725/12, 360, -2017) gives about $295,700. The $295,732 comes from the unrounded payment of $2,017.42. The exercise shows how.

With 25% down, the loan is 75% of price. Price ceiling = $295,732 / 0.75 = **$394,310**.

**Test 2, funded version (their rule).** They can carry a shortfall of up to $6,000, so P&I can run $6,000 above weak-case NOI.

Maximum annual P&I = $18,162 + $6,000 = $24,162. That supports a loan of about $295,158.

Price ceiling = $295,158 / 0.75 = **$393,544**.

That's a real choice, and they made it on purpose, before seeing any price. A buyer without their income or savings should use the strict version below.

**Test 2, strict version (what a buyer on the strict rule would see).** Breakeven in the weak case means P&I can't exceed weak NOI.

Maximum annual P&I = $18,162. That supports a loan of about $221,863.

Price ceiling = $221,863 / 0.75 = **$295,818**.

That's $103,182 under the asking price. Could more cash close the gap instead? Here's the check at $385,000:

- A breakeven loan of $221,863 means a down payment of $163,137, which is 42.4% of the price.
- Add closing costs and the $32,000 furnishing budget, and the total is about $208,906.
- Once the $51,000 emergency fund is set aside, they have $180,000 to put into a property.

Even at $385,000, breaking even would take about $208,906 of cash, more than the $180,000 they have. Under the strict rule, Cedar Ridge doesn't work anywhere near the asking price.

**Test 3.** Total cash needed at a given price has seven parts:

- the 25% down payment
- 3% closing costs
- 1 point on the loan
- $32,000 furnishing
- 6 months of PITIA in lender reserves
- two years of weak-case shortfall
- the $51,000 emergency fund

The cash ceiling is the highest price where that total stays at or under $231,000. Take away the emergency fund and $180,000 is left for everything else. The payment, the reserves, and the shortfall all rise with price. So find this ceiling by trying prices in a spreadsheet until the total reaches the limit. It lands at about **$409,984**.

**The walk-away price.** Maya and Chris chose the funded version of Test 2 before running numbers, so the strict $295,818 line doesn't apply to them. Take the lowest ceiling that does apply: $393,544 from the funded weak-case test, just under Test 1's $394,310. Round down: **$393,000**. Rounding down to the nearest $1,000 keeps the ceiling on the safe side. A buyer who can't fund a weak year uses the strict line, and at $295,818 this house is a pass.

The $10,000 buffer isn't inside that number. At $393,000 they'd keep only about $7,500 unallocated. So the walk-away price is the most they'll ever sign at, and they aim to sign well under it.

The asking price of $399,000 fails. Their eventual contract price of $385,000 passes.

Here's the same check at four prices. All figures are assumptions, DSCR quote.

| Price | Loan (75%) | Total cash in | Annual P&I | Base cash flow | Weak cash flow |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| $399,000 | $299,250 | $146,712 | $24,497 | $4,712 | -$6,335 |
| $392,000 | $294,000 | $144,700 | $24,067 | $5,142 | -$5,905 |
| $385,000 | $288,750 | $142,688 | $23,637 | $5,572 | -$5,475 |
| $372,000 | $279,000 | $138,950 | $22,839 | $6,370 | -$4,677 |

At $399,000, both tests fail: base cash flow is under $5,000 and the weak shortfall is over $6,000.

At $385,000, the cash plan looks like this:

| Use of cash at $385,000 | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Total cash in (cash to close + furnishing) | $142,688 |
| Lender reserves, held not spent: 6 months PITIA (6 x $2,557) | $15,342 |
| Weak-year reserve, 2 x $5,475 | $10,950 |
| Personal emergency fund | $51,000 |
| **Total committed** | **$219,980** |
| Unallocated from $231,000 | $11,020 |

One more caution. The model holds property tax at $3,850. Some counties reassess after a sale, and the tax could rise. Ask your agent or the county assessor what the tax would be at your price before you finalize the ceiling.

### PA.8.4 The terms that protect your ceiling

Price is one term. Eight others decide how much risk you carry between signing and closing. Purchase contracts are state-specific, often on a standard form your agent uses. Treat everything below as questions for your agent and attorney, not as rules.

**Earnest money.** This is the deposit you put down when the seller accepts, often held by an escrow or title company. It shows the seller you're serious. It goes toward your cash to close. The CFPB's [Loan Estimate explainer](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/loan-estimate/) notes that your estimated cash to close is reduced by any deposit you've already paid. The deposit doesn't lower the total cash you need. You pay part of it earlier, and it's credited at closing. Whether you get it back if the deal fails depends on the contract and which contingencies are still open. A larger deposit can make an offer stronger. It also raises what you could lose if you miss a deadline.

**Inspection contingency.** This gives you a set number of days to inspect and then cancel or renegotiate. For an STR, you're also testing the business: permits, homeowners association (HOA) or deed restrictions, insurance quotes, septic capacity for your guest count. PA.9 builds that list. Make sure the period is long enough to get STR insurance quotes and a written answer from the licensing office.

**Financing contingency.** This lets you cancel if your loan isn't approved by a date. Match the date to your lender's realistic timeline, not the seller's preference. If you're on the conventional route with a DSCR backup, the date needs room for a switch.

**Appraisal contingency and appraisal gap.** Lenders generally size the loan on the lower of the price or the appraised value; ask yours to confirm. If the appraisal comes in low, the loan shrinks and you need more cash. An appraisal contingency lets you renegotiate or cancel. An appraisal gap clause promises the seller you'll cover some or all of a shortfall with cash. Only offer gap coverage you've already counted in Test 3.

**Seller concessions.** The seller pays some of your closing costs, usually as a credit. The CFPB's Loan Estimate explainer notes that seller credits reduce your estimated cash to close. Lenders can limit how much the seller contributes and what the money can pay for. The limit may depend on the occupancy type, meaning how the lender classifies the house (PA.7 covers second home versus investment property). Ask your lender for its limit in dollars before you ask the seller.

**Closing date.** Two things drive it. Your rate lock has an expiration date, and extensions cost money. Your launch season matters too. Closing in time to furnish, photograph, and list before Cedar Ridge's lake season (its peak booking season) is worth more than a small price cut. Work backward from the date you want the first guest.

**Furniture and equipment.** A seller may offer to include furniture, a golf cart, a boat lift, or a hot tub. Ask three questions. Does it fit your design and guest plan (OP.6)? Is it in usable condition? How does your lender treat personal property included in the price? Ask before you agree to pay for it. If you want an item, list it in the contract or on a separate bill of sale. That's a short document for the sale of the items. If you don't, say it's excluded so the seller removes it.

**Existing bookings.** If the house already takes guests, ask whether future reservations transfer, who keeps those payments, and whether the platform even allows a transfer. PA.9 picks this up.

### PA.8.5 Before you waive or shorten a contingency

Sellers sometimes ask for a shorter inspection period, no financing contingency, or appraisal gap coverage. Get written answers to these before you agree.

Ask your agent:

1. If I shorten the inspection period, can we still fit the inspection, the septic or sewer check, and the STR insurance quote?
2. What happens to my earnest money if I cancel after this contingency expires?
3. What's normal in this market for this type of house? Is the seller's request unusual?
4. Can the permit or licensing answer arrive before the inspection period ends?

Ask your lender:

1. How many days do you realistically need to approve this loan with this property type?
2. What happens to my loan if the appraisal comes in $10,000 low?
3. Does your approval depend on proof of STR permit eligibility? When do you need it?
4. What's your limit on seller credits for this occupancy type?
5. When does my rate lock expire, and what does an extension cost?

If any answer puts your earnest money at risk for a problem you can't test in time, keep the contingency.

### PA.8.6 What changes after a low appraisal or an inspection finding

Two practice scenarios, both what-ifs on the accepted $385,000 contract. All figures are assumptions. Both use the buffer rule from PA.8.3: keep at least $10,000 of the $231,000 unallocated after any new cost.

**Scenario 1: the appraisal comes in at $375,000.**

The lender lends 75% of the lower value: $375,000 x 0.75 = $281,250. The price is $10,000 above the appraisal. The loan drops by 75% of that gap, which is $7,500. So at the full price they'd need $7,500 more cash.

| Option | Price | Cash to close | Total committed | Unallocated | Rule met? |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| As planned (appraises at $385,000) | $385,000 | $110,688 | $219,980 | $11,020 | Yes |
| Pay the full gap | $385,000 | $118,112 | $225,870 | $5,130 | No |
| Split the gap | $380,000 | $112,962 | $220,720 | $10,280 | Yes |
| Seller drops to value | $375,000 | $107,812 | $215,570 | $15,430 | Yes |

The smaller loan lowers PITIA to $2,506, so reserves and the weak-year shortfall shrink a little. Base cash flow rises to $6,186 and the weak case improves to -$4,861 in every option, because the loan is the same $281,250.

Decision rule: renegotiate. Ask the seller to drop to $375,000. Accept anything up to $380,000. If the seller holds at $385,000 and the appraisal contingency is still open, walk. They'd have to spend their margin to pay a price the appraiser didn't support. If the appraisal contingency has already expired, the earnest money may be at risk. Ask your attorney before you walk (PA.8.9).

**Scenario 2: the inspection finds $4,500 of work (assumption).**

Say the water heater is at the end of its life and several deck boards are soft. If Maya and Chris absorb the cost, total committed rises to $219,980 + $4,500 = $224,480. Unallocated cash falls to $6,520, under their $10,000 buffer.

Decision rule: renegotiate. Ask the seller to repair before closing, or to reduce the price. Ask the lender first whether a seller credit can go toward repairs or only toward closing costs. If the finding is a safety issue for guests, it gets fixed before launch either way. PA.12 covers that sign-off.

A different finding changes the answer. A failing septic system sized for fewer guests than you planned isn't a price problem. It can break the business plan. That's a walk unless the fix and the permit both work on paper.

### PA.8.7 Mock offer and counteroffer

Here's how the Cedar Ridge negotiation could run. The terms and amounts are assumptions for teaching, except the course example's asking and contract prices.

**Round 1: Maya and Chris offer.**

| Term | Offer |
|---|---|
| Price | $372,000 |
| Earnest money | $3,720 (1%) |
| Inspection contingency | 10 days |
| Financing contingency | 30 days |
| Appraisal contingency | Yes, no gap coverage |
| Seller concessions | None requested |
| Closing | 45 days |
| Furniture | Excluded; seller removes |

Why $372,000? It's $21,000 under their $393,000 walk-away price. That leaves room to move up without crossing the line.

**Round 2: the seller counters.**

| Term | Counter |
|---|---|
| Price | $392,000 |
| Earnest money | 2% |
| Inspection contingency | 7 days |
| Financing contingency | Unchanged (30 days) |
| Appraisal contingency | Unchanged (yes, no gap coverage) |
| Closing | 30 days |
| Furniture | Included for an extra $8,000 |

Maya and Chris check the counter against their tests. $392,000 is under $393,000, but only by $1,000. Base cash flow is $5,142, barely above the $5,000 minimum. The weak shortfall is $5,905, just inside $6,000. It passes the three tests on paper with almost no margin.

The buffer is thin too. Signing at $392,000 would leave only about $7,900 unallocated. That's under their $10,000 buffer before any surprise shows up.

The terms matter as well. Seven days isn't enough to get the septic inspection and an STR insurance quote, according to their agent. A 30-day close is tight for their lender. The furniture doesn't fit the design plan in OP.6, and their lender told them it won't count it in the loan (assumption for this mock).

**Round 3: Maya and Chris counter.**

| Term | Counter |
|---|---|
| Price | $385,000 |
| Earnest money | $7,700 (2%), accepting the seller's request |
| Inspection contingency | 10 days |
| Financing contingency | 30 days |
| Appraisal contingency | Yes, no gap coverage |
| Closing | 45 days |
| Furniture | Excluded |

They gave the seller what cost them little: a larger deposit they plan to put toward closing anyway. The larger deposit is low-risk here because the inspection, financing, and appraisal contingencies all stay open. If one of those were waived, more of that deposit could be at risk (PA.8.4). They held the terms that protect their ceiling: inspection time, financing time, and the appraisal contingency.

The seller accepts at $385,000, which is $14,000 (3.5%) under asking. That's the course example's contract price. No seller credit, so their total cash to close stays $110,688. The $7,700 deposit is part of that total, paid early.

### PA.8.8 Exercise: your walk-away worksheet and decision log

**Part 1: set your ceiling.** Use Sam's PA.4 numbers and your more expensive financing quote.

| Input | Your number | Source |
|---|---:|---|
| Base-case NOI | | PA.4 |
| Weak-case NOI | | PA.4 |
| Rate, term, points | | Written quote (PA.6 / PA.7) |
| Down payment % | | Written quote |
| Closing costs % | | Lender or agent estimate |
| Furnishing and launch budget | | OP.6 plan |
| Liquid cash available | | Bank statements |
| Minimum base-case cash flow | | Your rule |
| Weak-case rule (breakeven, or maximum funded shortfall) | | Your rule |
| Personal emergency fund kept aside | | Your rule |
| Lender reserves required | | Written quote |

| Ceiling | Formula | Result |
|---|---|---:|
| Test 1 | (Base NOI minus minimum cash flow) → max loan at your rate → divide by loan-to-price | |
| Test 2 | (Weak NOI plus allowed shortfall) → max loan → divide by loan-to-price | |
| Test 3 | Highest price where total committed cash ≤ liquid cash (try prices in a spreadsheet) | |
| **Walk-away price** | Lowest of the three, rounded down | |

To turn an annual P&I limit into a loan amount, use your lender's calculator or a spreadsheet's PV function: PV(rate/12, months, -monthly payment). Example: PV(0.0725/12, 360, -2017) gives about $295,700. Enter the rate as a decimal, and use 360 months for 30 years.

**Part 2: your offer terms.** Fill in price, earnest money, and each contingency with its days. Add any appraisal gap and its dollar cap. Then add seller concessions (in dollars, within the lender's limit), closing date, and included or excluded items.

**Part 3: your decision log.** Before your first offer, write your buffer: the minimum cash you'll keep unallocated after any surprise cost. Then, for every offer, counter, appraisal, and inspection result, record the date, the numbers, and one of three decisions: proceed, renegotiate, or walk. Write the reason in one line, tied to a test or a rule.

### PA.8.9 Who confirms what

**Confirm with:** your real estate agent, the local purchase contract form, contingency periods, earnest money handling, and what's normal in this market.

**Confirm with:** your attorney, what each contingency lets you do, when your earnest money is at risk, and how any appraisal gap clause is worded.

**Confirm with:** your lender, the maximum seller credit for this occupancy type, whether it can cover repairs, how personal property in the price is treated, the approval timeline, and the rate lock expiration.

**Confirm with:** the county assessor or your agent, the likely property tax at your purchase price.

**Confirm with:** your insurer, an STR quote inside the inspection period.

**Confirm with:** the local licensing office, that STR use is allowed at this address before the inspection contingency expires (SR.1).
