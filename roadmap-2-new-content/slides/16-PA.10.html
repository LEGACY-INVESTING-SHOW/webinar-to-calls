Your lender often picks the entity before you do

Slide 1 · Your lender often picks the entity before you do

Somebody has already told you to put your rental in an LLC. Somebody else probably said to run it through an S-corp. This lesson sorts those answers out.

People blur three questions together. Who holds title? Who operates the rental? How is the activity taxed? By the end you'll know which professional answers each one, and where each name shows up in the paperwork.

I won't give you a universal recommendation, because there isn't one. The right structure depends on your state, your lender, your insurer, your partners, and your tax facts. And very often your lender settles the first question before you get to choose.

Slide 2 · Title, operator, and tax treatment are three separate choices

Here are the three questions.

Title owner is whose name is on the deed. That person or entity signs as the buyer. Operator is who runs the rental: whoever holds the Airbnb account, the permit, the cleaner and co-host agreements, and the payouts. Tax treatment is how the IRS classifies the entity and the activity, and which forms get filed.

'Put it in an LLC' answers the first question. 'Run it through an S-corp' answers the third. Neither one answers the question your lender asks first, which is: who is borrowing?

All three can be you and your spouse, personally. Or they can differ. Every layer you add costs money and paperwork, so add one only when it solves a problem you can name.

Slide 3 · An S-corp is a tax election an LLC can make

A limited liability company is created under state law. You file with the state, you pay its fees, and the state's law decides what liability protection you get.

An S corporation is a federal tax status that an LLC can elect. The IRS sets the defaults by who owns the LLC. One member is disregarded, so the activity shows on your own return, on Schedule C or E. Two or more members is a partnership that files Form 1065. Spouses in a community property state can treat it either way, and your CPA decides which applies. File Form 2553 and it's taxed as an S corp.

An S-corp means payroll, a reasonable salary for any owner who works in it, and a second return. If anyone suggests putting the property itself in an S corporation, ask your CPA what happens when you refinance or sell.

Slide 4 · Your loan type often decides who holds title

Your financing often settles the title question before you pick an entity. PA.7 compares the two loan paths.

Conventional loans sold to Fannie Mae go to individual people, with limited exceptions like certain land trusts. Take Maya and Chris, the couple buying our Cedar Ridge lake house. If they take the conventional quote, expect title and the note in their personal names.

DSCR lenders set their own rules, and some let an LLC borrow. Ask yours in writing, and ask whether it requires a personal guarantee from the owners.

If you sign a guarantee, the LLC doesn't separate you from the loan debt. It may still separate you from some other claims. Your attorney explains which ones in your state.

Slide 5 · Pick the entity before closing, not after

Now the order of things. A mortgage or deed of trust usually has a due-on-sale clause. It lets the lender demand full payment if the property is transferred without consent.

Federal law lists some transfers a lender can't treat as a trigger on certain residential loans, like some transfers to a spouse or into certain living trusts. A transfer to your own LLC isn't on that list. Some lenders will consent to it, and some won't.

So decide before closing, as early as you can, and ask your lender how late the borrower's name can change. Then the lender, title company, and insurer all draw up documents with the same names. If you've already closed in your own names, get the lender's written consent before you deed the property anywhere. And ask your title company whether it affects your title policy.

Ask your insurer four questions about the named insured

  1. Who's named insured if an LLC holds title?
  2. Can the LLC and owners both be named?
  3. Is a separate operating company covered?
  4. Mortgagee listed, and in what exact name?
Slide 6 · Ask your insurer four questions about the named insured

Your STR policy protects the named insured. Say title sits in an LLC and the policy names you personally. You may have a gap when you file a claim. The reverse is a problem too.

So ask the insurer these four questions, in writing. Who should be the named insured if an LLC holds title? Can both the LLC and the owners be named? Does the policy cover the operating company, if that's a separate entity? Does the lender need to be listed as mortgagee, and in what exact name?

Airbnb tells hosts they're responsible for their own insurance, and AirCover for Hosts doesn't replace it. An umbrella liability policy is another way people handle liability. Whether an umbrella, an LLC, or both fits you is a joint call for your attorney and your insurer.

Slide 7 · Every document must show the names you chose

Draw this map before closing. Every document should match the structure you chose. Mismatches are how a claim, a permit renewal, or a tax filing goes sideways.

The deed names the title owner. The promissory note names whoever the lender approved as borrower. The insurance policy names the insured, plus the lender as mortgagee. The Airbnb and Vrbo accounts and the payouts go to the operator. The permit goes in whatever name the local office requires.

The written lesson has the full map. It adds the purchase contract, the mortgage, any guaranty, the title policies, the bank account, vendor agreements, utilities, and the operating agreement. Walk down it with your decisions open, and fix any wrong name before closing, not after.

Slide 8 · Ten personal days stays under the 21.2-day limit

Some tax facts matter whichever structure you pick, and your CPA needs them before advising on the entity. Personal-use days is one.

Maya and Chris plan about 10 personal days a year. Under IRS Publication 527, a dwelling counts as a home when personal use goes over 14 days or 10% of fair-rental days, whichever is greater. I'm counting the 212 rented nights as fair-rental days. 10% of that is 21.2 days, so the limit is 21.2. Ten days sits under it.

Any personal use still means dividing expenses. Here that's 212 rented days out of 222 days used, or 95.5% rental. Empty nights aren't in that count. Your CPA uses this alongside the depreciation work from PA.5, Sam's cost segregation lesson.

  • Participation tests decide if losses offset wages
  • Your hours and your cleaner's hours count
  • An LLC doesn't log the hours for you
Slide 9 · A 3.5-night average stay changes the tax tests

Next, average stay. Under IRS Publication 925, an activity isn't a rental activity for the passive rules if the average guest stay is 7 days or less. Cedar Ridge averages 3.5 nights.

So the usual rental-loss rules don't automatically apply. Participation tests decide whether a loss can offset your other income. That's what people mean by 'the STR loophole': the chance that losses, such as early depreciation, can offset W-2 wages. It's really a set of tests with facts behind them.

The hours you work matter, and so do your cleaner's or co-host's hours. Your CPA applies the tests. Your entity doesn't do those hours for you. And give your lender and your CPA the same honest personal-use number.

  • Which return does an LLC file, at what cost?
  • Is an S-corp election useful at all?
  • Does the entity limit a sale or 1031?
Slide 10 · Send your CPA these questions in writing

Take these questions to your CPA in writing. Written questions get written answers, and you'll want those answers later.

Ask which participation tests apply and how to log your time. Ask whether your personal use means dividing expenses. Ask whether it's Schedule E or Schedule C, which depends on the services you give guests. Ask which return an LLC would file and what it costs to prepare, and whether an S-corp election helps at all. And ask whether the structure limits a future sale or 1031 exchange. OP.12 covers the exit.

Slide 11 · Cedar Ridge could close under three structures

Maya and Chris are married, both on W-2 income, and they'll self-manage with a local cleaner. Here are three structures, laid out for their professionals. I'm not recommending any of them. Each depends on answers they don't have yet.

Option A keeps everything in their personal names. It fits either loan, and insurance plus an umbrella handles liability.

Option B puts title in Cedar Ridge LLC on a DSCR loan. That works only if the lender lends to LLCs, and a guarantee is likely. It files a partnership return, unless a community property exception applies.

Option C keeps title personal and puts the operation in a separate LLC under a written agreement. The open question is whether that holds up if challenged.

A holding company can wait. For a first property it often adds cost before it solves anything. Revisit it in OP.11.

Slide 12 · Option B's LLC costs $1,625 a year

Let's put a number on Option B. Assume a $300 state annual report fee, a $125 registered agent, and $1,200 for a partnership return. That's $1,625 a year, before one-time formation costs. State fees vary widely, so replace the $300 with your state's actual fee.

Their base-case cash flow after PA.9's due diligence is $5,172 a year. Take off $1,625 and it's $3,547. That's $1,453 under the $5,000 minimum yearly cash flow they set for themselves in PA.8.

That doesn't make Option B wrong. It means the liability benefit has to be worth about $1,625 a year to this household. Their attorney and insurer are the people who can tell them whether it is.

Slide 13 · Ask every professional from the same worksheet

Here's the exercise. Fill in your facts first, before your first call with any professional. Which state the property sits in and where you live. Who owns what share. Whether you're married, and in a community property state. Your loan path, personal-use days, average stay, and who does the work.

Then ask each professional their questions from the same sheet. Start with the lender, because the attorney's questions depend on whether a guarantee is required. Write every answer down with a date.

Last, record each decision: title owner, operator, tax classification, liability coverage, and when you'll open the bank account. Give each one a reason, who confirmed it, and a date. The lender, title company, and insurer need the same names in writing a week before closing. If they don't, don't close until they do.
