### PA.6.0 Learning outcome

After this lesson you can read a DSCR (debt service coverage ratio) loan quote the way the lender reads it. You'll know which income the lender puts on top of the ratio and which payment goes underneath. You'll also see why a gross-based ratio above 1.0 can still leave you losing money. You'll leave with a written question list for two lenders and a table for comparing their written offers on the same house.

### PA.6.1 What the title means, and what it leaves out

The title, "How They Qualify the Property, Not You," is shorthand. It points at the main difference from a regular mortgage. A DSCR lender sizes the loan mostly on the property's rental income, not your pay stubs.

But the lender still looks at you. Kiavi is a lender that publishes its DSCR loan terms online. I use its pages as examples in this lesson because they're public. That isn't an endorsement. Kiavi's [complete guide to DSCR rental loans](https://www.kiavi.com/the-complete-guide-to-dscr-rental-property-loans) says the lender considers the borrower's credit score, the available down payment, and the property's coverage ratio. Expect a DSCR lender to check some mix of these:

- your credit score and credit history
- how much cash you're putting in, which sets your loan-to-value (LTV): the loan as a percent of the price. 25% down means 75% LTV
- your reserves after closing, if that lender requires them
- the property type, condition, and appraisal
- whether short-term rental use is legal at that address
- the entity you'll hold title in, and who signs a personal guarantee

So "qualifies the property" means the property's income replaces your salary (your W-2 income) in the math. It doesn't mean nobody asks about you. Kiavi's terms are one lender's terms. Other lenders publish different minimums, and the same lender can change its terms. Treat every lender page in this lesson as an example, then get your own written quote.

### PA.6.2 The ratio itself: income on top, payment underneath

DSCR stands for debt service coverage ratio. In plain words: how many dollars of property income the lender counts for every dollar of loan payment.

The top number (the numerator) is some version of the property's income. The bottom number (the denominator) is some version of the payment. Every lender writes its own definition. Kiavi's material describes a common version: gross rent divided by PITIA, which is principal, interest, property taxes, insurance, and any HOA dues ([Kiavi DSCR guide](https://www.kiavi.com/the-complete-guide-to-dscr-rental-property-loans)). Kiavi's material also says lenders calculate it with different inputs. Some use gross rent. Some use a vacancy-adjusted figure, which is income cut down for the time the property sits empty. Some include items like flood insurance in the payment, while others leave them out.

That flexibility is the whole problem. The same house can produce very different ratios depending on three choices the lender makes:

1. Which income: gross booking revenue, a discounted version of it, market rent from the appraiser, or net operating income (NOI, revenue minus operating costs).
2. Whose income estimate: your pro forma (your own projected numbers), the appraiser's opinion, a third-party data report, or actual booking history.
3. Which payment: principal and interest only, or full PITIA.

Kiavi's guide says a ratio of at least 1.1 is needed to prequalify (get an early, conditional yes) and describes 1.25 or higher as strong. Your lender's minimum may be different, and pricing often changes with the ratio. Ask for the definition in writing before you compare any two quotes.

### PA.6.3 Cedar Ridge: one house, three ratios

Maya and Chris are buying Cedar Ridge for $385,000 with 25% down. All figures below are assumptions from the Cedar Ridge course example. The hypothetical DSCR quote is 7.25%, 1 point, 30-year fixed.

Start with the payment underneath. The loan is 75% of $385,000, or $288,750. Monthly principal and interest (P&I) is $1,970. Any mortgage calculator gives that from the loan amount, rate, and term. Property tax ($3,850 a year) and STR insurance ($3,200 a year) total $7,050 a year, or $587.50 a month. Cedar Ridge has no HOA dues. That makes monthly PITIA $2,557, or $30,684 a year (12 x $2,557).

Now put three different incomes on top.

**Ratio 1: gross booking revenue / PITIA.** Base-case gross revenue is $74,275. That's 212 booked nights (about 58% occupancy) at a $300 ADR (average daily rate, the average price per booked night). Those nights bring in $63,600. Add $10,675 of cleaning fees (61 stays at $175) and you get $74,275.

$74,275 / $30,684 = **2.42**

**Ratio 2: gross revenue discounted 20% / PITIA.** Some lenders haircut projected STR income. The 20% haircut here is an assumption, not any lender's published rule.

$74,275 x 0.80 = $59,420. Then $59,420 / $30,684 = **1.94**

**Ratio 3: NOI before property tax and insurance / PITIA.** Net operating income (NOI) is revenue after operating costs. Base-case operating costs are $45,066, so base-case NOI is $74,275 - $45,066 = $29,209. Those operating costs already include property tax and insurance. PITIA includes them too. So add them back to NOI, or they'd be subtracted twice: $29,209 + $3,850 + $3,200 = $36,259.

$36,259 / $30,684 = **1.18**

Same house, same loan, same year. The ratio runs from 1.18 to 2.42. The lender's written definition decides which one counts.

Now run the weak case. The course example's weak case drops ADR from $300 to $265 and occupancy from about 58% to 50%. Gross revenue falls to $57,330 and NOI to $18,162. Here are the three ratios on those inputs:

| Definition | Base case | Weak case |
|---|---:|---:|
| Gross revenue / PITIA | 2.42 | 1.87 |
| Gross revenue x 80% / PITIA | 1.94 | 1.49 |
| NOI before tax and insurance / PITIA | 1.18 | 0.82 |

All figures are assumptions from the Cedar Ridge example.

### PA.6.4 Why a lender ratio above 1.0 doesn't mean you make money

Look at the weak case on the gross definition: 1.87. That sounds comfortable. It says the house brings in almost twice its loan payment.

Here's what Maya and Chris actually keep in the weak case. Weak-case NOI is $18,162. Annual principal and interest on the DSCR loan is $23,637. Tax and insurance are already subtracted in NOI, so cash flow is NOI minus principal and interest:

$18,162 - $23,637 = **-$5,475 a year**, about -$456 a month.

A lender ratio of 1.87 and an owner losing $456 a month. Both are true at once.

Notice the NOI-based ratio doesn't have this problem. It's above 1.0 in the base case (1.18, with +$5,572 a year of cash flow). It's below 1.0 in the weak case (0.82, with -$5,475). It adds tax and insurance to both sides. So it's above 1.0 exactly when NOI covers principal and interest. The gross ratios are the ones that can look healthy while you lose money.

The reason is simple. A gross-revenue ratio ignores the platform fee, the cleaner, utilities, supplies, software, repairs, and the capital reserve. That's money you set aside for big replacements like a roof or HVAC. It isn't the same as the lender reserves in PA.6.7. An STR carries far more operating cost than a long-term rental where the tenant pays utilities. On Cedar Ridge, base-case operating costs are $45,066 on $74,275 of revenue. The gross ratio never sees that $45,066.

Even the base case is thin. Base-case cash flow is $29,209 - $23,637 = $5,572 a year, about $464 a month, before any reserves the lender requires you to hold.

So use the lender's ratio for one question only: will this lender make this loan at this size? Use your own NOI and cash flow for the other question: should you buy this house? PA.4, Reading a Full Underwriting Package, builds that NOI line by line. Bring its operating-cost total to every lender conversation.

### PA.6.5 How the definition changes the loan size

The definition does more than pass or fail you. It can shrink the loan.

Say a lender uses the NOI-based definition (Ratio 3) and requires a minimum of 1.20 (assumption). The most PITIA it will accept is $36,259 / 1.20 = $30,216 a year. Take out $7,050 of tax and insurance and the most principal and interest it allows is $23,166 a year. At 7.25% over 30 years, that supports a loan of about **$282,989**. Any mortgage calculator, or a spreadsheet's PV function, turns a yearly payment like this into a loan amount.

Maya and Chris asked for $288,750. This lender would cut the loan by about $5,761, and they'd bring that much more cash to closing.

Now take the 80% gross definition with a 1.25 minimum (assumption). The most PITIA it accepts is $59,420 / 1.25 = $47,536. Take out $7,050 and it allows $40,486 a year of principal and interest. On paper, that supports roughly $494,600 of loan, far more than the house is worth. Say that lender's maximum LTV is 75% (assumption). Then its cap is $288,750, and the LTV limit sets the loan size, not the ratio.

So ask each lender for the maximum loan its definition supports on your numbers, not just the rate.

This example used Maya and Chris's own income estimate. The next section shows the lender may use a lower one, which would shrink the loan further.

### PA.6.6 How a lender verifies STR income with no history

Cedar Ridge has no booking history under Maya and Chris. Neither will most houses you buy to convert. So where does the lender's income number come from?

Lenders use a few sources, and each lender picks its own:

- The appraiser's market rent. Kiavi says it requires an appraisal on its rental loans. Its rental loan page says the qualifying rent is the lower of 110% of appraised market rent or a valid lease rent ([Kiavi rental loans](https://www.kiavi.com/loans/rental)). That structure fits a long-term lease. Ask how the lender applies it to a house with nightly bookings and no lease.
- Short-term rental revenue. Kiavi publishes that it finances short-term rentals in some markets and that borrowers there can qualify on nightly rental revenue ([Kiavi Arizona DSCR programs](https://www.kiavi.com/arizona-dscr-loan-programs)). That page doesn't say which data source or haircut it applies. Ask.
- Third-party revenue estimates. Some lenders accept a report from an STR data provider. Ask which provider, which comparable set, and whether they discount it.
- Actual history. On an existing STR, a lender may ask for 12 or 24 months of platform statements or tax returns. The seller's history may not transfer to you, and it may not reflect your pricing or costs.

The lender's number will rarely match your pro forma. It shouldn't. The lender is protecting a loan, so it will usually lean lower on income and ignore upside. When the lender's income is lower than yours, don't argue the loan up. Ask whether your pro forma is the one that's too high.

**Confirm with:** each lender, in writing, which income source it uses for a no-history STR purchase, any haircut, and whether the appraiser must be STR-experienced.

### PA.6.7 The other terms that decide the real cost

The rate gets all the attention. Six other terms decide what the loan actually costs and how much room you have later.

**Down payment and LTV.** Kiavi's guide says DSCR loans typically need a larger down payment than a mortgage on a home you live in. It's often 20% to 25% ([Kiavi DSCR guide](https://www.kiavi.com/the-complete-guide-to-dscr-rental-property-loans)). The figure depends on property, lender, and borrower. Maximum LTV can drop for lower credit scores, lower ratios, or STR use. Cedar Ridge assumes 25%, which is $96,250.

**Points.** A point is an upfront fee equal to 1% of the loan, usually paid to lower the rate. The [CFPB explains points and lender credits](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/how-should-i-use-lender-credits-and-points-also-called-discount-points-en-136/): points cost more upfront and less over time; credits do the reverse. On Cedar Ridge, 1 point is $288,750 x 1% = $2,888. Closing costs rise from $11,550 (3% of price) to $14,438.

**Prepayment penalty.** Many DSCR loans charge a fee if you pay the loan off early, including through a refinance or sale. A common shape is a step-down. Take a 3-2-1 structure (an example, not a standard). The penalty is 3% of the balance in year one, 2% in year two, 1% in year three, then nothing. Kiavi publishes 3-, 5-, and 7-year step-down options for its rental loans ([Kiavi rental loans for investors](https://www.kiavi.com/rental-loans-for-investors)). Other lenders use flat percentages, months of interest, or no penalty at a higher rate.

This matters for Cedar Ridge. The course example assumes Maya and Chris refinance at month 24 to pull cash back out. OP.11, Scaling Your Portfolio: Refinancing, HELOCs & Property #2, covers how. The DSCR balance then is $282,951.

Suppose the loan carried a 3-2-1 schedule. This is a what-if; the Cedar Ridge quote doesn't state a penalty. A payoff in month 24 is still in year two:

$282,951 x 2% = **$5,659 penalty** (assumption: 3-2-1 schedule)

The planned refinance shows $15,725 of cash out. That's what's left after the new, larger loan pays off the old one and the refinance costs. Subtract the penalty and it drops to $10,066. Wait one more month, into year three, and the penalty falls to 1%. State law and loan type can limit prepayment penalties. Ask the lender how its penalty applies in your state.

**Reserves.** Some lenders require cash left in the bank after closing, usually measured in months of PITIA. Kiavi's DSCR page describes no cash reserve requirement ([Kiavi DSCR loans](https://www.kiavi.com/loans/dscr)). Confirm that with the lender, since terms change. Other lenders do require reserves, so ask. Six months of PITIA on Cedar Ridge (assumption) is 6 x $2,557 = $15,342. Lesson 0.3, Is Ownership Right for You? A Capital & Readiness Checklist, covered why that cash is on top of your cash to close.

**Entity and guarantor.** Many DSCR lenders let you close in an LLC. Kiavi's site says LLC borrowing is available for its DSCR rental loans, with requirements that vary ([Kiavi DSCR loans](https://www.kiavi.com/loans/dscr)). A lender may also ask the owners to sign a personal guarantee, which makes you personally liable if the LLC defaults. Ask who must guarantee, and whether title must be in the LLC before closing. PA.10, Entity & Tax Structure Setup, covers the entity decision itself.

**Permit and property eligibility.** A lender may refuse STR income where short-term rental use isn't allowed. It may also ask for proof of the permit. Some lenders exclude condos with rental restrictions, rural acreage, or unusual property types. Bring your STR compliance checklist from SR.1 (Regulatory compliance checklist, by state and city) to the first call.

### PA.6.8 Maya and Chris's liquidity after the DSCR loan

Put these terms against the buyers' actual cash. Maya and Chris have $210,000 in liquid savings (course example).

| Line | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Down payment (25%) | $96,250 |
| Closing costs incl. 1 point | $14,438 |
| Cash to close | $110,688 |
| Furnishing budget | $32,000 |
| Total cash in | $142,688 |
| Lender reserves, 6 months PITIA (assumption) | $15,342 |
| Savings left for everything else | $51,970 |

$210,000 - $142,688 - $15,342 = $51,970. The $15,342 of reserves stays in their account. But the lender expects it untouched, so it isn't spare cash. The remaining $51,970 still has to cover their personal emergency fund and a weak first season. The weak case loses $5,475 a year. PA.8, Offer Strategy & Negotiation for Investment Property, turns this table into their offer ceiling.

### PA.6.9 The questions you send to two lenders

Send the same written questions to at least two lenders, on the same house, on the same day if you can. Rates change daily, so quotes from different days can differ for reasons that have nothing to do with the lender.

1. What is your exact DSCR formula? Which income goes on top, and which payment goes underneath?
2. For a short-term rental with no history, which income source do you use? Do you discount it? By how much?
3. What minimum ratio do you require for this loan, and does the rate or maximum LTV change with the ratio?
4. What is the maximum loan you'll offer on this address with my numbers?
5. What credit score tier am I in, and how does it change the rate or LTV?
6. What are the rate, points, and all lender fees? Please send a written quote or Loan Estimate.
7. What prepayment penalty applies? Show the schedule by year.
8. How many months of reserves do you require after closing, and which accounts count?
9. Can I close in an LLC? Who must sign a personal guarantee?
10. What proof of legal STR use or a permit do you need, and when?
11. Does your appraiser need STR experience? Who orders the market-rent or revenue report?
12. How long is the rate lock, and what does an extension cost?

Three terms in that list need a quick definition. Your credit tier is the score band the lender prices you in. A Loan Estimate is the standard cost form lenders give on consumer mortgages; PA.6.10 explains why a DSCR lender may not use it. A rate lock holds your quoted rate for a set number of days while you close.

### PA.6.10 Comparing two written offers

Put the two written answers side by side. The illustration below shows the format. Lender A is the course example's DSCR quote. Lender B is invented for this exercise.

| Term | Lender A (course example) | Lender B (assumption) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Max loan on Cedar Ridge | $288,750 (75% LTV cap, assumption) | about $276,094 |
| Points | 1.0 | 0.0 |
| Prepayment | ask | 3-2-1 |
| Reserves | ask | 6 months PITIA |
| Guarantee required | ask | yes |
| Rate | 7.25% | 7.50% |
| DSCR definition | Gross / PITIA (assumption) | NOI-based / PITIA |
| Minimum ratio | ask | 1.20 |

All Lender B figures are assumptions. So are Lender A's definition and LTV cap. Lender A's loan is the amount Maya and Chris asked for, capped by LTV, not by the ratio. Lender B's maximum loan uses the NOI method from PA.6.5. It allows at most about $23,166 a year of principal and interest. At 7.50% over 30 years, that works out to about $276,094.

The 3-2-1 penalty in PA.6.7 was a what-if. Lender A's real prepayment schedule is still unknown, so its cell says "ask."

Read this table from the top down. First, does the lender make the loan you need at the size you need? Lender B's loan is about $12,656 short, so Maya and Chris would need that much more cash. Second, what's the full upfront cost, including points and fees? Third, what does it cost you to leave early? Rate comes after those. A lower rate with a longer penalty may cost more than a higher rate if you plan the year-2 refinance in OP.11. Kiavi, for example, publishes 5- and 7-year options.

The [CFPB's guide to comparing loan offers](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/compare/compare-loan-estimates/) tells borrowers to request several Loan Estimates and focus on the fees that vary by lender. One catch for DSCR loans. Federal rules treat credit to buy a rental you won't live in as business-purpose credit ([Regulation Z commentary to § 1026.3](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/interp-3/)). So a DSCR lender may give you a term sheet instead of a Loan Estimate. Ask for every fee in writing either way.

### PA.6.12 Who confirms what

**Confirm with:** each lender, the DSCR formula, income source for a no-history STR, minimum ratio, maximum loan, points, fees, reserves, and prepayment schedule, all in writing and dated.

**Confirm with:** the lender and your attorney, whether a prepayment penalty is enforceable in your state and how it applies to a refinance or sale.

**Confirm with:** your attorney, what a personal guarantee commits you to and whether title must sit in the LLC at closing.

**Confirm with:** the local licensing office, that STR use and any permit the lender asks for are available at this exact address (use your SR.1 checklist).

**Confirm with:** your CPA, how points and loan costs are treated for tax purposes on a rental.
