Permission attaches to one address, use, and person

Slide 1 · Permission attaches to one address, use, and person

Before any numbers matter, you need permission to run a short-term rental at one exact address. It's an easy check to skip, and it can end a deal outright.

Two terms. A short-term rental, or STR, is a furnished stay of less than about a month, the kind booked on Airbnb or Vrbo. Rental arbitrage means you lease a unit, you don't live in it, and you rent it to short-stay guests. This lesson asks whether that model is allowed at one address.

By the end you'll have a permission ladder for one address with a result: permitted, prohibited, or unresolved. We'll test a hypothetical unit, Alder Street, against two real rulebooks, New York City and Los Angeles. Rules change often, so check the official pages on the day you research.

Slide 2 · Every layer must say yes, not just the city

Students often ask whether a city is Airbnb-friendly. That label doesn't help you. A city can allow short stays in general while something else blocks your unit.

Picture a ladder. Each rung is a separate question, and one no anywhere ends the deal. Work from the widest authority down. The state may set rules everywhere. Zoning decides what a parcel may be used for. Many cities run a permit program on top of zoning, with categories, caps, and fees. Building and fire codes set occupancy and alarm rules. An HOA, condo, or co-op can ban short rentals in its recorded rules even where the city allows them.

And for arbitrage, the lease is the layer you control least. Lessons 3.5 and 5.3 cover the lease documents.

Slide 3 · Arbitrage is unhosted, and many cities ban unhosted stays

A hosted stay means the host lives in the unit and is there during the stay. Usually the guest rents a room. An unhosted stay means guests have the whole place and nobody lives there.

Cities treat these very differently. A hosted stay looks like a resident earning a bit from a spare room. An unhosted stay looks like a hotel room carved out of the housing supply.

Arbitrage is almost always unhosted. So here's a habit I want you to build. When you open an ordinance, don't read every word from the top. Search for the clause that decides whether the host must be present. That one clause often answers the question before you read anything else.

A primary-residence rule is usually a hard stop for arbitrage

  • You host only where you actually live
  • Usually over half the year
  • Some cities allow one hosting property only
  • Signing a lease doesn't make it home
Slide 4 · A primary-residence rule is usually a hard stop for arbitrage

A primary-residence rule says you may only host in the home where you actually live. Cities usually define that by time, like more than half the year. Los Angeles uses more than six months. Some add that it has to be the only property where you host.

For an arbitrage operator, this is usually a hard stop. You can't make a unit your primary residence by signing a lease on it while you live across town. Cities that use this rule often ask for proof, like a driver's license, voter registration, or tax records at that address.

So look for this test right after the hosted clause. If it's there and you don't live in the unit, the plan fails at this rung.

Slide 5 · Night caps hit arbitrage hardest because rent is due all year

Caps come in three forms. A night cap limits how many nights a year you may host. Some cities cap only unhosted nights. A permit cap limits how many permits the city hands out, and new applicants may land on a waitlist. A unit cap limits how many permits one person or one building can hold.

The night cap hurts arbitrage the most. Your rent is due twelve months a year. If the city lets you host only part of the year, the math has to work on those nights alone. In the worked example you'll see how fast that fails.

Many cities call under 30 nights short-term

  • Count the way your city counts
  • NYC: 29 nights is short-term, 30 isn't
  • Longer stays can bring tenant protections
  • Have an attorney review month-plus rentals
Slide 6 · Many cities call under 30 nights short-term

Many cities define a short-term rental by stay length, often under 30 nights. At or above that line, the stay is often treated as an ordinary residential tenancy, with different rules.

Check how your city counts. New York City treats the check-in date as day zero, so 29 nights is short-term and 30 is not. Your city may count differently.

Now, some of you are thinking: just take 30-night bookings and skip the rules. That isn't a loophole. Past the threshold, tenant protections can apply, depending on the state and city. That can mean eviction procedures and rent rules. Treat month-plus furnished rentals as a separate business, and have an attorney review it first.

Never assume a permit transfers, so ask who holds it

  • You take over an existing permit
  • The landlord sells the building
  • You add a partner or an LLC
Slide 7 · Never assume a permit transfers, so ask who holds it

Ask who the permit belongs to. Some cities issue it to the owner, some to the host, and some tie it to both.

That matters in three situations. A landlord says the unit already has a permit. If it belongs to the previous host, it may not cover you. The landlord sells the building. A permit issued to the owner may end with the sale. You bring in a partner or form an LLC. Some programs require the host to be a natural person, meaning an individual, so an LLC may not qualify.

Ask the licensing office in writing, and keep the reply in your deal folder.

Slide 9 · In New York City, Alder Street fails five rules

New York City runs Local Law 18 registration through its Office of Special Enforcement. Hosts must register, or the rental is illegal. The host has to be a natural person who lives there and stays in the unit with guests. No more than two paying guests, and no entire apartment for less than 30 days.

Alder Street's operator lives elsewhere. Fails. It's unhosted. Fails. It sleeps five. Fails. Three-night stays of the whole unit. Fails. So the operator can't register, and there's no number for the platform to verify. Building owners can also put a building on a Prohibited Buildings List, but at this point it doesn't matter.

Result: prohibited. The entire-unit rule alone ends it.

Slide 10 · In Los Angeles, the primary-residence rule alone ends it

Los Angeles runs a Home-Sharing Ordinance. Home-sharing is limited to your primary residence, where you live more than six months a year, and it must be your only home-share. The standard limit is 120 days a year unless you qualify for Extended Home-Sharing. Units under the Rent Stabilization Ordinance, or RSO, are excluded. That covers most multifamily units built before October 1, 1978. Renters need the landlord's notarized approval, and hosts register for the city's hotel tax.

Alder Street fails primary residence, fails the only-property rule, and 255 nights blows past 120. You check RSO status in the city's ZIMAS tool.

Result: prohibited. Same unit, same lease, two different no answers for two different reasons.

Slide 11 · At 120 nights, Alder Street loses $10,445 a year

Suppose someone tried to fix the Los Angeles problem. Move in, make it your primary residence, and host within the 120-day cap. I'll treat that as 120 booked nights and keep every other assumption the same.

One hundred twenty nights at $205, plus cleaning fees on 40 stays, is $29,000 of bookings. Airbnb's fee takes $4,495. Cleaning takes $4,360. Then the fixed costs arrive in full, $30,590, with $23,400 of that being rent.

Net is minus $10,445 a year, about minus $870 a month. At 255 nights it was plus $12,218. To break even, the nightly rate would need about $308. If you really live there, part of that rent is your own housing, and that's a different plan. As an arbitrage business, a 120-night cap doesn't work at this rent.

Slide 12 · Stop at the first no on every address

Here's the flow I want you to follow for every address.

Write down the exact plan: address, parcel number, hosted or unhosted, guests, stay length, nights a year. Use the city or county parcel lookup, because a mailing address can name a different city than the one whose rules apply.

Find the official ordinance and the program's own page. A blog or a Facebook group is not a source. If the ordinance bans your model, stop.

Then check zoning, building type, the HOA, the lease, and the permit mechanics. Each can stop you.

If anything is unclear, ask the licensing office a yes-or-no question in writing. Save dated copies of every page and reply. Sam runs this flow live in 3.2.

Slide 13 · One no means prohibited, and one unclear means unresolved

This is the exercise, the permission ladder. Fill it in for one real address, or a listing you'd realistically consider. The full template is in the lesson and in SR.1, the regulatory compliance checklist.

Write the plan facts first. Then answer 15 rungs, each written so Yes is the good answer. For an arbitrage plan, do the hosted, primary-residence, and night-cap rungs first. Any one of them can end it quickly.

Every answer needs an official source and the date you checked. The licensing office should confirm in writing that your plan fits a permit category, and an attorney should review the lease.

The rule is strict on purpose. One no means prohibited. One unclear means unresolved, and you don't sign a lease.
