### 0.2.0 Learning outcome

By the end of this lesson you can split an ownership return into four streams: cash flow, principal paydown, change in value, and tax timing. You can say which of the four you can actually spend this year. You can compare owning a house with leasing a similar one on the same assumptions. And you can run a simple test that tells you whether "ownership wins" is true for a specific deal, or only true on a slide.

### 0.2.1 Treat "wins" as a claim you have to test

The title of this lesson makes a claim: ownership wins. Sometimes it does. Sometimes it loses badly. The only honest way to use that title is as a question you answer for one property, one loan, and one household.

The case for owning usually lists three benefits: cash flow, tax savings, and equity. I'm going to split equity into two parts, because they behave differently. That gives four possible return streams:

1. Operating cash flow: what's left after operating costs and the loan payment.
2. Principal paydown: the part of each loan payment that reduces what you owe.
3. Change in value: what the house is worth later compared with what you paid.
4. Tax timing: deductions, mainly depreciation, that may lower your tax bill now and may be partly taken back when you sell.

Each stream has a cost or a risk attached. And only one of them lands in your bank account on a normal month. The mistake I want you to avoid is adding all four together and calling the total "income". It is not income. Three of the four are either locked inside the house or depend on facts that won't be known for years.

### 0.2.2 Stream one: operating cash flow

This is the only stream you can spend this year. It is the money left after the house pays for itself.

For Cedar Ridge, the canonical base case works like this. All figures are assumptions from the course example file.

- Gross booking revenue: 212 booked nights at $300, plus 61 cleaning fees of $175, for $74,275.
- Operating costs, including platform fees, cleaner, utilities, repairs reserve, capital reserve, property tax, and insurance: $45,066.
- Net operating income (NOI): $74,275 minus $45,066, which is $29,209.
- Annual principal and interest on the DSCR quote: $23,637.
- Cash flow: $29,209 minus $23,637, which is $5,572 a year, or about $464 a month.

That $5,572 is real money, if the base case happens. Notice what it already pays for: a 5% repairs reserve and a 5% capital reserve. If you drop those reserves to make the number look bigger, you are borrowing from the next roof.

Now the cost side. Against $142,688 of cash in (down payment, closing costs, and furnishing), $5,572 is a 3.9% cash-on-cash return. And in the weak case, with ADR at $265 and occupancy at 50%, the same house loses $5,475 a year after debt service. So stream one is small in a good year and negative in a bad one.

### 0.2.3 Stream two: principal paydown

Each month, part of the loan payment reduces the loan balance. That part is not an expense in the economic sense. It turns into equity.

On the Cedar Ridge DSCR quote, the balance falls by $2,795 in year one. That number grows a little each year because more of each payment goes to principal as the balance shrinks.

Here's the catch. You paid that $2,795 out of your cash flow already. It's inside the $23,637 of debt service. You can't spend it. You get it back only when you sell or refinance. The canonical refinance model at the end of year two shows a cash-out of $15,725 after costs. That is how locked equity becomes cash, and it comes with a new, larger loan payment. The same model shows base-case cash flow falling from $5,572 to $4,752 after the refinance.

Principal paydown also depends on the house holding its value. If the value falls, you can pay down the loan and still have less equity than you started with.

### 0.2.4 Stream three: change in value

This is the stream people talk about most and control least.

For teaching, the canonical example assumes Cedar Ridge's value grows 3% a year (assumption). In year one that would be 3% of $385,000, or $11,550. It is an assumption, not a forecast. Values can fall. Lake markets can see new supply, rule changes, or a slow year.

Three rules for this stream:

- Never add modeled appreciation to spendable monthly income. $11,550 of assumed value change is not about $960 a month you can use.
- You only collect it by selling or borrowing against it, and both cost money.
- Selling costs are large. The canonical model uses 7% of the sale price. On an assumed $446,321 value after five years, that is $31,242.

Here is a test worth running on any deal. What happens if the value is flat for five years? For Cedar Ridge, that means selling at $385,000. Subtract 7% selling costs of $26,950 and the year-five loan balance of $272,519. Net proceeds before tax are $85,531. You put in $142,688. The shortfall is only covered if cash flow and tax timing make up the difference. Section 0.2.8 runs this.

### 0.2.5 Stream four: tax timing

Depreciation is a paper deduction for wear on the building. You don't write a check for it, but it can reduce taxable income. Whether it reduces your tax bill depends on several facts. Each one is a question for your CPA, not a number to assume.

**Basis and land.** You depreciate the building, not the land. IRS Publication 527 states that you can never depreciate land ([IRS Publication 527](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p527)). So the first input is how much of the price belongs to the land. The canonical example assumes 20% (assumption). On a basis of $391,000 (price plus $6,000 of capitalized closing costs, assumption), that leaves $312,800 for the building.

**Placed in service.** Depreciation starts when the property is ready and available for rent, not when the first guest arrives. Publication 527 gives an example: a house ready for rent in July and first rented in September starts depreciating in July ([IRS Publication 527](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p527)). For Cedar Ridge, that means the month the house is furnished, safe, and listed matters. A late-year launch gives a small first-year deduction on the building.

**Recovery period and classification.** The canonical file shows the building on two schedules. At 27.5 years it's $11,375 per full year; at 39 years, $8,021. Short average stays can change how the property is classified. Your CPA decides which applies to your facts.

**Accelerated depreciation.** A cost segregation study can move some of the building into 5, 7, and 15-year property. Furnishings are also short-life property. The IRS has issued guidance on a permanent 100% first-year (bonus) depreciation deduction for eligible property acquired after January 19, 2025 ([IRS newsroom, Notice 2026-11 guidance](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/treasury-irs-issue-guidance-on-the-additional-first-year-depreciation-deduction-amended-as-part-of-the-one-big-beautiful-bill)). It applies to eligible property, not to land and not to the whole building. Sam's PA.5 walks through the study itself.

In the canonical illustration, a 22% reclassification (assumption) puts $68,816 of the building into short-life property. Add the $32,000 of furnishings, and the year-one bonus amount is $100,816, if eligible. Add a full year of 39-year depreciation on the remaining building ($6,256) and year-one deductions are about $107,072. At an assumed 32% marginal rate, that could lower tax by about $34,263.

Read the word "could" carefully. That result depends on the next three facts.

**Passive activity rules.** Losses from passive activities generally offset only passive income. IRS Publication 925 explains the rules ([IRS Publication 925](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p925)). Two parts of it matter most for short-term rentals:

- An activity is not a "rental activity" under these rules if the average period of customer use is 7 days or less. Publication 925 says you figure the average by dividing total days in all rental periods by the number of rentals. Cedar Ridge's canonical average stay is 3.5 nights: 212 nights over 61 stays.
- That exception does not make the losses non-passive by itself. It means the activity is tested like a business. The losses are non-passive only if you materially participate.

Publication 925 lists the material participation tests. Three of them matter most here. You participated more than 500 hours. Or your participation was substantially all the participation by anyone. Or you participated more than 100 hours and at least as much as any other individual, including people who don't own an interest ([IRS Publication 925](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p925)). That last point matters for an STR. A cleaner who turns the house over 61 times a year may log more hours than you do. If you hire a full-service co-host, their hours may exceed yours by a wide margin.

**The $25,000 allowance does not rescue a high-income household.** Publication 925 also describes a special allowance of up to $25,000. It applies to rental real estate with active participation. It is reduced by 50% of modified adjusted gross income above $100,000 and is gone at $150,000 ([IRS Publication 925](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p925)). Maya and Chris have $185,000 of combined W-2 income. If their modified AGI is near that figure, the allowance would give them nothing, even if it applied to this activity at all.

**Personal use.** Maya and Chris plan about 10 personal days a year. Publication 527 has a day test. A dwelling is used as a home if personal use exceeds the greater of 14 days or 10% of fair-rental days. It also requires dividing expenses between rental and personal use by days ([IRS Publication 527](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p527)). At 212 rented nights, 10% is 21.2 days, so the threshold is 21.2 days. Ten days is below it. More personal use would change the picture.

**It's timing, not a gift.** Depreciation lowers your basis. When you sell, some of the deduction can come back as taxable gain. That's why this stream is called "tax timing". Lesson OP.12 treats the tax on sale as a CPA input.

**Confirm with:** a CPA whether the activity is a rental activity for your household and whether your actual hours meet a participation test. Also confirm which recovery periods apply, and whether any loss is usable this year or suspended.

### 0.2.6 Owning compared with leasing a similar house

Part One of this course is about leasing. So a fair question is: why not lease a house like Cedar Ridge instead of buying it?

To compare fairly, keep the operating assumptions identical. Same revenue, same cleaner, same utilities. Change only what differs between owning and leasing. The lease figures below are a hypothetical arbitrage-style lease on a comparable house. **All figures in this table are assumptions.** Rent is $2,900 a month on a 12-month lease with written STR permission. The tenant carries minor repairs of $1,200 a year and a renter's STR liability policy of $1,800 a year. The landlord pays property tax, building insurance, and capital repairs.

| Line (annual) | Own Cedar Ridge (DSCR quote) | Lease a comparable house (assumption) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Gross booking revenue (base) | $74,275 | $74,275 |
| Platform fee, cleaner, utilities, supplies, software, handyman | -$30,588 | -$30,588 |
| Repairs and capital reserve | -$7,428 | -$1,200 |
| Property tax and building insurance / renter's liability policy | -$7,050 | -$1,800 |
| Principal and interest / rent | -$23,637 | -$34,800 |
| **Base-case cash flow** | **$5,572** | **$5,887** |
| Weak-case cash flow (ADR $265, 50%) | -$5,475 | -$6,856 |
| Base case with 18% co-host | -$7,798 | -$7,483 |
| Cash in (see below) | $142,688 | $44,500 |
| Cash-on-cash, base | 3.9% | 13.2% |
| Principal paydown, year 1 | $2,795 | none |
| Exposure to value change | Yes, both directions | None |
| Possible depreciation | Building, improvements, furnishings | Furnishings and your own improvements only |
| Exit | Sale (7% costs assumed) or refinance | Lease ends, or early-exit terms |

How the lease cash in adds up: $32,000 of furnishing, a $2,900 deposit, and $2,900 of first month's rent. Add a two-month operating reserve of $6,700 (rent plus $450 of utilities, times two). Total: $44,500.

Read the comparison slowly.

- Base-case cash flow is about the same. Leasing is $315 a year higher.
- Owning needs $98,188 more cash ($142,688 minus $44,500). That cash is the cost of streams two, three, and four.
- In the weak case, the lease loses more, because rent is fixed and higher than the owner's loan payment. The owner, though, also carries capital repairs that the reserve may not fully cover in a bad year.
- The owner builds equity and may get tax timing. The tenant gets neither.
- The tenant's exposure ends with the lease. The owner's continues until a sale.

This is also why the lease path is not a fantasy. For some households, $44,500 in a lease beats $142,688 in a house. For others, the long hold and the equity matter more than the cash-on-cash rate.

One practical caveat. In many drive-to lake markets, owners of three-bedroom houses may not lease them for short-term rental at all. The lease column is a teaching comparison. Whether a real lease exists is a market question for Sam's PA.2 and the Part One research lessons.

### 0.2.7 Worked example: stacking the four streams without cheating

Here is the right way to present Cedar Ridge's year one on the DSCR quote. Keep the streams in separate rows, and label each by whether you can spend it.

| Stream | Year 1 amount | Can you spend it this year? | What it depends on |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Operating cash flow | $5,572 | Yes | Base case revenue; weak case is -$5,475 |
| Principal paydown | $2,795 | No, locked until sale or refinance | Paying the loan every month |
| Change in value (3%, assumption) | $11,550 | No, and it may be negative | Market; only realized by selling or borrowing |
| Tax timing (illustrative, if usable) | up to about $34,263 | Only if the loss is usable against other income this year | Basis, land split, placed-in-service date, classification, passive rules, material participation, personal use, household tax rate; partly recaptured on sale |

Now look at return on cash in, one layer at a time:

- Cash flow alone: $5,572 / $142,688 = 3.9%.
- Cash flow plus principal paydown: $8,367 / $142,688 = 5.9%.
- Adding assumed appreciation: $19,917 / $142,688 = 14.0%.

Those three percentages describe three very different things. Only the first one pays a bill. The third one includes an assumption you can't check until you sell. When someone shows you a single "total return" number, ask for this table instead.

I left tax timing out of those percentages on purpose. It depends on your CPA's answers, and it can be zero in year one if the loss is suspended.

### 0.2.8 One case where ownership works, one where it doesn't

Now a five-year view, using the canonical sale model. Assumptions: base-case cash flow stays flat for five years (assumption), and the original DSCR loan stays in place. The house sells at the end of year five with 7% selling costs. Every figure is before income tax, and before any tax on the sale or depreciation recapture.

**Case A: ownership works (self-managed, base case, 3% value growth).**

- Five years of cash flow: 5 x $5,572 = $27,860.
- Net sale proceeds before tax: $142,560 (value $446,321, less loan balance $272,519, less selling costs $31,242).
- Total back: $27,860 + $142,560 = $170,420.
- Less cash in: $170,420 - $142,688 = $27,732 ahead, before tax.

Plus whatever tax timing your CPA confirms, less whatever tax is due on the sale. It is a modest result for five years and $142,688. But it is positive, it survives a year or two of weak revenue from reserves, and Maya and Chris keep control.

**Case B: ownership does not work (full-service co-host at 18%).**

Suppose Maya and Chris decide they don't want to run the house. They hire a full-service local co-host at 18% of booking revenue. The co-host costs $13,370 a year in the base case.

- Annual cash flow on the DSCR quote: -$7,798.
- Five years: 5 x -$7,798 = -$38,990.
- Total back: -$38,990 + $142,560 = $103,570.
- Less cash in: $103,570 - $142,688 = $39,118 behind, before tax.

In this case they pay about $650 a month to own the house. The only path to breaking even is appreciation above the 3% assumption or tax timing, and neither is spendable while you're writing checks every month. If revenue is also weak, the co-host case loses $15,794 a year: weak NOI of $18,162, less the co-host's $10,319, less $23,637 of debt service.

Two more versions of the same test:

- Weak case, self-managed, 3% value growth: 5 x -$5,475 = -$27,375 of cash flow. Add $142,560 of proceeds and subtract $142,688 of cash in. Result: $27,503 behind, before tax.
- Base case, flat value: $27,860 of cash flow plus $85,531 of proceeds, less $142,688. Result: $29,297 behind, before tax.

So Cedar Ridge "wins" only on specific facts. Revenue hits the base case or better. The owners manage with local help. The house holds or gains value. Change any one of those and it loses over five years before tax. This is what "test it" means.

1. The Part B base case is ahead.

### 0.2.10 Who confirms what

**Confirm with:** your CPA the land allocation, placed-in-service date, recovery period, and bonus depreciation eligibility. Also confirm the passive-rule treatment, your participation hours, personal-use days, and the tax on a future sale.

**Confirm with:** your lender the loan balance schedule (amortization) and the actual payment, so principal paydown uses real numbers.

**Confirm with:** a local agent or appraiser the value assumptions you use for the five-year test. Treat any growth rate as your assumption, not theirs.

**Confirm with:** a cost segregation provider and your CPA whether a study makes sense for the property, before counting accelerated depreciation (Sam's PA.5, SR.3).
