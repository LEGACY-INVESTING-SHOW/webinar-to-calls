Slide 1 · Write each routine so a backup can run it from the driveway without calling you

After this lesson you'll have a short operating playbook for your house. A backup cleaner or a new co-host can follow it without calling you.

It has three pieces. The first is a turnover checklist. A turnover is the clean between one guest's checkout and the next check-in. The checklist comes with photo proof, supply levels, and a plan for a missed clean. The second is a message sequence, with a named person checking every message. The third is an emergency matrix for seven kinds of incident.

Then we'll run all of it at Cedar Ridge three times. There's a normal Sunday turnover, an internet outage on a Tuesday, and a CO alarm at 1:40 a.m.

Cedar Ridge is the course's hypothetical lake house. It sleeps eight in six beds, and Maya and Chris own it from about two hours away. The people come from OP.7. The cleaner has calendar access. The handyman takes the after-hours calls. Maya owns guest messaging and supplies. Chris handles approvals and bookkeeping, backs Maya up, and makes the owner calls in an emergency. If you haven't built your role map yet, do that first.

Slide 2 · A usable SOP has seven parts: trigger, owner, steps, standard, proof, escalation, record

A standard operating procedure, or SOP, is a written routine that gives the same result no matter who follows it. Most short-term rental checklists fail that test. They say "clean kitchen" and leave the standard to the cleaner's mood that day.

Every SOP in this lesson has these seven parts. The trigger is what starts it. The SOP owner is one named role, taken from your OP.7 role map. One role, not "the team." I say "SOP owner" on purpose, because Maya and Chris own the house, and that's a different thing.

The standard says what done looks like, specifically enough that two people would agree. The proof is a photo, a timestamp, or a reading. Escalation says who to call, and by when, if a step fails. The record says where the result gets written down.

Keep each one to a page. The person using it is standing in a driveway with a phone, often on the day they're covering for someone else.

The turnover runs more than any other SOP. At Cedar Ridge it runs about 61 times a year, once for each base-case stay. Airbnb's ground rules for hosts set cleanliness as an expectation. Slide 6 · The missed-clean ladder catches a no-show by 11 a.m., not at 3:30 p.m.

The most visible everyday failure is a guest arriving to a dirty house. Often it starts with a cleaner who doesn't show up and a host who finds out at 3:30 in the afternoon.

The ladder catches it by late morning. The night before, the cleaner confirms in the task app by 8 p.m. If not, Maya calls. If there's still no confirmation by 9, Maya asks the backup cleaner to hold the slot.

On the day, 11 a.m. is the deadline for the arrival photo, not the start time. If it isn't in, Maya calls the cleaner. No answer in 15 minutes, and the backup goes. A backup sent at 11:15 may not finish by 4. So the ladder's real job is to warn the guest early and offer a later check-in.

At 3:45, Maya reviews the final photos. If anything is missing, she messages the guest a delay before 4.

The backup gets a same-day premium, $200 instead of $150. Four times a year, that's $200 more. It's cheap next to a refund.

Slide 8 · No bot answers a message about smoke, gas, leaks, injury, lockouts, or refunds

Automation needs limits. Write these into the SOP and into the settings of any AI messaging tool you use.

The first limit is the important one. No automated or AI reply goes to a message about smoke, fire, gas, alarms, leaks, injury, police, lockouts, or refunds. A person answers those, immediately. Airbnb's scheduled replies fire on events like a booking or checkout. So this limit matters most for AI-suggested replies and any outside tool that answers what a guest writes.

Second, a person replies to any guest message within 30 minutes, even if an automatic acknowledgment went first. The 30 minutes is my assumption.

Third, if a door code changes after the check-in message went out, say after a lockout, a person checks the lock and sends it.

Fourth, an AI suggestion is a draft. A person approves it, unless it's an approved template. Fifth, any exception you grant goes in the booking notes, so the cleaner sees it.

Slide 10 · Anyone can stop work for safety, but only an owner reopens the calendar

Stop-work authority means the right to halt a turnover, a repair, or the next booking because something isn't safe.

At Cedar Ridge, anyone on the team can stop work for safety. The cleaner can. The handyman can. Nobody needs permission to say "this isn't safe, we stop."

Reopening works the other way. Only an owner, Maya or Chris, can reopen the calendar, and only after the right professional signs off. After a CO alarm, the fire department clears the scene first. Then the HVAC technician and the gas supplier clear the house in writing. Stopping is easy on purpose. Restarting is hard on purpose.

The contact sheet is the other half. It's laminated and kept inside the owner closet door, with a copy in the house manual. It lists 911 and the non-emergency police and fire numbers. It lists the utilities and where the water shutoff is. It lists the on-call phone, the handyman, the plumber, the HVAC company, and the insurer's claim line.

Fill in the real local numbers before the first booking. A blank line on that sheet at 2 a.m. costs time.

Airbnb bans indoor cameras and recording devices, even if off. Exterior cameras must be disclosed. Noise monitors that don't record are allowed in disclosed common spaces only.

  • Turnover: no new device has appeared indoors
  • Turnover: the noise monitor is still in its disclosed common-area spot
  • Turnover: the doorbell camera still points only at the entry
  • Messaging: camera footage and guest details stay in the platform or the device's own app
  • Messaging: nobody downloads them to a personal phone or shares them outside the team
Slide 11 · Privacy rules go in the SOPs, because well-meaning people often break them

Privacy rules belong in the SOPs, because the people who break them often mean well. A cleaner might want to set up a "nanny cam" to watch for parties. That's not allowed.

Airbnb's device rules prohibit security cameras and recording devices that monitor indoor spaces, even if they're off. Exterior cameras have to be disclosed in the listing. They can't watch indoor spaces or areas like an enclosed outdoor shower. Noise decibel monitors that don't record sound are allowed in common spaces if they're disclosed. They're not allowed in bedrooms, bathrooms, or sleeping areas.

Cedar Ridge has a doorbell camera and a noise monitor, from OP.6. So step 10 of the turnover SOP carries three device checks. The cleaner checks them every turnover, the same way they check the alarms. Two more lines go in the messaging SOP.

Those messaging lines matter because footage and guest details are easy to copy. They stay in the platform or the device's own app.

Slide 16 · Four checks tell you whether your SOPs are finished or still drafts

These four checks come from the lesson's decision rules. Run them before your first booking, and again after any incident.

First, every step needs a named owner. If a step says "someone checks the alarms," it isn't finished. Put the role there.
