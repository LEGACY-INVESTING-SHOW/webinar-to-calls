A study can pull deductions into year one

Slide 1 · A study can pull deductions into year one

This workbook estimates how much depreciation a cost-segregation study might move into year one, and what that could mean for your tax. I built it for the conversation with your CPA. It doesn't replace that conversation.

Whether any of this applies depends on the property, the dates, and how you use it. It also depends on your participation, your income, and the tax year. The workbook makes those inputs visible. Your CPA decides them.

Our example is Cedar Ridge, the hypothetical lake house Maya and Chris bought for $385,000. Use this after Sam's PA.5 lesson. No property yet? Build it anyway with the Cedar Ridge inputs.

A study splits off short-life parts you can deduct in year one.

Slide 2 · A study splits the building into short-life parts

When you buy a rental, you can't deduct the building all at once. You deduct it a slice at a time over 27.5 or 39 years. That paper write-off for wear on the building is depreciation. Land is never depreciated.

A cost-segregation study splits the building into parts. Some, like land improvements, qualify for 5-, 7-, or 15-year lives. Bonus depreciation is an extra first-year deduction. When it applies, those parts plus your furniture can be deducted in full in year one.

For Cedar Ridge, a study adds about $67,000 of year-one deduction. At a 32% rate, that's about $21,000 before a $4,500 study fee. That holds only if the loss is usable this year.

Slide 3 · Cedar Ridge's base case deducts $107,072 in year one

Here's the base case by hand. Basis is the $385,000 price plus $6,000 of closing costs, so $391,000. Land at 20% comes off and never depreciates. That leaves a $312,800 building.

The study moves 22% of it into short lives, $68,816. Add the $32,000 of furniture and, with 100% bonus, $100,816 comes off in year one. The rest of the building, $243,984, runs over 39 years. That's $6,256.

Total: $107,072. At 32%, that's a $34,263 tax effect, if all of it is usable this year. I've used a full first year to keep it simple. With a June start, the building line is closer to $3,389.

Slide 4 · Five tabs, and you only type in Inputs

Build it as a spreadsheet with five tabs, and keep these names, because the formulas point at them.

Inputs holds every assumption. Calc is formulas only. Outputs holds the results and the open items you bring to your CPA. Scenarios puts low, base, and high side by side. Sources logs each rule and each CPA confirmation.

Shade the input cells. Those are the only cells anyone types in. Rows 1 and 2 hold the title and headers, so the first field sits in B3. Don't insert heading rows, or every address shifts.

The workbook does four jobs. It collects every input, separates ordinary from accelerated depreciation, shows the tax effect only after loss limits, and lists what's still unverified.

Slide 5 · Every input carries a status until your CPA confirms it

Every input gets a value and a status. Verified means the document is in hand. CPA to supply means it's their call. Unverified means an estimate or a placeholder. Study means it comes from the report.

Here are five worth watching. The contract date matters because a contract signed on or before January 19, 2025 can change the bonus rate, even if you closed later. The land share stays unverified until your CPA picks a method. The 15%, 0%, and 7% split is a placeholder until you have a study.

The usable share is the big one. The base case sets it at 100%, and Maya and Chris haven't logged their hours yet. Your CPA sets that number.

The Outputs tab keeps every line separate. Ordinary depreciation is $6,256. Accelerated is $100,816. Together that's $107,072 before loss limits. In the base case all of it is usable, so nothing carries forward, and the tax effect at 32% is $34,263.

Don't add that tax effect to your operating cash flow in another model. It's a one-time estimate that depends on your CPA's rulings. It isn't income the property earns.

The tab also carries two depreciation pools and the adjusted basis, $315,928. When you sell, part of the depreciation you took can be taxed. OP.12 picks that up.

Check the workbook for these six mistakes

  • Typing into the Calc tab
  • Guessing a CPA cell
  • Bonus on land or the whole building
  • Counting furniture twice
Slide 7 · Check the workbook for these six mistakes

Six mistakes turn an honest estimate into a number that looks like cash.

Adding the tax effect to cash flow or to a lender's math. Treating the deduction as cash before your CPA sets the usable share. Typing into the Calc tab, because an overwritten formula breaks every line that depends on it. Guessing a CPA cell instead of leaving it blank and marking it CPA to supply.

Applying bonus to land or to the whole building. Bonus applies to the short-life pieces only. And counting furniture twice: once in Inputs B14 and again inside the study. Ask the provider how they treated furnishings already on your books.

Slide 8 · Four gates decide if it counts this year

The workbook builds in rules that work like yes-or-no gates.

First, dates. The IRS says 100% bonus is permanent for eligible property acquired after January 19, 2025. The screen checks the contract, closing, and in-service dates. Your CPA confirms eligibility.

Second, the Publication 527 home-use test. Cedar Ridge's 10 personal days sit under the 21.2-day limit.

Third, Publication 925's average stay. The average is 3.48 days, which is 7 days or less, so the participation tests decide passive status.

Fourth, the usable share. The $25,000 allowance is gone by $150,000 of MAGI, so it doesn't help Maya and Chris at about $185,000. If the loss is passive, it may be suspended and carried forward.

Your CPA also picks the building life. At 27.5 years instead of 39, the building line is $8,872, not $6,256.

The Scenarios tab changes only two inputs: the land share and the share of the building the study moves.

Low case: 30% land, 15% moved, a $79,020 deduction. High case: 15% land, 30% moved, $137,670. That's a wide range from two inputs, so that's where I'd spend my questions. Ask your CPA how the land share will be set. Ask the study provider how they support the split.

Look at the bottom row. A bigger year-one deduction means a lower adjusted basis. In the high case, basis drops to $285,330. That feeds the gain at sale in OP.12.

Slide 10 · If the loss is passive, this year's tax effect is $0

Now the changes your CPA is likely to raise, one at a time.

A June start with the mid-month convention takes the tax effect to $33,346. Dividing expenses by rental use, 212 of 222 days, takes it to $32,720. With no study at all, furniture still gets bonus and the building still depreciates, so you'd still see $12,807.

Then the passive case. If none of the loss is usable this year, the tax effect is zero. The $107,072 isn't lost. It carries forward. That's the case to fear, because the base assumes 100% usable and the hours aren't logged.

Against no study, the study adds $67,051 of deduction. That's $21,456 at 32%, or $16,956 after the fee.

Slide 11 · Get the study's evidence in writing first

The IRS publishes a guide for its examiners on cost-segregation studies, Publication 5653. It says there are no standards for preparing them, and that they vary widely in method, documentation, and preparer expertise.

So use it to shape what you ask for before you pay. The first four items here come from that guide. The last two are my additions, so the study fits the workbook. The asset schedule fills Inputs B17 to B19.

The lesson's full list has twelve items. It also covers the land method, the placed-in-service date the study assumed, and how they treated furniture already on your books. Get all of it in writing before you engage anyone.

Walk your CPA through it in four steps

  1. Fill documented inputs, leave CPA cells blank
  2. Send the workbook, receipts, calendar, and hour log
  3. Go in order: dates, methods, limits, rates
  4. Rerun Scenarios and save a dated copy
Slide 12 · Walk your CPA through it in four steps

Before the meeting, fill every input you can document. Leave the CPA cells blank rather than guessing, and mark each status honestly.

Send the workbook with the settlement statement, furnishing receipts, the study quote, your day calendar, and your hour log.

In the meeting, go in order. Dates and basis first. Then the method choices: bonus rate, convention, rental-use division. Then participation and limits. Then the rates.

Afterward, rerun Scenarios with their inputs, save a dated copy, and log their name and the date on the Sources tab. Then decide on the study with the added deduction, the passive case, and the fee side by side.

To open the meeting, try this: here is my estimate and every assumption behind it. Which inputs are wrong, and what would you enter instead?
