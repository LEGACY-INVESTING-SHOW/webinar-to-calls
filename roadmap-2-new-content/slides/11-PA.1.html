Write the buy box before you see listings

Slide 1 · Write the buy box before you see listings

This lesson starts the acquisition phase. By the end of it, you'll have a written, one-page buy box.

That page ranks your goals. It states your constraints and names your hard requirements. It describes the property you want in terms someone else could search with. And it ends with a pass rule, a sentence that tells you when to walk away from a listing without arguing about it. When I say pass, I mean decline, as in "we'll pass on that one."

This comes before market research because Sam's PA.2 and PA.3 use this page. PA.2 picks a market with it. PA.3 builds your pipeline with it.

We'll build Maya and Chris's buy box, then test it on Cedar Ridge at two prices.

Slide 2 · A buy box stops budget drift one listing at a time

A buy box is a search specification. It says what you'll buy, where, at what price, and under what conditions. Anything outside the box, you don't look at twice.

It protects you from two things. The first is drift. You start looking for a three-bedroom house at $380,000. A month later you're touring a $450,000 house with a pool, because the photos were good. Nobody decided to raise the budget by $70,000. It happened one listing at a time.

The second is wasted work. Every serious look costs hours: rules, comparable listings, underwriting, lender calls. Sam's PA.3 builds a pipeline of 5 to 10 qualified properties. Without a buy box, qualified means nothing.

So keep it short and specific, write it first, and change it only on purpose, with numbers. Never in a driveway during a showing.

Slide 3 · Rank your goals, because they pull against each other

Before you describe a property, rank your goals. The common seven are cash flow, long-term ownership, personal use, tax planning, portfolio growth, how involved you want to be, and safety. Safety means protecting your household's savings and income if the house underperforms.

They conflict. Your own peak-season week is a week you didn't rent. A deal bought for the depreciation can lose money every month, and the deduction may not be usable for your household. In 0.2, a co-host taking 18% of booking revenue turned Cedar Ridge's $5,572 a year into minus $7,798. And stretching to buy again sooner leaves a thinner reserve for this house.

Rank all seven, 1 to 7. Only the top two or three shape the search. Maya and Chris put safety first and tax planning fifth. Check with your CPA before tax planning goes in your top three.

Write down the limits you can't wish away

  • Max cash to close
  • Max total cash in, with furnishing
  • Loan type and down payment
  • Drive time, hours, and reserve floors
Slide 4 · Write down the limits you can't wish away

Constraints are limits you can't wish away. Most come straight from lesson 0.3.

Money comes as two caps. Max cash to close is the down payment, closing costs, and points, all due on closing day. Max total cash in adds furnishing. To find it, start with your liquid savings. Take out your emergency savings, your property reserve, and the other startup costs from your 0.3 cash stack. What's left is the most you can put in.

Why two caps? The cash-to-close cap stops a cheaper furnishing plan from turning into a bigger house.

Then your financing path: which loan types and what down payment. The lender decides eligibility later, in PA.6 and PA.7. Then distance in driving hours, time in hours per week, and minimum reserves. Those reserves are floors you don't spend below.

Slide 5 · Describe the property so Sam's filters can use it

Now the property itself. Keep each line concrete enough that Sam's filters can apply it.

Name the market type and what drives demand there. Sam's PA.2 picks the actual market. Inside that market, rules can change street by street, so write where you'll look and where you won't.

Property type changes legality, lending, and insurance. Condos and HOAs bring their own rules, and some lenders restrict certain types.

Price needs a floor and a ceiling. The floor screens out houses too small or worn. The ceiling comes from your money and your downside rule, not from what sellers ask. I'll show you how to calculate it.

More beds can raise the nightly rate and the wear. Water or height brings insurance questions. Septic, wells, long roads, and snow each add cost or response time. Decide how much you'll accept.

Fail one hard requirement and you walk away

  • Legal unhosted STR use at the address
  • A written insurance quote
  • Lender fit in writing
  • Under both cash caps
  • Weak case inside your downside rule
Slide 6 · Fail one hard requirement and you walk away

Hard requirements are lines a property must clear, every one, before it gets more of your time. They aren't preferences. Fail one, and you pass.

Five belong in almost every buy box. One: legal STR use at the exact address. Unhosted means you don't live there during guest stays. Any HOA or deed restriction has to allow it too. Use the SR.1 checklist, and the city or county licensing office confirms it.

Two: a written insurance quote at a premium your numbers can carry. Three: lender fit, in writing. Four: your two cash caps. No exceptions because the house is great.

Five: minimum downside performance. Set a floor on weak-case cash flow after the loan payment, and a minimum ratio of base-case net operating income (NOI) to that payment.

Add a sixth if 0.3 surfaced one. For Maya and Chris, the deal must work with self-management.

Agree on the pass rule before you see a listing

  • Any failed requirement means pass
  • Over the ceiling? Offer at it first
  • Log the reason for every pass
  • Change the box only in writing
Slide 7 · Agree on the pass rule before you see a listing

The pass rule is one or two sentences you agree on in advance. It has four parts.

The trigger is any failed hard requirement, or a price over your ceiling that the seller won't bring down. The action is pass. A listing above your ceiling can still get an offer at or under it. If the seller won't accept, pass. The record: log which requirement failed. And the change process: the box changes only on purpose, in writing, with new numbers, signed by every owner. Never during a showing.

Why log every pass? After 20 passes you'll see a pattern. Maybe every house in your range fails the insurance quote. That's information for Sam's PA.2 market choice, not a reason to lower your standards.

The log also stops you re-reading the same bad listing three times. You already decided.

Slide 9 · Their cash caps set a $393,000 price ceiling

Now the caps become a price.

On their DSCR loan terms, cash to close is 25% down, 3% closing costs, and one point. A point is 1% of the loan, and the loan is 75% of the price, so the point adds 0.75%. Cash to close is 28.75% of the price.

Take the $145,000 cap, minus $32,000 of furnishing. That leaves $113,000. Divide by 28.75% and you get $393,043. Their $115,000 cash-to-close cap would allow $400,000. The tighter cap wins, so the box ceiling is $393,000.

The downside rule gives another ceiling, but only house by house, because it depends on that house's revenue. For Cedar Ridge, the weak case reaches minus $6,000 at about $392,000. The lowest ceiling wins, so for this house the limit is about $392,000.

Slide 10 · Their buy box fits on one page

Here's the box. A drive-to lake leisure market, within 2.5 hours of home and a 10-minute drive of lake access. Sam's PA.2 picks the market.

Detached single-family. No HOA, or one with written permission to host. No condos. Three to four bedrooms, sleeps six to eight.

$325,000 to $393,000. The floor is their judgment call.

No pool. A hot tub only if the insurer quotes it. The punch list, small repairs before guests arrive, stays under $4,000.

The downside rule is theirs, not a lender's. Weak-case cash flow no worse than minus $6,000 a year, about $500 a month they could cover from salary. And base-case NOI at least 1.20 times the loan payment, a cushion before cash flow turns negative. Insurance must quote at or under $3,500 a year.

Then the pass rule, written out so neither of them argues later.

Slide 11 · Cedar Ridge clears the box at $385,000 and fails at $399,000

Now we test the box on Cedar Ridge.

At the $385,000 contract price, every number clears. Cash to close and total cash in are under both caps. The weak case loses $5,475, better than the minus $6,000 floor. And base NOI covers the $23,637 yearly loan payment 1.24 times.

Four checks are still pending: a written insurance quote, the address-level legal check, a written lender answer, and a named cleaner plus backup. Pending doesn't mean it clears.

At the $399,000 asking price, the same house fails three hard requirements. Total cash in goes over $145,000. The weak case loses $6,475. Coverage drops to 1.19. Base-case cash flow is still positive, $4,572, which is how people talk themselves into houses like this.

The $14,000 negotiation is what made the deal. If the seller had refused, the pass rule says walk. PA.8 covers that.

Slide 12 · A $435,000 pool house fails before anyone models revenue

This third test is my favorite, because it's so short.

A four-bedroom house with a pool comes up in the same market, listed at $435,000. It's hypothetical.

Cash to close is 28.75% of the price, $125,063. That's already over the $115,000 cap. Add $32,000 of furnishing, and total cash in is $157,063, over $145,000. And the pool is outside their complexity limit. An offer at $393,000 wouldn't fix the pool.

Slide 13 · Compute three price ceilings and use the lowest

Now your turn. The template has seven sections, A to G: goals, constraints, price ceiling, property description, hard requirements, pass rule, and a pass log. Save it as one page.

Section C is where your caps become a price. First, cash to close as a percentage of price: your down payment percentage, plus closing costs, plus points times the loan percentage. For Maya and Chris it was 28.75%.

Then divide your cash-to-close cap by that percentage. Then take your total cap, minus furnishing, and divide by the same percentage.

The downside-rule line comes from Sam's PA.4 model, house by house. Leave it blank for now.

Your ceiling is the lowest line you've filled in. Before you rely on it, your lender confirms the terms you used. If the real terms differ, recompute it.

Slide 14 · Write and sign your one-page buy box

Three tasks. Most take an evening.

First, rank your seven goals. If there are two of you, rank separately, then compare. Where you disagree is the conversation to have now, not at the offer.

Second, fill in your constraints and compute your price ceiling. Leave the downside line blank until PA.4.

Third, describe the property, write your hard requirements and pass rule, and have every owner sign. A signature is what makes the box hard to change in a driveway. Then start the pass log with the first listing that fails.

Take the page into Sam's lessons. In PA.2, check which markets allow unhosted STR use at all. In PA.3, your pass rule is the filter. In PA.4, test your downside rule on a real listing. PA.5 covers the tax timing from 0.2. Then I pick up at PA.6, where your financing line becomes a written lender quote.
