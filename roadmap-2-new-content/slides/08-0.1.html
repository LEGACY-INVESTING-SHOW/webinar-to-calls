Owning removes the limits a lease gave you

Slide 1 · Owning removes the limits a lease gave you

Welcome to Part Two. This is STR Concierge, the ownership side of the course. STR means short-term rental.

In Part One you leased a unit and ran it. Here you buy the house. The check is bigger, the loan runs for decades, and you'll hear from more professionals before you commit.

This lesson is the map. By the end you'll know who holds title, who carries the loan, who pays for a new roof, and who makes the final call. You'll see the course as ten go/no-go gates, and roughly how much money is on the line at each one.

I'll use Cedar Ridge, the lake house Maya and Chris are looking at, next to Alder Street, the Part One apartment.

A lease has edges. Owning removes them.

Slide 2 · A lease has edges. Owning removes them.

Think about what capped your downside at Alder Street. The lease had a term. The furniture had a price. The rent was a known monthly number.

Owning takes those edges away. You buy the building, the land under it, and every problem inside the walls. You sign a loan that runs for decades. Your cash goes in as a down payment and closing costs, and it doesn't come back out unless you refinance or sell.

Your Part One skills still work here: pricing, cleaning, guest communication, and local rules. What changes is the size of the commitment, and how many professionals you need to hear from before you make it.

Slide 3 · As an owner, the loan and the roof are yours

Here's what changes. Read the two repair rows twice. Maintenance is routine upkeep. Capital repairs replace a big piece of the building, like the roof, the HVAC system, or the septic system.

At Alder Street, a failed water heater is usually the landlord's problem. You lose some nights and you call them. At Cedar Ridge, it's your bill and your emergency. You pick the plumber, approve the cost, and pay it the same week.

The loan row matters too. A mortgage is secured by the house. Depending on the loan, the lender may also want a personal guarantee. Then your household is on the hook, not just the property. Your entity, the company that may hold title, is a PA.10 topic.

Owning the deed doesn't give you the right to host.

Slide 4 · Owning the deed doesn't give you the right to host

The approvals row changes shape when you own. In arbitrage, a landlord can say no, and many do. As an owner, there's no landlord to ask. But the city, the county, and any homeowners association still apply. Airbnb itself tells hosts to check HOA rules and local laws before hosting. Only the local rules give you the right to host at that address.

One more row: what you build. An owner can earn from four places: cash flow, loan paydown, a change in value, and tax timing. An operator earns mainly from cash flow. Four streams sound better, and sometimes they are. But each one carries a cost, and some never reach your bank account until you sell. Lesson 0.2 tests that with numbers.

This is the whole program on one slide. It runs from "what should I buy" to "is it performing the way I underwrote it". Underwriting is your spreadsheet estimate of what the property will earn and cost.

The 0.x lessons are readiness. PA lessons cover buying, and OP lessons cover operating. My lessons teach the decisions. Sam runs the live demos, like PA.2 to PA.5 and OP.10.

Slide 6 · Each professional answers one question. You make the call.

Owning brings more people into the deal, and each one has a narrow job. Trouble starts when you ask one of them a question that belongs to someone else. Your agent is not your tax advisor. Your lender is not your insurer.

A lender approval means they expect to be repaid. It doesn't tell you the deal is good for you. The inspector checks condition, and hiring one is your job. The appraiser estimates value for the lender. Title and escrow cover liens and how money moves at closing. An attorney handles contracts and local STR law as needed.

If you add a co-host later, you can hand off tasks. You can't hand off accountability. You make the final decision at every gate.

Slide 7 · Most bad deals should die at Gates 0 to 3

A deal moves forward only when it clears a gate. There are ten, numbered 0 to 9. Here are the first five.

The weak case is a bad-year version of your numbers: lower nightly rates and fewer booked nights. Your downside rule, from PA.1, is the most you'll accept losing in that year. Use a lender's current rate at Gate 3. The written quote comes at Gate 4.

Gates 0 to 3 cost only time. Gate 2 and Gate 3 are where most bad deals should die, because the city's rules are public and the underwriting is your own spreadsheet. The city or county licensing office confirms Gate 2, before any offer. A buyer who treats these gates as formalities finds the problem at Gate 6, after the inspections are paid for and earnest money is on the line.

Slide 8 · After Gate 5, walking away gets expensive

Here are the second five. Gate 5 is the offer. If the seller won't come to your walk-away number, you walk. PA.8 shows you how to set that number.

Gate 6 is diligence: the inspection, insurance quote, title, appraisal, and permit. And it has a clock. Contingencies are conditions in your purchase contract that let you cancel, and they run on deadlines. Miss one, and your options can shrink. Have your agent or attorney walk you through every deadline before you sign.

Gate 7 is closing. Check the Closing Disclosure, the lender's final statement of costs, and the wire instructions. If anything is off, stop before money moves. At Gate 8, don't publish the listing until it's insured, safe, permitted, furnished, and staffed. Gate 9 comes after you own, in Sam's OP.10 review.

Slide 9 · Cedar Ridge needs $142,688 on day one, 7 times Alder Street

Maya and Chris are modeling a $385,000 price, against a $399,000 asking price. That's the price the seller later accepts in PA.8. Their quote is a DSCR loan, short for debt service coverage ratio. PA.6 explains it.

The down payment is 25%, which is $96,250. Closing costs, including one point on the loan, come to $14,438. That's $110,688 of cash to close. Add the $32,000 furnishing budget and you're at $142,688.

Alder Street needed $20,370 on day one. So Cedar Ridge takes 7 times the cash. And that number still has no lender reserves or emergency savings in it. Lesson 0.3 adds those.

Slide 10 · In a bad year, Alder Street still earns. Cedar Ridge loses.

Here's the rest of the comparison. Alder Street's rent is $1,950 a month. Cedar Ridge's PITIA on the DSCR quote is $2,557. That's principal, interest, taxes, insurance, and association dues, and there's no HOA. The owner also sets aside 5% of gross booking revenue for repairs and 5% for capital items. That's $7,428 a year.

Now the bad year. Alder Street's stress case, at $180 a night and 60% booked, still nets $1,548. Cedar Ridge's weak case, at $265 a night and 50% booked, loses $5,475 after debt service. That's the pre-diligence figure, and PA.9 revisits it. The loss comes from reserves or the household.

Cedar Ridge does pay down $2,795 of loan in year one, but you can't spend that this year. Lesson 0.2 tests all four streams.

Slide 11 · The later a deal dies, the more it costs you

Here's why early gates matter, in dollars. These are running totals for Maya and Chris.

Gates 0 to 3 cost only time. Gate 4 may cost a lender fee. At Gate 5, earnest money goes in: $7,700, which is 2% of price and the figure they agree in PA.8. Whether you get it back depends on your contract. It stays at risk through Gate 6, which adds about $1,500 of inspections.

Then the jump. If you close at Gate 7, $110,688 of cash to close is committed, and the earnest money counts toward it. That's also the amount a wire-fraud scam would target, which PA.11 covers. Gate 8 adds $32,000 of furnishing, plus carrying costs until the first booking.

So the work you do in PA.1 and in Sam's PA.2 to PA.4 is the cheapest protection you'll buy.

  • Evidence: a document, link, or email
  • Who confirmed it
  • The date
  • Pass, fail, or open
  • If it failed, what ended it

No offer goes out until Gates 0 to 4 have written evidence.

Slide 12 · Log every gate, and leave it open until it's in writing

This is your working tool for the whole buying phase. You don't have a property yet, so today you set up the blank log. Fill in Gate 0 after lesson 0.3. Then start a new copy for each property you seriously consider from Sam's PA.3 list.

Each row gets the evidence, who confirmed it, the date, and the result. The evidence column is the one that matters. "The agent said it's fine" is not evidence. A city code section and the date you read it is.

Leave a gate open until you have a written answer. When a property fails, write down what ended it. After a few logs, you'll start to see patterns in your buy box and your market.
