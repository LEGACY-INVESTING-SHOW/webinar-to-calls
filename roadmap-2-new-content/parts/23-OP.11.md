### OP.11.0 Learning outcome

After this lesson you can build a trailing-12-month report for your first property and set it beside the original underwriting. You can price a cash-out refinance and a home equity line of credit (HELOC) in dollars per month, including a bad season. Then you write a pass rule for property two and apply it. The result is a dated yes, not yet, or no, with the numbers that produced it.

### OP.11.1 Property two is a decision about property one

Most people start the second-property conversation with a listing. I want you to start it with a bank statement.

The case for buying a second property rests on one claim: the first one works about the way you said it would. If that claim is true, a second similar property is a repeat of a tested plan. If it is false, a second property doubles an untested plan and adds a second loan.

So the first job is proof. You need to know what property one actually earned, what it actually cost, and how much cash you have left after both. The underwriting from Sam's PA.4 lesson was a forecast. Twelve months of operating records are evidence. This lesson is about reading the evidence before you commit more money.

The second job is liquidity. Buying property two usually means pulling cash from somewhere: savings, a refinance of property one, or a loan against your own home. Each source changes your monthly obligations and what you can survive. A second property funded by borrowed money needs a wider margin than one funded by savings.

### OP.11.2 Reading trailing performance against underwriting

Sam's OP.10 lesson shows the 30-day and 90-day review. Bring that report here. It is your baseline for the categories you track and how you pull the data. The trailing-12 is the same review stretched to a full year, so every season is in it.

Build it from records, not memory. Pull payouts and fees from each booking platform's earnings reports. Pull expenses from the property's bank account and card. Pull booked nights, stays, and average nightly rate from your property management software or the platform calendar. If your bookkeeping mixes property one with personal spending, fix that first; the trailing-12 is only as clean as the account behind it.

Set the report up in two columns: the underwriting you bought on, and the actual trailing 12 months. Then add a variance column. You're looking for four things.

First, split the revenue miss into rate and occupancy. A lower nightly rate and a lower occupancy point to different fixes. Rate problems often trace to pricing settings or competition. Occupancy problems can trace to calendar gaps, minimum stays, reviews, or demand. Sam's OP.5 lesson covers pricing tools; you only need to know which lever moved.

Second, find the expense lines that grew. Utilities, insurance renewals, and repairs are the usual ones. One large repair in the year may be a one-off. A pattern of repairs is a budget problem.

Third, check whether you actually funded the capital reserve. Underwriting often includes a line such as 5% of gross for future replacements. If that money stayed in the operating account and got spent, your cash flow looks better than it is.

Fourth, compare debt service. Principal and interest are fixed on a fixed-rate loan, but tax and insurance escrows can change at renewal or reassessment.

The number that matters most is net operating income (NOI): revenue minus operating costs, before the mortgage. NOI is what a lender and a future buyer look at. Cash flow is NOI minus debt service. If trailing NOI is well under the underwritten base case, the second property should wait until you know why.

### OP.11.3 Refinance math

A cash-out refinance replaces your current loan with a bigger one and hands you the difference, minus costs. It can feel like free money from appreciation. In practice you take on a new loan with a new payment, a new term, and new closing costs.

Here is the arithmetic in order:

1. New loan amount = appraised value × the lender's maximum loan-to-value (LTV).
2. Equity removed = new loan amount − current loan balance.
3. Cash out = equity removed − refinance closing costs.
4. New payment = the principal and interest on the new loan at the new rate and term.
5. New cash flow = NOI − new annual principal and interest.

Every input comes from a written lender quote, not a rule of thumb. The appraisal may come in lower than your estimate. The lender may cap LTV below what you expect for a short-term rental. The lender may also apply its own coverage ratio, as PA.6 explained. Compare quotes the way PA.7 taught, using the [CFPB's guide to comparing Loan Estimates](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/compare/compare-loan-estimates/) where the lender issues one.

Watch three things.

The term reset. A new 30-year loan starts amortizing from zero again. Your payment may look similar, but you are paying down principal more slowly.

Costs relative to cash. When the equity you remove is small, closing costs can eat a large share of it.

The low season. Run the new payment against your weak-case NOI as well as the base case. The refinance makes the loan larger, so it makes a weak year worse.

### OP.11.4 HELOC mechanics and the risk to your home

A home equity line of credit is a revolving line secured by a home you already own, usually your primary residence. The [CFPB's HELOC explainer](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-home-equity-line-of-credit-heloc-en-107/) describes two phases. During the draw period (the CFPB gives 10 years as an example) you can borrow up to your limit. Many plans set minimum payments based on the balance. When the draw period ends, the repayment period begins. Depending on the plan, you may be allowed to repay over time or you may owe the whole balance at once ([CFPB HELOC brochure](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_heloc-brochure.pdf)).

HELOCs usually carry a variable rate. The rate is based on a publicly available index, such as a prime rate. That means your payment can change from month to month ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-home-equity-line-of-credit-heloc-en-107/)). Price the line at today's rate and at a higher stress rate.

Two risks deserve plain words.

Your home is the collateral. The CFPB warns that if you can't repay what you borrowed, you could lose your home ([CFPB HELOC booklet](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/201603_cfpb_booklet_heloc.pdf)). When a short-term rental funded this way underperforms, the house you live in is on the line too.

The line can shrink when you need it. The same CFPB booklet says plans generally let the lender freeze or reduce the line in two situations. One is a significant decline in the home's value. The other is when the lender reasonably believes a material change in your finances will stop you from paying ([CFPB HELOC booklet](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/201603_cfpb_booklet_heloc.pdf)). If you planned to use an undrawn HELOC as your emergency reserve, a market dip can remove it at the worst time. For that reason, don't count undrawn HELOC capacity as a reserve.

The lender must disclose the draw and repayment periods, fees, how the minimum payment is figured, and how the rate can change ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-home-equity-line-of-credit-heloc-en-107/)). Read those disclosures before you model anything.

There is also a tax question for your CPA. IRS Publication 936 limits the home mortgage interest deduction on a home equity loan or line. The money must buy, build, or substantially improve the home securing it ([IRS Pub. 936](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p936)). Using the line for a rental down payment does not meet that test. Whether the interest can be deducted another way, such as a rental expense, depends on how the CPA traces the loan proceeds. Don't assume either answer in your model.

### OP.11.5 Same market, new market, and team capacity

Once the numbers work, the operating question starts. Can your team run two properties as well as it runs one?

A second property in the same market can share things. The same cleaner, handyman, and supply run may cover both. You already know the permit process, the seasonality, and the guest profile. You can reuse your OP.8 playbook with small edits. The risk is concentration: one bad season, one new ordinance, or one storm now hits both properties.

A second property in a new market spreads that risk. It also resets the learning curve. You'll need a new market check (Sam's PA.2), a new address-level permission review (resource SR.1), a new local team, and new vendor backups. Your first-year results from property one don't transfer to a market you've never operated in.

Either way, test team capacity against the OP.7 role list. The roles are turnover cleaner, backup cleaner, handyman, emergency contact, guest messaging, inspections, bookkeeping, and supplies. For each one, write who covers it for property two and who backs them up. If one cleaner covers both houses and both have Saturday turnovers, you need a second cleaner before you buy.

Then set up systems that work across properties. That means one bookkeeping file with a tag per property and one calendar view. Add one guest-message template set with property details, and one reserve policy. If a co-host helps, give them the least access they need. [Airbnb lets you set co-host permission levels](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/1534), and the listing owner remains responsible for what a co-host does.

### OP.11.6 Worked example: Cedar Ridge at the end of year 2

Maya and Chris bought Cedar Ridge with the DSCR loan (B4). It's now the end of year 2. They're thinking about a second lake-market house. They have three funding ideas: savings, the B7 cash-out refinance, and a HELOC on their primary home.

**Step 1. Trailing 12 months vs underwriting.** The trailing-12 below is a hypothetical year I built for teaching. The underwriting column is the canonical base case (B1).

All figures are assumptions. Trailing-12: ADR $289, occupancy 55%, 201 booked nights, 57 stays.

| Line | Underwriting (B1) | Trailing 12 (assumption) | Variance |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Gross booking revenue | $74,275 | $68,064 | -$6,211 |
| Platform service fee (15.5%) | -$11,513 | -$10,550 | +$963 |
| On-call handyman / inspection retainer | -$1,800 | -$1,800 | $0 |
| Cleaner pay | -$9,150 | -$8,550 | +$600 |
| Utilities, internet, trash | -$5,400 | -$5,850 | -$450 |
| Supplies | -$1,525 | -$1,596 | -$71 |
| Software | -$1,200 | -$1,200 | $0 |
| Repairs and maintenance | -$3,714 | -$5,200 | -$1,486 |
| Capital reserve (5% of gross, funded) | -$3,714 | -$3,403 | +$311 |
| Property tax | -$3,850 | -$3,850 | $0 |
| STR insurance | -$3,200 | -$3,450 | -$250 |
| **Operating costs** | **-$45,066** | **-$45,449** | **-$383** |
| **NOI** | **$29,209** | **$22,615** | **-$6,594** |
| DSCR loan principal and interest | -$23,637 | -$23,637 | $0 |
| **Cash flow** | **$5,572** | **-$1,022** | **-$6,594** |

Read it the way section OP.11.2 described.

Revenue came in at 91.6% of underwriting ($68,064 / $74,275). Both levers moved: the rate is $11 lower and occupancy is 3 points lower. Costs are only $383 higher in total. The platform fee and cleaner pay fell with lower revenue, while repairs, utilities, and insurance rose.

NOI came in at 77.4% of underwriting ($22,615 / $29,209). An 8.4% revenue miss became a 22.6% NOI miss. Most costs stay put when revenue drops, so the miss grows on the way down.

Cash flow went from a planned $5,572 to -$1,022. Maya and Chris covered the gap from savings. The capital reserve was funded, which is good. It means the -$1,022 is honest.

The repairs line includes a water heater replacement (assumption). If that's a one-off, next year's repairs may fall back toward $3,714. They should still find out why rate and occupancy slipped before counting on a better year.

**Step 2. The refinance (B7).** Value has grown 3% a year (assumption) to $408,446.

- New loan at 75% LTV: $408,446 × 0.75 = $306,334.
- Equity removed: $306,334 − $282,951 balance = $23,383.
- Cash out: $23,383 − $7,658 refinance costs = $15,725.
- New principal and interest at 7.0% for 30 years: $2,038 a month, or $24,457 a year.
- Change in annual debt service: $24,457 − $23,637 = $820 more a year.

The costs take 32.8% of the equity removed ($7,658 / $23,383). The rate is a bit lower, so the payment only rises $820. But the loan is bigger and the 30-year clock restarts.

Cash flow after the refinance:

| Case | NOI | New P&I | Cash flow |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Underwritten base | $29,209 | -$24,457 | $4,752 |
| Underwritten weak (B2) | $18,162 | -$24,457 | -$6,295 |
| Trailing 12 (assumption) | $22,615 | -$24,457 | -$1,842 |

The refinance delivers $15,725. That is not enough for a second down payment. Cedar Ridge's total cash in was $142,688. It also pushes the actual year's shortfall from -$1,022 to -$1,842.

**Step 3. The HELOC on their primary home.** Their home is worth $520,000 with a $260,000 mortgage. Suppose a lender allows combined loan-to-value (CLTV) of 80% (assumption; the lender sets the real limit).

- Maximum total debt: $520,000 × 0.80 = $416,000.
- Maximum line: $416,000 − $260,000 = $156,000.

Suppose they draw $100,000 (assumption) toward property two. I priced it at two variable rates (assumptions). During the draw period I assumed interest-only payments. For the repayment period I assumed a 20-year payoff.

| $100,000 drawn | 8.0% rate | 10.0% rate |
|---|---:|---:|
| Interest-only payment, monthly | $667 | $833 |
| Interest-only cost, annual | $8,000 | $10,000 |
| 20-year repayment payment, monthly | $836 | $965 |
| 20-year repayment payment, annual | $10,037 | $11,580 |

Now test what that does to a second house. Suppose property two is a Cedar Ridge twin with the same DSCR terms (assumption). Its base cash flow is $5,572 and its weak case is -$5,475 (B4). Subtract the HELOC interest:

| Property two cash flow after HELOC interest | 8.0% | 10.0% |
|---|---:|---:|
| Base case: $5,572 − interest | -$2,428 | -$4,428 |
| Weak case: -$5,475 − interest | -$13,475 | -$15,475 |

A base case that was thin becomes negative once the down payment is borrowed. The weak case becomes a five-figure annual gap, and their primary home secures part of the money.

One more stress: if their home's value drops 10% to $468,000 (assumption), the 80% limit falls to $374,400. With $100,000 drawn, they owe $360,000, which is 76.9% CLTV. The lender could freeze the remaining undrawn line under the terms the CFPB describes.

**Step 4. Liquidity.** At purchase, Maya and Chris had $210,000 in savings and put $142,688 into Cedar Ridge. That left $67,312 (assumption: before two years of income, spending, and the trailing shortfall). Six months of Cedar Ridge PITIA is $15,342 ($2,557 × 6). A second financed property would need its own reserve. A lender may require more ([Fannie Mae reserve requirements](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-4.1-01/minimum-reserve-requirements) show how conventional-loan reserves can extend past the down payment).

**Step 5. Apply the pass rule.** Here is the rule they wrote before looking at listings. The thresholds are their choices (assumptions), not industry standards.

| Test | Their threshold | Cedar Ridge result | Pass? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trailing-12 NOI vs underwritten base | At least 90% | 77.4% | No |
| Property one covers its own debt service | Cash flow ≥ $0 without top-ups | -$1,022 | No |
| Cash reserves after buying property two | 6 months PITIA per financed property plus personal emergency fund, none borrowed | Twin costs $142,688 in total. With $100,000 from the HELOC, savings supply $42,688 and $24,624 remains, below $30,684 (6 months PITIA × 2) before any emergency fund | No |
| Property two weak case with financing at stress rate | Loss they can cover from W-2 income for 12 months without new debt | -$15,475 at 10% | Needs their household budget |
| Team backups | Named backup for every OP.7 role for both houses | One cleaner, no backup | No |

Result: not yet. Maya and Chris don't need a new property. They need a better year from the one they have. Their next steps are specific. They find the cause of the rate and occupancy gap with Sam's OP.5 tools. They confirm the water heater was a one-off, and they hire a backup cleaner. Then they rerun this worksheet after the next 90-day review.

Notice what did not drive the answer. The refinance and HELOC were available. Borrowing capacity is not the same as a reason to borrow.

### OP.11.8 Who confirms what

**Confirm with:** your bookkeeper or CPA that the trailing-12 matches the property's books and bank records, and that repairs vs improvements are classified correctly.

**Confirm with:** the refinance lender, in writing: appraised value, maximum LTV for a short-term rental, and the coverage ratio definition. Also get rate, points, closing costs, reserves, and any prepayment penalty on the existing loan.

**Confirm with:** the HELOC lender, in the plan disclosures: maximum CLTV, draw and repayment periods, index and margin, and any rate caps. Also get the minimum payment formula, fees, and the conditions that let the lender freeze or reduce the line.

**Confirm with:** your CPA how HELOC or refinance interest will be treated, given IRS Publication 936 and how you use the proceeds. Ask how a second property affects your depreciation and loss position (resource SR.3).

**Confirm with:** the local licensing office or an STR attorney, for property two's exact address, that the planned use is permitted (resource SR.1).

**Confirm with:** your insurer that property two can be covered for short-term rental use, and whether a combined policy changes deductibles or terms.
