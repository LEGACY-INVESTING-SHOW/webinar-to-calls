### 3.3.0 Learning outcome

After this lesson you can build a channel-by-channel tax worksheet for one address. It shows which lodging taxes apply, who collects and remits each one on Airbnb, Vrbo, and direct bookings, and what you still owe. You'll know how to enter and maintain a permit or registration number on a listing, and what happens when it lapses. And you can reconcile a quarter of bookings against your tax filings before the agency finds the gap.

### 3.3.1 Three registrations people mix up

Separate three things students blur together. Each has its own agency, number, and deadline.

**The operating permit or registration.** This is permission to run a short-term rental at the address. The city's licensing or planning office issues it. It comes with a number you display on listings. Lesson 3.1 covered how to get it.

**The tax registration.** This is an account with each tax agency that collects lodging tax from you. It comes with its own account number and a filing schedule. Having a permit doesn't open a tax account. Having a tax account doesn't give you a permit. Los Angeles, for example, lists Transient Occupancy Tax registration as its own eligibility step for home-sharing hosts ([LA City Planning](https://planning.lacity.gov/blog/what-home-sharing-program)).

**Federal income tax.** This is tax on your profit, reported to the IRS once a year. Occupancy tax is money the guest pays. Income tax is money you pay on what's left. The IRS explains in [Publication 527](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p527) that rental income normally goes on Schedule E. If you provide substantial services for the guest's convenience, such as regular cleaning or changing linens, it may go on Schedule C instead. Your CPA decides which applies. Lesson 9.9 covers working with a CPA. This lesson stays on occupancy taxes.

### 3.3.2 The tax layers

Lodging taxes stack. One stay can carry several:

- State: a state sales tax that applies to lodging, a state hotel occupancy tax, or both.
- County: a county lodging or tourism tax.
- City: a city transient occupancy tax, often the largest local piece.
- Special districts: tourism improvement districts, convention center districts, or similar. Some charge a percentage. Some charge a flat amount per occupied night.

Each layer can have its own agency, its own form, and its own due date. Sometimes one agency collects several layers on a single return. Sometimes you file separately with each one.

The rules for each layer differ in four ways you must check:

- Taxable stay length. Most lodging taxes apply to stays under a set number of nights. Texas, for example, defines a short-term rental for its hotel tax as 29 days or less ([Texas Comptroller hotel tax FAQ](https://comptroller.texas.gov/taxes/hotel/faq.php)). Your city's line may differ.
- What's in the taxable base. Nightly rate, cleaning fee, pet fee, extra-guest fee. Airbnb says that where it collects, cleaning fees are included in the taxable base ([Airbnb help 1036](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/1036)). Your city's own rules decide what you must include on bookings you collect yourself.
- Exemptions. Some agencies exempt certain guests or stays, and they publish the list. Texas publishes [its hotel tax exemptions](https://comptroller.texas.gov/taxes/publications/96-224.php). Don't apply an exemption unless the agency's own document says it applies and you keep the paperwork it asks for.
- Filing frequency and zero returns. Monthly, quarterly, or annually, depending on the agency and sometimes on your volume. Many agencies expect a return every period once you're registered, even when you owe nothing. Texas, for example, requires "No Tax Due" hotel tax reports to be filed electronically ([Texas Comptroller hotel tax FAQ](https://comptroller.texas.gov/taxes/hotel/faq.php)). A missed zero return can bring a penalty in some jurisdictions.

### 3.3.3 Who collects on Airbnb

Airbnb collects and remits some occupancy taxes automatically in some places. It lists those places in its help center ([Airbnb help 2509](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/2509)). The system decides which taxes apply from the listing address you entered, so an address error can mean the wrong taxes ([Airbnb help 2509](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/2509)).

The part people miss is the gap. Airbnb says that even where it collects some taxes, you may still need to collect others. Its example is Airbnb collecting regional taxes but not local ones. Hosts in those areas remain responsible for assessing all other obligations ([Airbnb help 2509](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/2509); [Airbnb help 1036](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/1036)).

For taxes Airbnb doesn't collect, you have two choices. You can collect them yourself outside the platform. Or you may be able to use Airbnb's custom tax tool, if you're eligible. It lets you set a tax as a percentage per booking, or as a fee per guest, per night, or per guest per night. You also choose what it applies to ([Airbnb help 2523](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/2523)). Airbnb charges those taxes to the guest and pays them to you separately from your booking payout. You remain responsible for the amount, the filing, and the payment ([Airbnb help 2523](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/2523)).

Airbnb also says that whether or not it collects taxes, your payout is the same: your price minus Airbnb's service fee ([Airbnb help 1036](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/1036)). The tax sits on top.

### 3.3.4 Who collects on Vrbo

Vrbo works on a similar split. Where Vrbo is liable for a lodging tax, it collects and remits that tax on a return filed under HomeAway.com, Inc.'s taxpayer account. Where it isn't, the owner or manager is responsible for collecting and remitting ([Vrbo: collection and remittance of taxes](https://help.vrbo.com/articles/What-Stay-Taxes-Lodging-Taxes-does-HomeAway-collect-and-remit)). Vrbo publishes the jurisdictions where it collects by state ([Vrbo tax by jurisdiction](https://help.vrbo.com/articles/Tax-by-jurisdiction)).

For the taxes you owe, Vrbo describes three ways to handle them ([Vrbo: manage your lodging taxes](https://help.vrbo.com/articles/How-can-I-manage-my-lodging-taxes)):

1. Collect, file, and pay them yourself outside the platform.
2. Have Vrbo collect them from travelers and send them to you with your payout. You still file and pay.
3. Have Vrbo collect them with Avalara MyLodgeTax preparing, filing, and paying. Vrbo describes this as available at no extra cost.

Vrbo says it always collects and remits where the law requires it, whichever option you pick. Under option 2, the tax lands in your bank account and still belongs to the agency.

### 3.3.5 Direct bookings

On a direct booking, nobody collects for you. You quote the tax, charge it, hold it, report it, and pay it for every layer. Your booking engine or PMS needs the correct rates and base for each layer. Sam's 9.11 and 9.12 lessons show the direct-booking and Guesty setup. Before your first direct booking, confirm the tax settings in that tool against your worksheet.

The Texas Comptroller spells out this split plainly. Owners must collect and remit state hotel tax on rentals through their own website or a platform that doesn't collect. When a collecting platform handles state tax, the owner should still contact the county and city about local hotel tax ([Texas Comptroller hotel tax FAQ](https://comptroller.texas.gov/taxes/hotel/faq.php)). That's one state's rule. The pattern is common, though: platform collection often covers some layers, not all.

### 3.3.6 Entering and maintaining your registration number

Where a city requires a permit or registration number, the platform usually has a field for it. On Airbnb you add it under the listing's regulations settings. Go to the listing, find the laws and regulations section, and add the permit number ([Airbnb help 3953](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/3953)). Some cities let a listing show "City registration pending" while an application is in process. Others allow "Exempt" for categories the city exempts, such as a hotel ([Airbnb help 1634](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/1634)). Only use those statuses when the city's rules allow them for your listing.

In some cities the platform checks the number against the city's records. New York City is the clearest case. A booking service may not collect a fee for a short-term rental unless it has checked the city's electronic verification system. The check confirms the registration number is valid and the host's legal name and address match ([NYC OSE booking services FAQ](https://www.nyc.gov/site/specialenforcement/faq/faq-for-booking-services.page)). Airbnb's New York City page says hosts waiting for approval may set a 30-night minimum in the meantime ([Airbnb help 868](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/868)).

Numbers expire. Airbnb's Los Angeles page says the Home-Sharing Registration is valid for one year and must be renewed annually. The renewal window opens 30 days before expiration. If the registration lapses, you must apply again and enter the new number on the listing ([Airbnb help 864](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/864)). Failing to renew may affect your ability to host.

What happens when a number fails verification or expires depends on the city and the platform's arrangement with it. In a city like New York, a platform may not be able to take a booking without a verified number. So treat a lapse as lost revenue plus a compliance problem. Put every expiration date on your calendar with a reminder 60 days out (assumption: my suggested buffer). File the renewal as soon as the window opens.

Keep the name and address identical on the permit, the tax accounts, and the listing, down to the unit number. Save a dated screenshot of the listing showing the number after every renewal.

### 3.3.7 Reconciling booked revenue to tax reports

Reconciliation means proving that the tax you filed matches the stays you hosted. You do it every filing period, before you file. It catches four common errors. A direct booking gets the wrong rate. A tax layer gets left off. A cancellation stays in taxable receipts. Or platform-remitted receipts get reported as if you collected them.

You need three records for the period:

1. Your reservation report from the PMS: every stay, channel, nights, check-in date, nightly revenue, cleaning fee, status (completed, cancelled, refunded).
2. Each platform's earnings or transaction report, which shows payouts and any tax lines paid out to you.
3. Your tax worksheet, showing each layer's rate, base, and who collects it on each channel.

The steps:

1. Start from the reservation report. Remove cancelled stays with full refunds. Adjust partially refunded stays to what the guest actually paid.
2. Split taxable receipts by channel.
3. For each layer, separate the receipts where a platform remitted the tax from the receipts where you owe it.
4. Compute tax owed by layer on your share.
5. Add up the tax you actually collected by layer: platform tax payouts, Vrbo pass-throughs, and direct-booking tax charged.
6. Compare owed with collected. Explain every difference.

A shortfall usually means you forgot to charge something. You owe it anyway, out of your own pocket. An overage usually means you charged a guest tax that didn't apply. Ask the agency how to handle it. Some returns also ask you to report gross receipts including platform-remitted stays, then deduct them. Follow the form's instructions for each agency.

### 3.3.8 Worked example: Alder Street tax worksheet

Alder Street is Unit 4B, 218 Alder Street, a hypothetical two-bedroom in an unnamed city. Its canonical numbers leave occupancy taxes out of revenue and expenses, because guests pay them and they pass through. This worksheet shows what that pass-through looks like.

**Every rate, rule, and collection arrangement below is an assumption for teaching.** They describe a made-up jurisdiction, "Alder City (hypothetical)." They are not any real city's rates. Before you use a worksheet like this, replace each row with the official agency's rate, its link, and the date you checked it.

**The hypothetical tax layers (all assumptions)**

| Layer | Rate | Base | Taxable stays | Filed with | Frequency |
|---|---:|---|---|---|---|
| State lodging tax | 6.0% | Nightly + cleaning | 29 nights or fewer | State revenue department | Quarterly |
| County lodging tax | 2.0% | Nightly + cleaning | 29 nights or fewer | County treasurer | Quarterly |
| City transient occupancy tax | 5.0% | Nightly + cleaning | 29 nights or fewer | City finance office | Monthly |
| Tourism district assessment | $2.00 per occupied night | Nights | 29 nights or fewer | City finance office, on the city return | Monthly |

**Who collects, by channel (all assumptions)**

| Layer | Airbnb | Vrbo | Direct |
|---|---|---|---|
| State 6% | Airbnb collects and remits | Vrbo collects and remits | Operator |
| County 2% | Airbnb collects and remits | Operator (Vrbo collects and passes through) | Operator |
| City 5% | Operator (custom tax tool) | Operator (Vrbo collects and passes through) | Operator |
| District $2/night | Operator (custom tax tool) | Operator (Vrbo collects and passes through) | Operator |

**Channel mix (assumption).** The canonical base case has 85 stays of 3 nights, 255 nights, at $205 plus a $110 cleaning fee. Split those stays: 64 on Airbnb, 13 on Vrbo, 8 direct. The canonical A1 model charges the 15.5% Airbnb fee on all revenue. Vrbo and direct fees differ, but we don't re-run A1 here. This split only drives the tax worksheet.

**Step 1: taxable receipts by channel.**

| Channel | Stays | Nights | Nightly ($205 x nights) | Cleaning ($110 x stays) | Taxable receipts |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Airbnb | 64 | 192 | $39,360 | $7,040 | $46,400 |
| Vrbo | 13 | 39 | $7,995 | $1,430 | $9,425 |
| Direct | 8 | 24 | $4,920 | $880 | $5,800 |
| **Total** | **85** | **255** | **$52,275** | **$9,350** | **$61,625** |

The total matches canonical gross booking revenue.

**Step 2: tax by layer and channel, for the year.**

| Channel | State 6% | County 2% | City 5% | District $2/night | Total tax |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Airbnb | $2,784.00 | $928.00 | $2,320.00 | $384.00 | $6,416.00 |
| Vrbo | $565.50 | $188.50 | $471.25 | $78.00 | $1,303.25 |
| Direct | $348.00 | $116.00 | $290.00 | $48.00 | $802.00 |
| **Total** | **$3,697.50** | **$1,232.50** | **$3,081.25** | **$510.00** | **$8,521.25** |

Guests pay about $8,521 in occupancy taxes on $61,625 of bookings, roughly 13.8% on top. For one typical stay: $725 x 13% = $94.25, plus 3 nights x $2 = $6. That's $100.25 of tax on a $725 stay.

**Step 3: who remits what.**

| Layer | Remitted by platforms | Remitted by operator |
|---|---:|---:|
| State | $3,349.50 (Airbnb $2,784.00 + Vrbo $565.50) | $348.00 (direct) |
| County | $928.00 (Airbnb) | $304.50 (Vrbo $188.50 + direct $116.00) |
| City | $0 | $3,081.25 (all channels) |
| District | $0 | $510.00 (all channels) |
| **Total** | **$4,277.50** | **$4,243.75** |

Half the tax flows through the operator's own accounts in this setup. That's with Airbnb carrying 75% of the stays. A student who assumes "Airbnb handles taxes" would miss $4,243.75 of filings. That includes every dollar of the city and district tax.

**Step 4: a quarterly reconciliation.** Take one quarter (assumption: 24 stays of 3 nights at $205, split 17 Airbnb, 4 Vrbo, 3 direct). One direct stay cancelled with a full refund. On the two remaining direct stays, the operator's booking tool charged state, county, and city tax but was missing the district fee.

*Receipts:*

| Channel | Stays kept | Taxable receipts | Note |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Airbnb | 17 | $12,325 | 51 nights |
| Vrbo | 4 | $2,900 | 12 nights |
| Direct | 2 | $1,450 | 3 booked, 1 refunded ($725 removed) |
| **Total** | **23** | **$16,675** | 69 nights |

*Platform-remitted this quarter (not on the operator's returns as tax owed):* Airbnb state $739.50 and county $246.50. Vrbo state $174.00.

*Tax the operator owes, by layer:*

| Layer | Calculation | Owed |
|---|---|---:|
| State | Direct $1,450 x 6% | $87.00 |
| County | (Vrbo $2,900 + direct $1,450) x 2% | $87.00 |
| City | $16,675 x 5% | $833.75 |
| District | 69 nights x $2 | $138.00 |
| **Total owed** | | **$1,145.75** |

*Tax the operator actually collected:*

| Source | Layers | Collected |
|---|---|---:|
| Airbnb custom tax payout | City $616.25 + district $102.00 | $718.25 |
| Vrbo pass-through | County $58.00 + city $145.00 + district $24.00 | $227.00 |
| Direct bookings | State $87.00 + county $29.00 + city $72.50 | $188.50 |
| **Total collected** | | **$1,133.75** |

*Variance:* $1,145.75 owed - $1,133.75 collected = **$12.00 short**. That's the district fee on the two direct stays: 2 stays x 3 nights x $2. The operator pays the $12 from operating cash and fixes the booking-tool setting before the next direct booking.

Twelve dollars is small. The same mistake across several units and years isn't, and neither is a whole missed layer like the $3,081 of city tax in Step 3.

*A check on the Airbnb payout:* $12,325 of bookings less the 15.5% service fee ($1,910.38) is a booking payout of $10,414.62. The $718.25 of custom taxes arrives separately. If your bank deposits and the platform report don't reconcile to those two figures, find out why before you file.

### 3.3.10 Who confirms what

**Confirm with:** each tax agency (state, county, city, district), in writing or on its official site. Confirm the rate, taxable base, taxable stay length, exemptions, filing frequency, and zero-return rule.

**Confirm with:** each platform's help page for your exact address, on the day you set up the listing, which taxes it collects and remits there. Recheck when you renew your permit, because collection agreements change.

**Confirm with:** the city licensing office, how the permit number must appear, whether platforms verify it, and what happens to bookings if it lapses.

**Confirm with:** a CPA, on three points. How collected occupancy taxes and platform fees appear in your books. Whether your rental income belongs on Schedule E or Schedule C. Your estimated income tax payments. Lesson 9.9 covers that relationship.

**Confirm with:** your bookkeeper or PMS vendor, that the direct-booking tax settings match your worksheet before the first direct reservation. Lesson 9.8 covers bookkeeping.
