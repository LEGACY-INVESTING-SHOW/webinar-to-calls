### 3.1.0 Learning outcome

After this lesson you can list every layer of permission that applies to one exact address, from the state down to the lease. You can tell whether a city's rules allow the model you plan: hosted or unhosted, primary residence or not, how many nights, how long each stay. You'll also know which answers must come in writing, and from whom. The output is a completed permission ladder for one address with a permitted, prohibited, or unresolved result.

### 3.1.1 Why "Is Airbnb legal in this city?" is the wrong question

Students often ask whether a city is "Airbnb-friendly." That label doesn't help you. Permission attaches to an address, a use, and a person. A city may allow short stays in general. The building, the zoning district, the permit category, or your lease can still block the unit you want.

Here's a simple way to picture it. Think of a ladder with rungs. Each rung is a separate authority that can say no. You need a yes on every rung for your exact use. One no anywhere ends the deal, even if every other rung says yes.

Airbnb's own [regulations overview](https://www.airbnb.com/resources/hosting-homes/a/learn-about-hosting-regulations-7) says many cities require you to register, get a permit, or get a license before you list or accept guests. It also points out zoning rules and taxes. Treat that page as a starting point. It isn't the final word for any address. The final word is the official ordinance, the city office that administers it, and the documents that bind your building and your lease.

### 3.1.2 The layers of permission

Work from the widest authority down to the narrowest. Each layer has its own source document.

**State.** Some states set rules that apply everywhere in the state, such as statewide lodging tax registration or limits on what cities may regulate. Many states leave most short-term rental rules to local governments. Check whether your state has a law on the subject before you assume it's purely local.

**County or city land use and zoning.** Zoning decides what uses a parcel may have. A residential zone may treat short stays as a commercial lodging use and prohibit it. It may allow short stays only as an accessory use to someone's home. The city's zoning map tells you the district. The zoning code tells you what that district allows.

**Short-term rental permit or registration.** Many cities run a separate licensing program on top of zoning. This is where you find the permit category, the eligibility test, fees, caps, renewal, and the registration number you must display. Some programs sit in the planning department. Others sit in housing, finance, or a special enforcement office.

**Building, fire, and safety rules.** Building and fire codes can set occupancy limits, smoke and carbon monoxide alarm rules, and exit requirements. Some cities inspect before issuing a permit. Some programs also bar short stays in certain building types, such as rent-regulated or affordable housing.

**HOA, condo, or co-op rules.** Private rules recorded against the property can ban short rentals, even where the city allows them. Read the declaration, bylaws, and house rules. Board minutes sometimes show a ban in progress.

**Lease and landlord.** For arbitrage, this is the rung you control least. The lease must allow the use, in writing. Some cities make the landlord's written consent part of the permit application. Lesson 3.5 and the updated 5.3 addendum lesson cover the documents.

Two practical layers sit alongside these. Your insurer must agree to cover guests at this address. The booking platform must accept your registration number where the city requires one. Lesson 3.3 covers the platform side.

### 3.1.3 Hosted vs. unhosted stays

A hosted stay means the host lives in the unit and is present during the stay. Guests usually rent a room while the host is home. An unhosted stay means guests have the whole unit and nobody lives there.

Cities treat these very differently. Hosted stays look like a resident earning income from a spare room. Unhosted stays look like a hotel room carved out of the housing supply. Many cities allow the first and restrict or ban the second.

Arbitrage is almost always unhosted. You lease a whole unit and you don't live there. So when you read an ordinance, find the words that decide whether the host must be present. That one clause often settles the question before anything else.

### 3.1.4 Primary-residence rules

A primary-residence rule says you may only host in the home where you actually live. Cities usually define it by time, such as living there more than half the year. Some also require that it be the only property where you host.

For an arbitrage operator, a primary-residence rule is usually a hard stop. You can't make a unit your primary residence by signing a lease on it while you live somewhere else. Cities that use this rule often ask for proof such as a driver's license, voter registration, or tax records at that address.

### 3.1.5 Caps: nights, permits, and units

Caps come in several forms:

- A night cap limits how many nights per year you may host. Some cities cap unhosted nights and leave hosted nights uncapped.
- A permit cap limits how many permits the city issues, sometimes by neighborhood or zoning district. New applicants may join a waitlist.
- A unit cap limits how many permits one person or one building may hold.

Night caps hit arbitrage hardest. Your rent is due 12 months a year. If the city lets you host only part of the year, the math has to work on those nights alone. The worked example below shows how quickly that fails.

### 3.1.6 Minimum-stay rules

Many cities define a "short-term rental" by stay length, often under 30 nights. Stays at or above the line are treated as ordinary residential tenancies, with different rules.

Count carefully. New York City explains that under state law the check-in date is day zero. So a 29-night stay is a short-term rental and a 30-night stay is permanent occupancy ([NYC FAQ for prospective hosts](https://www.nyc.gov/site/specialenforcement/faq/faq-for-prospective-hosts.page)).

Longer stays aren't a loophole. Once a guest stays past the threshold, tenant protections can apply. Depending on the state and city, that can mean eviction procedures and rent rules. Treat month-plus furnished rentals as a separate business and have an attorney review it.

### 3.1.7 Transferability: does the permit follow the operator?

Ask who the permit belongs to. Some cities issue it to the property owner. Some issue it to the host, meaning the person who lives there or runs the rental. Some tie it to both.

This matters in three situations:

- You take over a unit that "already has a permit." If the permit belongs to the previous host, it may not cover you.
- The landlord sells the building. A permit issued to the owner may end with the sale.
- You add a partner or form an LLC. Some programs require the host to be a natural person, so an entity may not qualify.

Never assume a permit transfers. Ask the licensing office in writing and keep the answer.

### 3.1.8 Two cities, two rulebooks

Here are two large cities with very different rules. I'm using them to show how much the answer changes with the address. Rules change, so check the official pages on the day you research.

**New York City (Local Law 18 registration).** The city's Office of Special Enforcement (OSE) administers it. Here's what its official pages say:

- Hosts of short-term rentals in private dwellings or Class A multiple dwellings must register with the city, or the rental is illegal. The application fee is $145, non-refundable ([OSE tips for hosts](https://www.nyc.gov/site/specialenforcement/registration-law/tips-for-hosts.page)).
- The host must be a natural person and the permanent occupant of the unit. The applicant must certify that a lease or other agreement doesn't prohibit short-term rentals ([OSE tips for hosts](https://www.nyc.gov/site/specialenforcement/registration-law/tips-for-hosts.page)).
- Short-term rentals are allowed only if the host stays in the same unit as the guests. No more than two paying guests may stay at a time. Host and guests must share a common household, and guests must have access to the whole unit. Interior doors that lock guests into their room count as a lack of access ([OSE tips for hosts](https://www.nyc.gov/site/specialenforcement/registration-law/tips-for-hosts.page)).
- You cannot rent out an entire apartment or home for less than 30 days, even if you own or live in the building. That applies to all permanent residential buildings ([OSE FAQ for prospective hosts](https://www.nyc.gov/site/specialenforcement/faq/faq-for-prospective-hosts.page)).
- A registered host must include the registration number in every advertisement ([OSE registration rules and laws](https://www.nyc.gov/site/specialenforcement/registration-law/registration-rules-and-laws.page)).
- Booking services such as Airbnb and Vrbo may not collect a fee for a short-term rental unless they've checked the city's electronic verification system. The check confirms a valid registration number and that the host's legal name and address match ([OSE booking services FAQ](https://www.nyc.gov/site/specialenforcement/faq/faq-for-booking-services.page); [Local Law 18 text](https://legistar.council.nyc.gov/View.ashx?GUID=B39F0576-95A1-425C-9005-DB2111F440AF&ID=10898489&M=F)).
- Building owners can put a building on a Prohibited Buildings List by certifying that leases there prohibit short-term rentals. OSE must deny registration for units in listed buildings ([OSE Prohibited Buildings List](https://www.nyc.gov/site/specialenforcement/registration-law/pbl.page)).

Airbnb's [New York City page](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/868) says hosts waiting for approval may set a 30-night minimum while OSE reviews eligibility.

**Los Angeles (Home-Sharing Ordinance).** City Planning runs registration, and the housing department explains who is eligible. Here's what the official pages say:

- Home-sharing is limited to your primary residence, meaning where you live more than six months of the year. It must also be the only property where you home-share ([LA Home-Sharing FAQ](https://planning.lacity.gov/ordinances/docs/homesharing/FAQ.pdf); [What is the Home-Sharing Program?](https://planning.lacity.gov/blog/what-home-sharing-program)).
- Standard home-sharing allows up to 120 days per calendar year. Going past 120 requires an Extended Home-Sharing registration, which has extra criteria. One is holding a valid home-sharing registration for at least six months ([LAHD Home Sharing Ordinance](https://housing.lacity.gov/articles/home-sharing-ordinance); [LA Home-Sharing FAQ](https://planning.lacity.gov/odocument/1dab409a-d9cb-47f2-9a29-0348389cf752/FAQ.pdf)).
- Units covered by the Rent Stabilization Ordinance (RSO) are not eligible. That covers most multifamily units built before October 1, 1978. Legally restricted affordable housing and certain units taken off the rental market are also excluded ([LAHD Home Sharing Ordinance](https://housing.lacity.gov/articles/home-sharing-ordinance); [Council District 4 illegal home-sharing page](https://cd4.lacity.gov/illegal-homesharing/)). The same city pages say you can check RSO status by address in the city's ZIMAS parcel tool, under the Housing tab.
- Hosting must be in a structure built for residential use, with no pending citations or violations on the property ([What is the Home-Sharing Program?](https://planning.lacity.gov/blog/what-home-sharing-program)).
- A renter must submit the landlord's written, notarized approval with the application ([Landlord's Authorization affidavit](https://planning.lacity.gov/odocument/b044ea0f-be5d-4da5-8144-18b0c3fad092/AFFIDAVIT.pdf); [LA Home-Sharing FAQ](https://planning.lacity.gov/odocument/1dab409a-d9cb-47f2-9a29-0348389cf752/FAQ.pdf)).
- Hosts must register for the city's Transient Occupancy Tax and post their registration number on every listing ([What is the Home-Sharing Program?](https://planning.lacity.gov/blog/what-home-sharing-program)).
- The city's FAQ lists an $89 registration or renewal fee. Extended Home-Sharing review costs $850 more, or $5,660 when discretionary review is required ([LA Home-Sharing FAQ](https://planning.lacity.gov/odocument/1dab409a-d9cb-47f2-9a29-0348389cf752/FAQ.pdf)). Confirm current fees with City Planning, since FAQ documents can lag fee updates.

Airbnb's [Los Angeles page](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/864) says stays of 1 to 29 days need a home-sharing permit on the listing before it's published. It also says the registration is valid for one year and must be renewed annually.

Compare the two. New York City allows hosted stays with the host present and two guests at most. Los Angeles allows unhosted stays in your own primary residence, with a night cap and building-type exclusions. Both cities allow some short-term renting. Neither allows the Alder Street model, and they block it for different reasons.

### 3.1.9 Worked example: Alder Street against New York City and Los Angeles

Alder Street is Unit 4B, 218 Alder Street: a hypothetical two-bedroom that sleeps 5, in a four-unit building. The student leases it and lives elsewhere. The plan is unhosted, whole-unit stays averaging 3.0 nights, at 70% occupancy (255 nights a year). All figures are assumptions from the course's canonical example. The city is unnamed on purpose. Here we drop the same unit into two real rulebooks.

**Test 1: if Alder Street were in New York City.**

| Rule | Alder Street plan | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Host is a natural person and permanent occupant | Operator lives elsewhere | Fails |
| Host present in the unit during the stay | Unhosted | Fails |
| No more than two paying guests | Sleeps 5 | Fails |
| No entire unit for less than 30 days | Whole unit, 3-night stays | Fails |
| Building not on the Prohibited Buildings List | Unknown | Check |
| Registration number on every ad; platform verifies it | Operator can't register | Fails |

Result: **prohibited** as planned. The entire-unit rule alone ends it. A 30-night-plus furnished rental is a different business under tenant law, and that falls outside this model. A hosted room with the host present isn't arbitrage.

**Test 2: if Alder Street were in Los Angeles.**

| Rule | Alder Street plan | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Primary residence (host lives there more than 6 months a year) | Operator lives elsewhere | Fails |
| Only property where the host home-shares | Would be a second property | Fails |
| Not an RSO unit | Four-unit building; RSO status depends on build date and other facts | Check in ZIMAS |
| Up to 120 days a year unless Extended | Plan is 255 nights | Fails |
| Notarized written landlord approval | Landlord offered a lease; approval not yet signed | Open |
| TOT registration and number on every listing | Not yet | Open |

Result: **prohibited** as planned. The primary-residence rule alone ends it.

Now suppose someone tried to fix it. Could they move in, make it their primary residence, and host within the standard cap? Here's what 120 nights does to the numbers, keeping every other canonical input the same:

- 120 nights at $205 = $24,600 nightly revenue
- 40 stays x $110 cleaning = $4,400
- Gross booking revenue = $29,000
- Platform fee at 15.5% = -$4,495
- Cleaner and supplies: 40 x ($95 + $14) = -$4,360
- Fixed costs (rent, utilities, insurance, software, repairs, permit) = -$30,590
- **Net = -$10,445 a year**, about -$870 a month

To break even at 120 nights, the nightly rate would need to reach about $308 (assumption: it solves to $308.01 from the canonical inputs). That's roughly 50% above the $205 base rate. And the student would now be living in the unit, which changes everything else about the plan. Under a 120-night cap, the arbitrage model stops working at this rent.

**What the example teaches.** The same unit, the same lease, the same math, gets two different "no" answers for two different reasons. In the unnamed city where Alder Street actually sits, you still don't know the answer. You find it by working the ladder in 3.1.10 at the real address.

### 3.1.10 The permission flow, in words

Here's the flow I want you to follow for every address. Picture it as a vertical chain of boxes, each with a "stop" exit on the right side.

1. **Write down the exact plan.** Address, unit, parcel number, hosted or unhosted, maximum guests, typical stay length, nights per year. Every answer depends on these facts.
2. **Identify every jurisdiction.** Is the address inside city limits or unincorporated county? Is it in a special district? Many students check the wrong city because of a mailing address. Use the city or county parcel lookup.
3. **Find the official ordinance.** Use the city's code and the licensing program's own page. Airbnb's city pages and regulation overview can point you there. A blog post, a Facebook group, or another host's opinion is not a source. Stop exit: the ordinance prohibits your model outright.
4. **Check the parcel and building.** Zoning district, building type, rent-regulation or affordable-housing status, and any building-level list like New York City's Prohibited Buildings List. Stop exit: the district or building type is excluded.
5. **Check private rules.** HOA, condo, or co-op documents and the lease. Stop exit: a recorded ban or a lease that prohibits the use.
6. **Check permit mechanics.** Category, eligibility, caps, waitlist, fees, renewal, transferability, required local contact, safety inspection, and the rule for displaying the number. Stop exit: you can't meet eligibility, or the cap is full.
7. **If anything is unclear, ask the licensing office in writing.** Describe the plan in the terms from step 1. Ask a specific yes-or-no question. Save the reply with the date and the name of the person who answered.
8. **Preserve the evidence.** Save a PDF or dated screenshot of each official page, the ordinance section, the zoning map, and every written answer. Put them in the deal folder with the SR.1 checklist.
9. **Record a decision.** Permitted, prohibited, or unresolved, with a recheck date. Unresolved means you do not sign a lease yet.

### 3.1.12 Who confirms what

**Confirm with:** the city or county licensing office, in writing. Ask whether your exact plan fits a permit category at this address, and who the permit holder must be.

**Confirm with:** the zoning or planning department that the parcel's zoning district allows the use.

**Confirm with:** an attorney licensed in the state. They confirm that the lease and any addendum allow the use, and whether stays of 30 nights or more create a tenancy.

**Confirm with:** the HOA, condo, or co-op board, in writing, if the property has one.

**Confirm with:** the landlord, in a signed document, that you may use the address on a permit application. Some cities require a specific form, as Los Angeles does with its notarized affidavit.

**Confirm with:** an insurer, that the policy covers short-term guests at this address under the permit category you'll hold.
