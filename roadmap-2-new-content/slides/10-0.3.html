Fund the full cash stack before you shop

Slide 1 · Fund the full cash stack before you shop

This lesson answers one question before you look at a single listing. Can you actually afford to own?

We'll add up the full cash an ownership deal needs, not just the down payment. We'll compare it with the money you really have, after you keep your own emergency savings. We'll test a delayed opening and a weak first season. And you'll finish with a one-page readiness scorecard.

That scorecard sends you to one of four places: PA.1, where you write your buy box; better financing first; the Part One lease path; or wait.

I'll run one example couple, Maya and Chris, through all of it. Hearing "not yet" here is much cheaper than hearing it at a closing table.

Slide 2 · $96,250 down against $210,000 saved looks ready twice over

Meet Maya and Chris. They're a hypothetical two-earner household with $185,000 of income and credit scores around 740. They have $210,000 in liquid savings outside retirement accounts.

They're buying Cedar Ridge, a three-bedroom lake-market house, for $385,000 with 25% down. They'll use it about 10 days a year. It isn't their main home.

They have two loan quotes. The DSCR loan qualifies mainly on the property's income, at 7.25% with one point. The conventional loan qualifies on their household income and debts, at 6.875% with no points. PITIA is the full monthly payment: principal, interest, taxes, insurance, and dues.

Most buyers stop at the down payment. $96,250 against $210,000 looks easy. That math leaves out most of the bill.

Slide 3 · The full bill has eight layers, and you keep two

Think of the cash requirement as a stack of eight layers. You need all of them funded, or you're counting on the house to perform from day one.

Six layers are money you spend. The down payment, closing costs and points, and furnishing. Then the repair and safety punch list, permits and inspections, and the carrying costs before the first booking. The loan payment starts whether or not a guest has booked.

Two layers are money you still hold after all that: the property reserve and your household's emergency savings.

The common failure is merging those two. A buyer counts the same dollars for the house and the family. Then the water heater fails in the month a paycheck is late, and there's one pile of cash for two problems.

Count only liquid savings toward the stack

  • Checking, savings, and brokerage count
  • Retirement money is a lender and CPA question
  • A HELOC is borrowed money, not savings
  • Gifts and seller credits come with conditions
Slide 4 · Count only liquid savings toward the stack

Money can come from several places, and your lender may treat each one differently. In this lesson I count only liquid savings: checking, savings, and taxable brokerage.

Some lenders may count part of a retirement balance toward reserves, but withdrawals can carry taxes and penalties. Ask your lender and CPA.

Maya and Chris have $260,000 of home equity on paper. A HELOC lets them borrow against it, usually at a variable rate, with their home as collateral. It adds a payment. OP.11 covers HELOCs for scaling.

Gifts and partner money follow lender rules (PA.10). A seller may cover some closing costs, but that's a PA.8 negotiation.

If the stack only works with borrowed money, write that down as a finding.

Slide 5 · The lender's reserve is a floor, not a plan

Lenders measure reserves in months of the new loan's payment. Fannie Mae has no minimum for a one-unit principal residence, but Cedar Ridge isn't Maya and Chris's main home. Their lender decides which category it falls in. The DSCR quote isn't a Fannie Mae loan, so that lender sets its own rule.

For teaching I assume 6 months on both quotes. That's $15,342 on the DSCR quote and $14,904 on the conventional. Get the real number from each lender in writing.

The lender will also check your credit and, on a conventional loan, your debt-to-income ratio. With no rental income counted, Maya and Chris come to about 35.6%. Ask each lender for its limit.

After closing, the lender stops watching. That reserve becomes your cushion for the house.

Slide 6 · A late opening plus a weak first year leaves $96

Two things go wrong often enough that you should budget for both.

The first is a delayed opening. Permits run long, furniture arrives late. Every month before the first guest costs $3,257 on the DSCR quote, with nothing coming in. Three extra months is $9,771.

The second is a weak first season. A new listing has no reviews. The example's weak case loses $5,475 a year.

Put them together. The $15,342 reserve pays for the delay, then the weak year, and $96 is left. That's the pairing that can force a sale at a bad time.

So Maya and Chris set their own reserve target to survive it: $15,246. The lender's six-month figure just covers it.

  • Some weeks bring an 11 p.m. lockout

Slide 7 · Self-managing Cedar Ridge takes about 140 hours a year

Cash isn't the only resource. Owning also takes time.

In the base case, Maya and Chris self-manage with a local cleaner and an on-call handyman. I assume 1.5 hours per stay and 4 hours a month of admin. With 61 stays, that's about 140 hours a year, or 2.7 hours a week. Some weeks take much more.

Those hours matter for taxes too. Material participation tests compare your hours with other people's, like the cleaner's, who may log 244. Lesson 0.2 covers it, and your CPA confirms how your hours count.

If you don't want those hours, you hire a co-host. In 0.2, one charging 18% of booking revenue left Cedar Ridge at -$7,798 a year. Decide now which kind of owner you'll be.

Slide 8 · Both of you have to live with the weak case

Lesson 0.2 ran a five-year test. It added five years of cash flow to the sale proceeds, then compared that with the $142,688 Maya and Chris put in. The base case came out $27,732 ahead. The weak case came out $27,503 behind.

I want both owners to look at that second number and say out loud whether they could live with it. If the honest answer is no, that's a readiness result, not a failure.

One more personal question: your own stays. Maya and Chris plan about 10 days a year. That's below the IRS Publication 527 threshold, which works out to 21.2 days here, and their CPA confirms the count. Those nights could also have earned up to $3,000, so plan your stays for slower weeks.

Slide 9 · Both reserves leave them $11,644 short

Here's the full stack for Maya and Chris.

On the DSCR quote they spend $155,302 before a single guest checks in. That's cash to close plus furnishing, repairs, inspections, permits, and two months of carrying costs.

Then they hold two reserves. The property reserve is $15,342. Their household spends $8,500 a month on essentials, and they keep six months of it: $51,000.

That's $221,644 against $210,000. They're $11,644 short on the DSCR quote and $8,172 short on the conventional.

A lender may still say yes. After spending, they'd hold $54,698, about 21 months of payments. But they can't fund the house's reserve and their own emergency savings at the same time. This is the gap the down payment check hides.

Close the gap with time first

  1. Save $3,500 a month for 3.3 months
  2. Take the conventional loan, if they qualify
  3. Lower the price
  4. Phase the furnishing

Never shrink the emergency fund to make the table balance.

Slide 10 · Close the gap with time first

Here are the options in the order I'd look at them.

Time first. If Maya and Chris save $3,500 a month, the DSCR gap closes in about 3.3 months. Searching and closing take a while anyway, so time is often the cheapest fix.

Financing second. The conventional quote needs $2,888 less at closing and has a lower payment, if they qualify. That shrinks the gap to $8,172. It doesn't close it. PA.7 compares the two loans.

Price third. PA.1 turns it into a hard cap. Furnishing fourth. OP.6 shows how to phase it without cutting safety.

Two things not to do. Don't shrink the emergency fund to make the table balance. And don't count a HELOC as savings.

  • Risk, personal use, lease path
Slide 11 · Score 13 lines Green, Yellow, or Red

Here's the one-page readiness scorecard. It has 13 lines, grouped here.

Score each line Green, Yellow, or Red. Green clears the line with room. Yellow clears it only with a written condition and a date. Red doesn't clear it now.

Line 1 is the big one: can liquid savings fund the full stack? A gap you can close by saving within six months is Yellow. A bigger gap, or a stack that needs borrowed money, is Red.

If a score rests on a lender figure you don't have in writing yet, mark it "lender to confirm" and add a date. The cut-offs on the full scorecard are my teaching defaults, not lender rules.

Be hard on yourself here. Nobody else sees this page.

Two or more Reds: wait, and rescore in six months.

One Red on time, involvement, risk, or personal use: wait, and fix that before money is at stake.

One Red on cash, emergency savings, or the lease comparison: use the Part One lease path. Alder Street, the course's leased apartment, needed $20,370 on day one.

One Red on lines 3 to 8: get written quotes, pay down debt, or save. Rescore in 60 to 90 days.

Build your own stack and score it

  • Total liquid savings, then set aside emergency savings
  • Fill in the eight-layer stack for one price
  • Score all 13 lines and read your outcome
Next: PA.1, defining your buy box.
Slide 14 · Build your own stack and score it

Three tasks. The first takes minutes.

Total your liquid savings, leaving out retirement accounts and home equity. Then take your essential monthly spending, times the months you'll keep as emergency savings. That money is off the table for the house.

Fill in the eight-layer stack for one realistic price. Where you don't have a number yet, use Maya and Chris's line and mark it as an assumption. Then replace each one: your lender confirms reserves and credit, your CPA the tax questions, your insurer the premium, and the permit office fees and timing.

Score all 13 lines, with evidence. If it says proceed, go to PA.1, where you write your buy box. Lease path means lesson 1.3. Wait means a rescore date on your calendar today.
