3.4 · Red Flags to Avoid in 2026§ 3.4.01 / 19
Slide 1 · Look for the fastest reason to say no before you research a deal

This lesson gives you a screen you can run on any address in about an hour of desk work. At the end, every address gets one of three labels: reject, investigate, or advance.

The screen has two stages. Stage one is six hard stops. Any one of them ends the deal by itself, and no amount of revenue fixes it. Stage two is thirteen commercial and lease red flags. You score each one with fixed point rules, so two people looking at the same address land on the same answer.

By the end you'll have a filled scorecard for one real address. You'll also have a written list of what someone must confirm before you spend more time on it. We'll run Alder Street through the whole thing, and then we'll drop the same apartment into New York City and Los Angeles to see what changes.

Slide 2 · Screen for a reason to reject before you get attached to the deal

Most students do this in the wrong order. They find a nice apartment, run revenue numbers, pitch the landlord, and only then check whether the city allows the plan. By then they're attached. The rules feel like an obstacle instead of an answer.

I want you to flip that. Look for the fastest reason to say no. If you find one, stop and move to the next address. If you can't find one, you've earned the right to spend real time on the deal.

Lesson 3.1 walked through the layers: address, building, zoning district, permit category, and the signed lease. This lesson turns those layers into a quick screen.

You'll notice I'm not handing you a list of cities to avoid. A list like that would be out of date within months, and it would be wrong at plenty of addresses on the day I made it. What you get instead is a set of conditions. If a condition is true at your address, the deal is dead or in trouble, no matter which city it's in.

Slide 3 · H1 to H3: the city can end an arbitrage deal before the landlord says a word

A hard stop is a condition that ends the deal by itself. The first three come from the city.

H1 is the big one. Many cities separate hosted stays, where you live there and rent part of it, from unhosted stays, where the whole unit goes to guests. Arbitrage is almost always unhosted. New York City is the clearest example. The Office of Special Enforcement says you can't rent an entire apartment for fewer than 30 days, and short stays need the host present with no more than two guests.

H2 is the primary-residence rule. Los Angeles limits home-sharing to the host's primary residence, and the host has to live there more than six months a year. If you won't live in the unit, that address is a reject for arbitrage.

H3 is the permit itself. Does the city issue it, is there a cap or a waitlist, and can it move to you? LA says a registration can't be transferred. NYC must deny registration in buildings on its Prohibited Buildings List. A previous tenant's permit is not your permit.

Slide 4 · H4 to H6: the building, the lease, or the insurer can end the deal too

The last three hard stops come from the building, the lease, and the insurer.

H4 is an HOA, condo, or co-op ban. The city might allow your use and the building can still block it. Airbnb itself tells hosts to check HOA and co-op rules before they start. If the governing documents ban rentals under a set number of days, stop there.

H5 is the one people talk themselves past. A lease that bans subletting, bans transient use, or just says nothing about short stays is not permission. A friendly text from the landlord isn't permission either. If the landlord won't sign a written short-term-rental addendum, the answer is reject. Some cities tie this to the permit too. NYC makes applicants certify the lease doesn't prohibit short-term renting, and Los Angeles requires written landlord approval for renters.

H6 is insurance. If nobody will write an STR liability policy for this unit and this use, you can't meet any sensible lease. Lesson 2.4 covers insurance in detail.

Slide 5 · The same apartment fails in both New York City and Los Angeles, for different reasons

Here's why friendliness rankings fail. Take one apartment and drop it into two big travel markets.

In New York City, an operator renting the whole unit for three-night stays hits H1 straight away. Short stays are allowed only when the host is present with up to two guests. Whole-unit stays under 30 days are out. One detail catches people: the OSE says New York State counts the check-in date as day zero, so a 29-night rental is still short-term.

In Los Angeles, the same operator hits H2 and H3. Home-sharing is limited to the host's primary residence, and the registration doesn't transfer. Then there's a second layer. The Housing Department says home-sharing isn't allowed in units under the Rent Stabilization Ordinance. That covers most multi-family units built before October 1, 1978. So the age of the building can end the deal before you even get to the residence rule.

These rules can change. I've dated them to 25 September 2026. Recheck the official page, and watch Sam's 3.2 for the live search process.

3.4 · Red Flags to Avoid in 2026§ 3.4.46 / 19
Slide 6 · Eight commercial flags ask whether the deal survives an ordinary slow stretch

Once an address clears the hard stops, the question changes. Does the deal survive an ordinary slow stretch? Lesson 1.3 built the math. Here I turn that math into eight flags.

C1 and C2 are about cushion. Break-even occupancy is where revenue exactly covers every cost, rent included. Compare it with your base case. Then run a stress case with lower rates and lower occupancy and see if it bleeds every month.

C3 is where your numbers come from. Use active comps with a full year of data, not the best listing in the neighborhood. Sam's 4.2 and 4.3 show you how to pull them.

C4 is concentration. If one stadium or one employer drives your bookings, your occupancy moves when they move.

C5, C6, and C7 are about money you've committed: rent you owe regardless, setup cost you've sunk, and the reserve that keeps one bad month from becoming a default. C8 is new supply, and Sam's 4.2 covers the oversupply screen.

No single flag here kills a deal. Together they tell you how fragile it is.

3.4 · Red Flags to Avoid in 2026§ 3.4.57 / 19
Slide 7 · Five lease flags decide whether you get to keep the business you build

The lease decides whether you get to keep what you build. These are the five terms I'd look for first.

L1 is termination. If the landlord can end the lease without cause on short notice, your setup money is at risk every month. You want termination only for a defined breach, with written notice and a chance to fix it. That chance to fix it is called a cure period.

L2 is guest rights. The lease or addendum should say guests are allowed, how many, how they get in, and which building areas they can use. Silence invites a fight later.

L3 is insurance. Some leases demand a standard renter's policy that excludes business use. Others want the landlord named on a policy you haven't even quoted. If the lease and your policy don't match, flag it.

L4 is anything verbal. If you're counting on it, it has to be on paper.

L5 ties back to the math. If your stress-case payback runs past the lease term, you could hand back the keys before you've earned your setup cost back.

Slide 8 · Commercial rows score on fixed thresholds, starting with a 15-point cushion

Here are the point rules for the commercial side. The bands are my teaching defaults, an assumption, not industry rules. Tighten them if the unit depends on one employer or one event. The reason they're fixed is simple. If you and a partner score the same address, you should get the same answer. Nobody gets to round up because they like the apartment.

A few of these need a word. C1 is measured in percentage points. If your base case is 70% and break-even is 60%, the cushion is 10 points, which scores a 1. C2 looks at the stress case per month. Anything below zero is a 2.

C3 and C8 have a rule I want you to keep: if you haven't done the work yet, it's a 1, not a 0. Comps not pulled scores 1. Supply not checked scores 1. You score the numbers you have, not the ones you hope for.

C5 is annual rent divided by base-case gross. Under 35% scores zero. Over 45% scores 2.

C7 counts months of rent plus utilities you can cover from cash after setup. Two months or more scores zero.

Slide 9 · Lease rows can force investigate on their own: any 2 blocks an advance

The lease rows use the same 0, 1, 2 scale, with one extra rule. Any lease row scored 2 means the label can't be advance, even if the total is low. A lease that lets the landlord walk away without cause is a problem no revenue number fixes.

Now the labels. Zero to four points with no 2 in a lease row is advance. That sends you to the landlord pitch in lesson 5.7 and full underwriting. Five to nine points, or any lease row at 2, is investigate. Ten or more is reject, even when every hard stop passed. Those cut-offs are my teaching defaults too, not industry rules.

And remember stage one sits above all of this. Any hard stop answered yes is reject before you score a single row. Any hard stop still unknown caps you at investigate.

Treat investigate as a to-do list, not a maybe. Every row scored 1 or 2 gets an owner and a date. That's what we'll build for Alder Street next.

The scorecard is desk work. Some answers you can't give yourself, and I want you to know exactly who gives them.

The local licensing or planning office confirms the stage-one city questions, in writing. Is unhosted use allowed at this exact address? Does a primary-residence test apply? Can a non-owner operator get the permit?

The HOA, condo board, or building manager confirms whether the governing documents restrict short stays.

An attorney licensed in the property's state confirms whether the lease and addendum give you explicit permission. They also tell you whether the termination and cure terms are enforceable as written. That's the L1 fix from Alder Street.

Your insurance broker confirms the policy covers this use at this address and matches what the lease requires.

And for C3 and C8, Sam's 4.2 and 4.3 methods, or your own market data, confirm the revenue and supply numbers. Until then, those rows stay at 1.
