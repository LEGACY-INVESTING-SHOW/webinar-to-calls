The title of this lesson makes a claim: ownership wins. Sometimes it does. Sometimes it loses badly. So I'm going to treat that title as a question. You answer it for one property, one loan, and one household.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to split an ownership return into four streams. You'll know which of the four you can actually spend this year. 0.2 · Why Ownership Wins§ 0.2.12 / 19
Slide 2 · An ownership return has four streams, and only one lands in your bank account

The usual pitch for owning lists three benefits: cash flow, tax savings, and equity. I split equity into two parts, because they behave differently. That gives four possible streams.

Stream one is operating cash flow. It's what's left after operating costs and the loan payment.

Stream two is principal paydown. It's the part of each loan payment that reduces what you owe.

Stream three is change in value. It's what the house is worth later compared with what you paid.

Stream four is tax timing. That's deductions, mainly depreciation, that may lower your tax now and may be partly taken back when you sell.

Every stream has a cost or a risk attached. And only one of them lands in your bank account in a normal month. The other three are either locked inside the house or depend on facts you won't know for years.

So when someone adds all four and calls the total "income", stop them. It isn't income.

Stream one is the only stream you can spend this year. It's the money left after the house pays for itself.

Here's the Cedar Ridge base case. 212 booked nights at $300, plus 61 cleaning fees of $175, gives $74,275 of gross booking revenue. Operating costs are $45,066. That covers platform fees, the cleaner, utilities, property tax, insurance, and two reserves: 5% for repairs and 5% for capital items. So net operating income is $29,209.

Then the loan. Principal and interest on the DSCR quote is $23,637 a year. That leaves $5,572, or about $464 a month.

That's real money, if the base case happens. Notice what it already pays for. If you drop those reserves to make the number look bigger, you're borrowing from the next roof.

Now the cost side. Against $142,688 of cash in, $5,572 is a 3.9% cash-on-cash return. And in the weak case, the same house loses $5,475 a year after debt service. Small in a good year. Negative in a bad one.

0.2 · Why Ownership Wins§ 0.2.34 / 19
Slide 4 · Principal paydown of $2,795 is equity you already paid for and can't spend

Stream two is principal paydown. Each month, part of the loan payment reduces the balance. Economically, that part isn't an expense. It turns into equity.

On the Cedar Ridge DSCR quote, the balance falls by $2,795 in year one. That grows a little each year.

Here's the catch. You already paid that $2,795 out of your cash flow. It's inside the $23,637 of debt service. You can't spend it. You only get it back when you sell or refinance.

The example file has a refinance at the end of year two. It frees $15,725 after costs. That's how locked equity turns into cash. But it comes with a new, larger loan payment. Base-case cash flow drops from $5,572 to $4,752 a year after that refinance.

And paydown depends on the house holding its value. If the value falls, you can pay the loan down every month and still end up with less equity than you started with. So this stream is real, but it's slow, and you can't reach it without a cost.

Stream three is change in value. People talk about it most and control it least.

For teaching, the example assumes Cedar Ridge grows 3% a year. In year one that's 3% of $385,000, or $11,550. That's an assumption. Values can fall. Lake markets can see new supply, rule changes, or a slow year.

Three rules for this stream. First, never add modeled appreciation to spendable monthly income. $11,550 of assumed growth isn't $960 a month you can use. Stream four is tax timing. Depreciation is a paper write-off for wear on the building. You don't write a check for it, but it can reduce taxable income. Whether it reduces your tax bill depends on facts your CPA confirms.

Stream four · The inputs

Depreciation starts when the house is ready for guests, and your CPA sets the schedule

  • You depreciate the building, never the land (IRS Pub 527). The land split is your first input.
  • Depreciation starts when the house is ready and available for rent. Ready in July, first rented in September: it starts in July.
  • A late-year launch gives a small first-year deduction on the building.
  • Per full year: $11,375 at 27.5 years, or $8,021 at 39 years. Short average stays can change the classification.
  • 100% first-year (bonus) depreciation is permanent for eligible property acquired after January 19, 2025 (IRS, Notice 2026-11).
  • Bonus applies to eligible property, not to land and not to the whole building.
0.2 · Why Ownership Wins§ 0.2.57 / 19
Slide 7 · Depreciation starts when the house is ready for guests, and your CPA sets the schedule

Each input here is a question for your CPA, not a number to assume.

Basis and land come first. IRS Publication 527 says you can never depreciate land. So how much of the price belongs to the land matters a lot.

Then the placed-in-service date. Depreciation starts when the property is ready and available for rent, not when the first guest arrives. Publication 527 gives an example: a house ready in July and first rented in September starts depreciating in July. For Cedar Ridge, the month the house is furnished, safe, and listed matters. A late-year launch means a small first-year deduction on the building.

Then the recovery period. The example shows the building on two schedules: $11,375 a year at 27.5 years, or $8,021 at 39 years. Short average stays can change how the property is classified. Your CPA decides which applies.

Finally, bonus depreciation. IRS guidance describes a permanent 100% first-year deduction for eligible property acquired after January 19, 2025. It covers eligible property. It doesn't cover land, and it doesn't cover the whole building.

0.2 · Why Ownership Wins§ 0.2.58 / 19
Slide 8 · Three tax tests decide whether the loss can offset your other income this year

Here's what has to be true before that deduction lowers your tax this year.

IRS Publication 925 covers passive activity rules. Losses from passive activities generally offset only other passive activity income.

Test one. An activity isn't a "rental activity" under these rules if the average period of customer use is 7 days or less. You divide total rental days by the number of rentals. Cedar Ridge is 212 nights over 61 stays, 3.5 nights. But that exception doesn't make the loss non-passive by itself. It means the activity gets tested like a business.

Test two. The loss is non-passive only if you materially participate. Three tests matter most here: more than 500 hours, or substantially all the participation, or more than 100 hours and at least as much as any other individual.

Test three is personal use, from Publication 527. The house counts as a home if personal use exceeds the greater of 14 days or 10% of fair-rental days. At 212 rented nights, that's 21.2 days. Maya and Chris plan 10. More personal use would change the picture.

0.2 · Why Ownership Wins§ 0.2.59 / 19
Slide 9 · A busy cleaner can out-log you, and the $25,000 allowance won't help at $185,000

Two traps that catch high-earning households.

First, the "more than 100 hours and not less than any other individual" test. That means other people's hours matter, owner or not. A cleaner who turns Cedar Ridge over 61 times a year may log more hours than Maya and Chris. If they hire a full-service co-host, the co-host's hours may beat theirs by a wide margin. Keep a real log of your hours. Your CPA will ask.

Second, the $25,000 special allowance. Publication 925 describes it for rental real estate with active participation. It's reduced by 50% of modified adjusted gross income above $100,000, and it's gone at $150,000. Maya and Chris have $185,000 of combined W-2 income. If their modified AGI is near that, the allowance gives them nothing, even if it applied to this activity at all.

And remember why I call this stream tax timing. Depreciation lowers your basis. When you sell, some of the deduction can come back as taxable gain. Lesson OP.12 treats the tax on a sale as a CPA input.

Slide 11 · Owning takes $98,188 more cash, and that cash buys streams two to four

Here's where the two paths split.

Owning needs $98,188 more cash: $142,688 against $44,500. That extra cash is the price of streams two, three, and four. The lease cash in is $32,000 of furnishing, a $2,900 deposit, $2,900 of first month's rent, and a two-month reserve of $6,700.

In the weak case, the lease loses more, $6,856 against $5,475, because rent is fixed and higher than the loan payment. But the owner also carries capital repairs, and in a bad year the reserve may not cover them all.

The owner builds equity and may get tax timing. The tenant gets neither. The tenant's exposure ends with the lease. The owner's lasts until a sale.

So the lease path isn't a fantasy. For some households, $44,500 in a lease beats $142,688 in a house. For others, the long hold and the equity matter more.

One caveat. In many drive-to lake markets, owners of three-bedroom houses may not lease them for short-term rental at all. Whether a real lease exists is a market question for Sam's PA.2.

0.2 · Why Ownership Wins§ 0.2.916 / 19
Slide 16 · Ownership wins for your deal only if all three of these tests pass

Here's the decision rule. Ownership wins for this deal only if all three are true.

One. The Part B base case is ahead after five years. If owning doesn't come out ahead when things go as planned, it isn't going to win when they don't.

Two. The weak case is behind by no more than you could accept losing. Put a real number on that before you look at listings. For Cedar Ridge, the weak case with 3% growth ends $27,503 behind. Some households can live with that. Some can't. Neither answer is wrong.

Three. In Part C, the extra cash for owning buys something you actually want. That could be a long hold, personal use, or equity. If a lease gets you the same cash flow for a third of the money, you should be able to say why you're buying anyway.

If any one fails, write down which one. Don't argue with it. Bring it to lesson 0.3, where we check whether your household can fund the full stack and get through a bad first year.

A local agent or an appraiser can help with value inputs for the five-year test. But any growth rate you use is your assumption. Don't let anyone hand you a growth rate as if it were a fact.

And a cost segregation provider, with your CPA, tells you whether a study even makes sense for this property. Do that before you count any accelerated depreciation. Sam's PA.5 covers the study, and resource SR.3 has an estimator you can use to prepare for that conversation.
