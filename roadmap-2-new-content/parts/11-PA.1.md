### PA.1.0 Learning outcome

By the end of this lesson you have a written, one-page buy box. It ranks your goals, states your constraints, names your hard requirements, and describes the property you're looking for in terms someone else can search with. It ends with a pass rule: a sentence that tells you when to walk away from a listing without debate. Sam's PA.2 and PA.3 lessons use this page to choose a market and build your property pipeline.

### PA.1.1 What a buy box is for

A buy box is a search specification. It says what you'll buy, where, at what price, and under what conditions. Anything outside the box, you don't look at twice.

It exists to protect you from two things.

The first is drift. You start looking for a three-bedroom house at $380,000. A month later you're touring a $450,000 house with a pool, because the photos were good and the agent said it "books great". Nobody decided to raise the budget by $70,000. It just happened, one listing at a time.

The second is wasted work. Every property you seriously consider costs hours: reading rules, pulling comparable listings, underwriting, calling lenders. Sam's PA.3 lesson builds a pipeline of 5 to 10 qualified properties. Without a buy box, "qualified" means nothing, and the pipeline fills with houses you should never have looked at.

A good buy box is short and specific. It's written before you see listings. And it's hard to change: if you want to change it, you rewrite it on purpose, with numbers.

### PA.1.2 Start with a goal hierarchy

Before property details, rank your goals. People usually want several things from an STR purchase, and they conflict. A ranking tells you which one wins when they do.

The common goals:

- **Cash flow.** Money left each month after all costs and the loan.
- **Long-term ownership.** Holding for many years and building equity through loan paydown and possible value change.
- **Personal use.** Using the house yourself some days each year.
- **Tax planning.** Depreciation and possible use of losses, which depend on facts your CPA confirms (lesson 0.2).
- **Portfolio growth.** Using this property as a step toward a second one.
- **Desired involvement.** How many hours you want to put in, and which jobs you want to hand off.

Here's how they conflict in practice:

- Personal use pulls against cash flow. Your own peak-season week is a week you didn't rent.
- Tax planning can pull against everything else. A deal bought "for the depreciation" can lose money every month. The deduction may also be unusable, depending on your household facts.
- Low involvement pulls against cash flow. In 0.2, a full-service co-host at 18% turned Cedar Ridge from $5,572 a year of cash flow to -$7,798.
- Portfolio growth pulls against safety. Stretching to buy now so you can buy again sooner leaves less reserve for this house.

Rank your goals from 1 to 6. Only the top two or three should shape the search. The rest are tie-breakers. If both owners rank differently, settle it now. A disagreement you skip now tends to come back at the offer.

### PA.1.3 Write down your constraints

Constraints are limits you can't wish away. They come mostly from lesson 0.3.

**Money.** Two caps. First, the maximum cash to close: down payment plus closing costs and points. Second, the maximum total cash in: cash to close plus furnishing. Both must leave your property reserve and personal emergency savings untouched. If your 0.3 scorecard carried a funding condition, write it here too.

**Financing path.** Which loan types you'll pursue (DSCR, conventional investment, or both) and the down payment you're planning around. The lender makes the eligibility call later, in PA.6 and PA.7. For now, write the path and the assumed terms.

**Distance.** How far the house can be from you. This isn't only about convenience. Distance decides whether you can handle a problem yourself or must pay someone. Write it in driving hours.

**Time.** The hours per week you'll actually give it, from 0.3.

**Minimum reserves.** The property reserve and personal emergency savings you won't spend below. These are floors, not targets.

### PA.1.4 Describe the property

Now the property itself. Keep each line concrete enough that Sam's PA.2 and PA.3 filters can apply it.

**Market type.** Drive-to leisure, urban, event-driven, business travel. Name the type and the demand driver. Sam's PA.2 picks the actual market.

**Property type.** Detached single-family, townhouse, condo, small multi-unit. This matters for legality (condo and HOA rules), for lending (some lenders restrict condos or certain property types), and for insurance.

**Location inside the market.** Rules can change street by street. Zoning districts, HOA boundaries, and permit caps can all differ within one town. Write where you'll look ("within 10 minutes of the lake") and where you won't.

**Price range.** A floor and a ceiling. The ceiling comes from your money constraints and your downside rule, not from what the market charges. Section PA.1.7 shows how to calculate it.

**Guest capacity.** Bedrooms, bathrooms, and how many guests it sleeps. More capacity can mean higher ADR, and also more wear, more cleaning time, and more noise risk. Local rules may cap occupancy too.

**Amenities.** Separate what guests in this market expect (parking, reliable internet, a working kitchen) from what adds complexity (pools, hot tubs, docks, boats). Every amenity with water or height carries insurance and safety questions. Your insurer's quote will tell you which ones cost extra or aren't covered.

**Operating complexity.** Septic and wells, long private roads, snow access, older systems, big lots. None of these is a deal-breaker by itself. Each adds cost, inspection work, or response time. Decide how much of it you'll accept.

### PA.1.5 Hard requirements

Hard requirements are the lines a property must clear, every one, before you spend real time on it. They aren't preferences. If a property fails one, you pass.

Five belong in almost every STR buy box:

1. **Legal STR use at the exact address.** Unhosted short-term rental use, for a non-owner-occupied house, must be allowed at that address. Any HOA or deed restrictions must allow it too. Airbnb itself tells hosts to check local laws and HOA rules before hosting ([Airbnb Help Center, article 1397](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/1397)). Use the SR.1 checklist and record the official source.
2. **Acceptable insurance.** A written quote from an insurer willing to cover STR use of this house, at a premium your underwriting can carry. Platform protection is not a substitute; PA.12 covers that.
3. **Lender fit.** The property type, location, and projected income fit a lender's written guidelines for the loan you plan to use.
4. **Maximum cash to close and total cash in.** From PA.1.3. No exceptions for "a great house".
5. **Minimum downside performance.** A number for how badly the weak case may perform at your actual loan quote. For example, set a floor on weak-case annual cash flow after debt service. Then add a minimum base-case ratio of net operating income to principal and interest.

Add a sixth if 0.3 surfaced it. For Maya and Chris, it's "the deal must work with self-management".

### PA.1.6 The pass rule

The pass rule is one or two sentences you agree on in advance. It tells you what to do when a listing doesn't fit.

A good pass rule has three parts:

- **The trigger.** Any failed hard requirement. Or a price above the ceiling. Or a preference score below a stated level.
- **The action.** Pass, and log why. Don't "keep an eye on it" unless the price drops below the ceiling.
- **The change process.** The buy box changes only on purpose: in writing, with the new numbers, signed by every owner. Never during a showing.

Why log the pass? Because after 20 passes you'll see a pattern. Maybe every house in your price range fails the insurance quote. That's information for Sam's PA.2 market choice, not a reason to lower your standards.

### PA.1.7 Worked example: Maya and Chris's buy box, tested on Cedar Ridge

Maya and Chris finished 0.3 with "proceed to PA.1, with conditions". Here's how they turn their goals and numbers into a buy box. Canonical figures come from the course example file. New figures are marked (assumption).

**Their goal hierarchy**

| Rank | Goal | How it shapes the search |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Protect the household | Emergency fund stays at $51,000; property reserve stays at 6 months PITIA |
| 2 | Long-term ownership, 10+ year hold (assumption) | Favor durable houses and stable rules over a short-term price opportunity |
| 3 | Positive base-case cash flow while self-managing | Base case must be positive after debt service and reserves |
| 4 | Personal use, about 10 days, off-peak | Drive time matters; personal days go in slow weeks |
| 5 | Tax planning | Tie-breaker only; no deal is bought for depreciation |
| 6 | Portfolio growth | Not before the OP.10 review and OP.11 decision on property two |

Desired involvement: self-manage with a local cleaner and an on-call handyman, about 2.7 hours a week on average (assumption, from 0.3).

**Their constraints**

- Maximum cash to close: $115,000 (assumption).
- Maximum total cash in, including $32,000 of furnishing: $145,000 (assumption).
- Funding condition from 0.3: no offer until liquid savings cover the full stack.
- Financing: DSCR at the hypothetical canonical quote (7.25%, 1 point, 25% down), with a conventional quote requested for comparison.
- Distance: 2.5 hours' drive or less from home (assumption: they live in the metro that Cedar Ridge's market serves).
- Reserves: 6 months PITIA as property reserve (assumption), plus $51,000 personal emergency savings (assumption).

**Turning the money constraints into a price ceiling.** On the DSCR terms, cash to close is 25% down plus 3% closing costs. Add 1 point on a loan of 75% of price. So cash to close is 0.25 + 0.03 + 0.0075 = 28.75% of the price.

- Cash-to-close cap: $115,000 / 0.2875 = $400,000.
- Total-cash-in cap: ($145,000 - $32,000) / 0.2875 = $393,043.

The tighter cap wins. Their ceiling is **$393,000 contract price** on these terms.

**The downside rule.** Maya and Chris set two numbers (assumption):

- Weak-case annual cash flow after principal and interest: no worse than -$6,000.
- Base-case NOI divided by annual principal and interest: at least 1.20.

These are their own rules, not a lender's ratio. PA.6 covers how lenders define coverage, and the canonical file shows three different ratios for the same house.

**The buy box itself**

| Field | Maya and Chris |
|---|---|
| Market type | Drive-to lake leisure market; Sam's PA.2 selects the market |
| Location | Within 2.5 hours of home; within a 10-minute drive of lake access (assumption) |
| Property type | Detached single-family; no HOA, or HOA with written STR permission; no condos |
| Size | 3 to 4 bedrooms, 2+ baths, sleeps 6 to 8 |
| Price range | $325,000 (assumption) to $393,000 contract price |
| Financing | DSCR 25% down; conventional quote for comparison |
| Amenities wanted | Parking for 3 cars, reliable internet, outdoor space, year-round road access |
| Complexity limits | No pool; hot tub only if the insurer quotes it; septic and well accepted with inspection and water test; punch list under $4,000 |
| Hard requirements | (1) Legal unhosted STR use at the address, and HOA if any; (2) written STR insurance quote at or under $3,500 a year (assumption); (3) lender fit in writing; (4) cash to close at or under $115,000 and total cash in at or under $145,000; (5) weak case no worse than -$6,000 a year, base NOI/P&I at least 1.20; (6) works with self-management and a named local cleaner plus backup |
| Pass rule | Pass on any listing that fails a hard requirement, or whose price is above $393,000 unless the seller has shown they'll accept a price at or under it. Log every pass with the reason. The box changes only in writing, signed by both. |

**Test 1: Cedar Ridge at the contract price, $385,000.**

| Check | Result | Pass? |
|---|---|---|
| Cash to close | $110,688, under $115,000 | Yes |
| Total cash in | $142,688, under $145,000 | Yes |
| Weak-case cash flow | -$5,475, better than -$6,000 | Yes |
| Base NOI / annual P&I | $29,209 / $23,637 = 1.24 | Yes |
| Insurance | $3,200 a year in the canonical model; written quote needed | Pending |
| Legal STR use | Address-level check needed (PA.2, SR.1) | Pending |
| Property fit | 3 bed, 2 bath, sleeps 8, no HOA, drive-to lake market | Yes |

At $385,000, Cedar Ridge fits the box, subject to the two pending checks.

**Test 2: Cedar Ridge at the asking price, $399,000.** Same revenue and operating costs as the canonical case. One change: property tax scales with price at 1% (assumption, which matches the canonical $3,850 on $385,000). All figures below are assumptions.

| Check | Result | Pass? |
|---|---|---|
| Cash to close | $114,713 | Yes, barely |
| Total cash in | $146,713, over $145,000 | **No** |
| Weak-case cash flow | -$6,475, worse than -$6,000 | **No** |
| Base NOI / annual P&I | 1.19, under 1.20 | **No** |
| Base-case cash flow | $4,572 | (for reference) |

At asking price, the same house fails three hard requirements. So the $14,000 negotiation from $399,000 to $385,000 is what makes Cedar Ridge a deal for Maya and Chris. If the seller had refused to go below $399,000, their pass rule says walk. PA.8 covers setting that walk-away price.

The downside rule also gives a price ceiling on its own. For a house with Cedar Ridge's weak-case revenue, the weak case reaches -$6,000 at about $392,000 (assumption, same property-tax scaling). That sits right next to the $393,000 cash ceiling, which is a good sign that the two rules agree.

**Test 3: a pass example.** A four-bedroom house with a pool, listed at $435,000 in the same market (hypothetical). On their DSCR terms, cash to close would be $125,063 and total cash in $157,063. It fails the cash caps before anyone looks at revenue. It also has a pool, which is outside their complexity limit. Pass, logged as "over cash cap; pool". No underwriting needed.

That's the value of the box. The $435,000 house never takes an evening of their time.

### PA.1.9 Who confirms what

**Confirm with:** the city or county licensing office that unhosted STR use is allowed at each address that passes your other filters. Use the SR.1 checklist. If the answer is unclear, an STR attorney.

**Confirm with:** your insurance agent the premium and exclusions for STR use on each finalist, before the diligence window.

**Confirm with:** your lender that the property type, location, and planned use fit their loan, and the terms you've used in section C.

**Confirm with:** your CPA before tax planning is ranked above 4 in your goal list, so the ranking reflects your household's actual situation.
