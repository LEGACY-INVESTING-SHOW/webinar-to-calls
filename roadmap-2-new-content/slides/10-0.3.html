Most buyers start with one number: the down payment. For Cedar Ridge, 25% of $385,000 is $96,250. Maya and Chris have $210,000 in liquid savings outside retirement accounts. On that math alone, they could make the down payment twice.

That math leaves out most of the bill.

An owned short-term rental needs money for closing, furnishing, repairs, and permits. It needs money for the months before the first guest arrives. It needs reserves, for the lender and for you. And your own household has to stay safe if the house has a bad year.

Slide 3 · The cash stack has eight layers: six you spend and two you must still hold

Think of the cash requirement as a stack. Each layer sits on the one below it. You need all of them funded. If you don't, you're counting on the house to perform from day one.

Layers 1 to 6 are money you'll spend. The down payment, closing costs and points, furnishing, the repair and safety punch list, permits and inspections, and the carrying costs before the first booking. That last one catches people. The loan payment starts whether or not a guest has booked.

Layers 7 and 8 are money you must still have after spending all that. Layer 7 is the property reserve. The lender may require it to make the loan, and after closing it becomes your cushion for the house. Layer 8 is your household's own emergency savings.

The most common failure is merging 7 and 8. A buyer counts the same dollars twice, once for the house and once for the family. Then the water heater fails in the same month a paycheck is late. And there's one pile of cash for two problems.

Borrowed or conditional

Slide 4 · Count only liquid savings, because a HELOC is borrowed money secured by your home

Money for the stack can come from several places. Each one has limits, and your lender may treat them differently.

Liquid savings are the cleanest. That's checking, savings, and taxable brokerage. Lenders usually want to see where it came from and how long it's been there.

Retirement accounts are different. Maya and Chris's $210,000 leaves retirement out. Some lenders may count part of a retirement balance toward reserves. But withdrawals can carry taxes and penalties. That's a lender and CPA question, not spendable cash.

Home equity looks tempting. Their home is worth $520,000 with a $260,000 mortgage, so they have $260,000 of equity on paper. A HELOC lets them borrow against it. The CFPB notes that HELOCs usually carry a variable rate, and your home is the collateral. If you can't repay, you could lose your home. A HELOC is borrowed money, not savings, and it adds a payment. OP.11 covers HELOCs for scaling.

So in this lesson, we count only liquid savings. If the stack only works with borrowed money, write that down as a finding.

Reserves

The lender's reserve rule is a floor to get the loan, not a plan for a bad season

  • Fannie Mae measures reserves in months of the new loan's PITIA (Selling Guide B3-4.1-01).
  • No minimum for a one-unit principal residence. Second homes and investment properties do have one.
  • Desktop Underwriter sets reserves for DU loans. The Eligibility Matrix sets them for manual loans.
  • More financed properties add 2%, 4%, or 6% of the other balances. For Maya and Chris that's likely zero.
  • DSCR lenders set their own rule. Get each lender's number in writing (PA.6, PA.7).

0.3 · Capital & Readiness Checklist§ 0.3.45 / 20
Slide 5 · The lender's reserve rule is a floor to get the loan, not a plan for a bad season

Lenders measure reserves in months of the new loan's payment. That payment is principal, interest, taxes, insurance, and association dues. People shorten it to PITIA.

Fannie Mae's Selling Guide, section B3-4.1-01, lays out the rules for loans it buys. There's no minimum for a one-unit principal residence. Second homes and investment properties do have requirements. For loans run through Fannie Mae's automated system, Desktop Underwriter, that system sets the number. For manually underwritten loans, it's in the Eligibility Matrix.

If you have other financed properties, more reserves apply, as a percentage of those balances. The add-on excludes the house you're buying and your principal residence. Maya and Chris would have two financed properties, their home and Cedar Ridge. So the add-on would likely be zero. Their lender confirms it.

The DSCR quote isn't a Fannie Mae loan. Each DSCR lender sets its own rule. For teaching I assume 6 months: $15,342 on the DSCR quote, $14,904 on the conventional.

After closing, the lender stops watching. That reserve becomes your cushion. The lender's number is a floor, not a plan.

Three questions shape whether a lender will lend, before you even pick a house.

Credit first. Maya and Chris have scores around 740. Each lender sets its own minimum and prices its rate by score. Kiavi's DSCR guide, for example, says it looks at credit score and down payment, not only the property's coverage. That's one lender, not a market standard.

Slide 7 · Each month between closing and the first guest costs $3,257 with nothing coming in

Two things go wrong often enough that you should budget for both. The first is a delayed opening.

Permits take longer than expected. Furniture arrives late. A contractor misses a week. Every month between closing and the first guest costs the full carrying cost, with no revenue.

For Cedar Ridge on the DSCR quote, that's $3,257 a month. It's the $2,557 PITIA, plus the example's $450 of utilities, $100 of software, and $150 for the handyman retainer.

The base stack already includes two months of carrying costs as an assumption. That's $6,514. Now suppose you get three more months of delay on top of that. That's another $9,771.

Where does that money come from? Not from the house. The house isn't earning yet. It comes from the property reserve, and if that's not enough, from your household.

When you build your own stack, don't use the fastest timeline anyone's quoted you. Ask the permit office for real processing times. Ask the furniture vendor for realistic delivery dates.

The second thing is a weak first season. A new listing has no reviews. It may book below its long-run potential for a while.

The example's weak case is $265 ADR at 50% occupancy. On the DSCR quote it loses $5,475 a year, about $456 a month. On its own, the $15,342 reserve covers about 2.8 years of that, if nothing else goes wrong.

That's the pairing I want you to plan for. It's the one that forces owners to sell at bad times, because the cushion runs out right when the house is weakest.

This is also why the lender's reserve rule isn't a plan. The lender got its six months. Your plan has to survive this chart. Maya and Chris will set their own target, and you'll see it on the scorecard.

Slide 9 · Self-managing Cedar Ridge takes about 140 hours a year, and the cleaner may log more

Cash isn't the only resource. Owning also takes time, and a stomach for ups and downs.

In the base case, Maya and Chris self-manage with a local cleaner and an on-call handyman. Assume 1.5 hours per stay on messages, scheduling, and turnover checks. With 61 stays, that's 91.5 hours. Add 4 hours a month on bookkeeping, pricing, and vendor calls. That's 48 more. Together, about 140 hours a year, or 2.7 hours a week. Some weeks take much more: a lockout at 11 p.m., a broken water heater on a Saturday.

That hour count matters for taxes too. As 0.2 explained, whether losses are usable can depend on material participation. Publication 925's tests compare your hours with other people's, like the cleaner's. If the cleaner spends 4 hours on each of 61 turnovers, that's 244 hours. That's more than the owners' 140. Your CPA confirms how your hours count.

If you don't want to spend those hours, the alternative is a co-host. In 0.2, an 18% co-host sank the base case to minus $7,798. Decide now which kind of owner you'll be.

0.3 · Capital & Readiness Checklist§ 0.3.710 / 20
Slide 10 · Both owners should say out loud whether they could live with being $27,503 behind

Two more resources: your own calendar, and your nerves.

Personal use first. Maya and Chris plan about 10 personal days a year. IRS Publication 527 treats a dwelling as used as a home if personal use exceeds the greater of 14 days or 10% of fair-rental days. It also requires dividing expenses by days. At 212 rented nights, the threshold is 21.2 days, so 10 days is below it.

There's also a revenue cost. If those 10 nights would have booked at the $300 base rate, they're worth up to $3,000 of nightly revenue. So plan your own stays for slower weeks.

Now risk tolerance. In 0.2, the five-year test showed Cedar Ridge $27,732 ahead before tax in the base case. In the weak case it was $27,503 behind. I want both owners to look at that second number and say out loud whether they could live with it.

If the honest answer is no, that's a readiness result. It isn't a failure. It's the scorecard doing its job before any money is at risk.

Slide 18 · Your lender, CPA, insurer, and permit office each confirm a line of your stack

Here's who confirms what for your stack.

Each lender, in writing: the reserve months and how they calculate them, the minimum credit score, and the down payment. Also which sources of funds they accept, like HELOC money, gifts, or retirement assets. And how they count rental income. Ask every lender the same questions so you can compare the answers side by side.

Your CPA: the tax effect of any retirement withdrawal, how your hours count for material participation, and how personal-use days are treated.

Your insurance agent: the premium and when it's due. Depending on timing, it lands in closing costs or in launch carrying costs. Either way, it needs to be a real number.

The local permit office: the fees and the processing time. That's what tells you whether two months of launch carrying costs is realistic, or whether you need to plan for more.

Every one of these turns an assumption on your stack into a confirmed number. The more lines you confirm, the more you can trust your scorecard.
