### 1.3.0 Learning outcome

By the end of this lesson you can take one specific apartment and decide whether a lease on it has room for profit. You will compute its contribution per stay, its break-even occupancy, a stress case, and the months it takes to earn back your setup cash. You will then run it through three gates (address-level permission, written landlord permission, conservative math) and record a sign, investigate, or walk decision.

### 1.3.1 The question you're really asking

People ask me whether arbitrage still works in 2026. By arbitrage I mean leasing an apartment from a landlord, with their written permission, and renting it to short-stay guests on platforms like Airbnb. You keep what's left after rent and costs.

My short answer: yes, it still works at some addresses and not at others. The only way to know is to test one lease at a time. That's what this lesson teaches.

The question has no useful answer at the national level. A lease is a promise to pay a fixed rent at one address for a fixed term. So the only question that matters is about this unit. At this rent, in this building, under this city's rules, does it earn more than it costs in an ordinary bad month?

National numbers can look strong while your unit loses money. Airbnb's [Q2 2026 results](https://news.airbnb.com/airbnb-q2-2026-financial-results/) reported revenue up 17% to $3.6 billion. Its booking count, which Airbnb calls "nights and seats booked", rose 10% year over year. Gross booking value, the total dollar value of bookings on the platform, was $27.2 billion, up 16%. That tells you travelers are still booking on the platform. It says nothing about the rent your landlord wants, the listings opening in your building, or whether your city lets you host at all.

AirDNA, a company that sells short-term rental market data, points the other way at the listing level. As summarized on its site, its [2026 outlook](https://www.airdna.co/outlook-report) expected supply to grow slightly faster than demand in 2026. It projected occupancy down about 1% (as AirDNA reports it) and average nightly rate up about 1.5%. Revenue per available night was projected up about half a percent. Its [midyear update](https://www.airdna.co/blog/u.s.-mid-year-outlook-report-2026) is titled "A Better Year to Own Than to Buy" and says a tougher economy favored existing hosts. For a new operator, that means newcomers start at a disadvantage against listings that already have reviews.

Read both as weather reports. A platform can grow while the average listing's occupancy slips, because the new demand spreads across more listings. Your job as a new operator is to find out two things. Can your unit win its share of that demand? And does the rent leave a margin? Sam, who records the market-research demos in Module 4 (lessons 4.1 through 4.5), shows you how to pull the market data. This lesson shows you what to do with it once you have it.

### 1.3.2 Why a strong travel market does not rescue a weak lease

An arbitrage deal has two kinds of costs. Some rise and fall with bookings: the platform fee, the cleaner, supplies. Others arrive every month whether anyone books or not: rent, utilities, insurance, software, a repair reserve, your permit fee.

Rent is the big fixed one. On most leased units it's the largest line on the page. A travel boom can lift your nightly rate a bit. It can't lower your rent. So when the fixed costs are too high for the unit's realistic revenue, more tourists in the city won't close the gap. They book the listings with better locations, better reviews, or lower prices first.

That's why I want you to think in contribution margin. Contribution is what each stay leaves behind after the costs that come with that stay. Those contributions pile up during the month. The pile has to cover the fixed costs before you keep a dollar.

### 1.3.3 Contribution margin in plain terms

Here's the chain for one stay:

1. Booked nights times the nightly rate gives nightly revenue.
2. Add the cleaning fee the guest pays. That's the stay's gross revenue.
3. Subtract the platform service fee. Airbnb's [service fee page](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/1857) describes a single fee structure where most hosts pay 15.5%. It also describes a split fee, where the host pays 3% and the guest pays a separate fee. Our example uses the 15.5% single fee on nightly plus cleaning.
4. Subtract what you pay the cleaner and the supplies that stay uses.
5. What's left is the stay's contribution.

Then add up every stay's contribution for the year and subtract fixed costs. That's your net before income tax.

If a local manager takes a percentage of revenue, that fee also moves with bookings. Put it in the variable costs. Lesson 6.5 covers when a manager makes sense. The worked example below assumes you manage it yourself.

Occupancy taxes are a separate stream. Guests pay them on top of the price, and they get passed through to the tax authority. They are not your revenue. Lesson 3.3 covers who collects which tax on each channel.

### 1.3.4 Break-even occupancy

Break-even occupancy is the share of nights you must book, at a given rate, for contributions to exactly cover fixed costs. Below it, you lose money. Above it, you keep something.

The formula is simple once you have the pieces:

- Break-even stays = annual fixed costs / contribution per stay
- Break-even nights = break-even stays x average stay length
- Break-even occupancy = break-even nights / 365

What makes break-even useful is the gap between it and your expected occupancy. That gap is your cushion. If you expect 70% and break even at 50%, you can lose a lot of bookings before you lose money. If you expect 62% and break even at 57%, one soft quarter wipes you out.

Break-even also moves with price. When you cut your rate to fill nights, each stay contributes less, so you need more stays to cover the same rent. Check break-even at the rate you'd charge in a slow month, not just your average.

### 1.3.5 Seasonality: the months that hurt

An annual average hides the months when you lose money. Most markets have a slow season. In a slow month your fixed costs stay put while bookings and rates both drop.

This matters for cash, not just profit. A deal can make money over a year and still run out of cash in February. You pay rent on the first of the month. You get paid by the platform after guests check in, according to its payout timing. If you enter a slow stretch with no cash reserve, you end up funding rent from your personal account.

So for each deal, sketch the slow months separately. Ask what occupancy and rate are realistic in the weakest two or three months. Then confirm your operating reserve covers the shortfall. Sam's 4.3 AirDNA tutorial shows where to see monthly history for comparable listings. Bring those monthly figures back to this worksheet.

### 1.3.6 Nearby listings and new supply

Your unit competes with every similar listing a guest sees in the same search. If five new two-bedrooms open within a few blocks, some guests who would have booked yours will book theirs. You feel that as lower occupancy, lower rates, or both.

Two things to check before you sign:

- How many comparable listings sit near the address today, and how their occupancy and rates look across the last 12 months.
- Whether new supply is coming. Look for new apartment buildings, new listings in the last few months, and demand that depends on one employer or one event.

AirDNA's outlook describes supply growing a little faster than demand in 2026. Whether that happens on your block is what Sam's 4.2 lesson (supply vs. revenue and oversupply screening) teaches you to check. For this lesson, turn it into a number: how much occupancy could new competition take from you before you hit break-even? That number is your expected occupancy minus your break-even occupancy. Expect 70% and break even at 50%, and new listings can take about 20 points before you lose money. Step 7 of the Alder Street example in 1.3.10 shows a smaller hit.

### 1.3.7 The stress case

A base case is what you expect. A stress case is what you can survive. I want you to build both before you sign anything.

A useful stress case changes the two inputs the market decides for you: nightly rate and occupancy. Keep every other line the same, so the only thing you see is the effect of the stress. Then run it a second time with a higher rent, to test a landlord's increase at renewal. In this lesson, "the stress case" means the rate and occupancy change alone. "Stress plus rent" means both.

If the stress case is still positive, the deal has room. If it's barely positive, you're betting on the base case. By barely positive I mean it stays positive only as long as nothing else goes wrong. For example, one rent increase at renewal would turn it negative. If it's negative, the lease has no margin for a normal slow year.

One note on units. A change in occupancy is measured in percentage points, or "points". Going from 70% to 60% is a drop of 10 points. A change in rate is a plain percent: $205 down 12% is about $180.

### 1.3.8 Startup cash and payback against the lease term

Setting up a unit costs money you don't get back: furniture, linens, safety gear, photos, the permit application. That's sunk setup cost. Other cash is tied up but not spent: the security deposit, the first month's rent paid up front, an operating reserve. The first month's rent is the same rent that's already in your yearly costs. It sits here because you pay it before any booking income arrives.

Payback is sunk setup divided by monthly net. It tells you how many months until the business returns the money you put in. Payback counts only the sunk setup. The deposit can come back at the end of the lease if you meet its terms. The reserve is your own cash, held for bad months. Compare payback with the lease term. If payback takes longer than the lease, a landlord who declines to renew leaves you with used furniture and a loss.

### 1.3.9 The three gates before signing

I use three gates. A deal must pass all three before you sign a lease.

**Gate 1: address-level permission.** The city, county, and state rules for this exact address must allow the use you plan. That use is unhosted (you don't live there), whole unit, short stays. The building, the zoning district (the city's land-use category for the address), and any HOA (homeowners association) or condo rules must allow it too. Module 3 teaches this gate. Lesson 3.1 explains the layers, Sam's 3.2 shows the live search, and 3.3 covers occupancy tax and registration numbers.

**Gate 2: written landlord permission.** The owner must approve short-term rental use in writing, in the lease or a signed addendum. A verbal "sure, that's fine" is not permission. Airbnb's own [hosting guidance](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/1397) tells hosts to read their lease and check with their landlord and HOA before hosting. Lessons 3.5, 5.3, and 5.7 cover the addendum and the landlord conversation.

**Gate 3: conservative deal math.** The stress case must stay positive, even with a rent increase at renewal. Break-even must sit at least 10 points below your expected occupancy. Base-case payback must be under half the lease term. The exercise in 1.3.11 turns these into four rows you mark pass, fail, or unknown.

The order matters. Check permission first, because it's the cheapest to check and the most absolute. Great math on a unit you can't legally operate is worth nothing.

### 1.3.10 Worked example: Alder Street

Alder Street is Unit 4B, 218 Alder Street: a hypothetical two-bedroom, two-bath apartment that sleeps 5. It's 10 minutes from a hospital campus and downtown in a mid-size city we don't name. The landlord owns the four-unit building and has offered a 24-month lease. All figures are assumptions from the course's recurring example.

**Step 1: contribution per stay at the base rate.**

| Line | Per stay | How |
|---|---:|---|
| Nightly revenue | $615.00 | 3.0 nights x $205 |
| Cleaning fee charged | $110.00 | |
| Stay gross revenue | $725.00 | $615 + $110 |
| Platform fee | -$112.38 | 15.5% x $725 |
| Cleaner pay | -$95.00 | |
| Supplies | -$14.00 | |
| **Contribution per stay** | **$503.63** | About 69% of stay revenue |

That's about $167.88 of contribution per booked night.

**Step 2: annual fixed costs.**

| Fixed cost | Annual |
|---|---:|
| Rent ($1,950 x 12) | $23,400 |
| Utilities and internet ($260 x 12) | $3,120 |
| Short-term rental (STR) liability insurance | $1,300 |
| Software: booking management, pricing, smart lock ($90 x 12) | $1,080 |
| Repairs and replacement reserve ($120 x 12) | $1,440 |
| Permit / registration | $250 |
| **Total fixed** | **$30,590** |

Monthly, that's about $2,549 you owe before the first guest arrives.

**Step 3: base case.** At 70% occupancy you book 255 nights. (70% of 365 is 255.5; round down to whole stays.) That's 85 stays of 3 nights.

- 85 stays x $503.63 = about $42,808 of contribution
- $42,808 - $30,590 = **$12,218 net before income tax**, about $1,018 a month

Gross booking revenue is 85 stays x $725 = $61,625, which is 2.63 times the year's rent. Lesson 1.5 uses that ratio as a quick screen.

**Step 4: break-even occupancy at $205.**

- Break-even stays = $30,590 / $503.63 = 60.7 stays
- Break-even nights = 60.7 x 3 = 182.2 nights
- Break-even occupancy = 182.2 / 365 = **49.9%**

Expected occupancy is 70%. The cushion is about 20 points of occupancy. A point is one percentage point: 70% down to 60% is 10 points.

**Step 5: stress case.** Nightly rate drops 12% to $180, and occupancy falls 10 points to 60%.

- Stay revenue: 3 x $180 + $110 = $650
- Contribution per stay: $650 - $100.75 platform fee (15.5% x $650) - $95 cleaner - $14 supplies = $440.25
- 219 nights, 73 stays: 73 x $440.25 = about $32,138
- $32,138 - $30,590 = **$1,548 net**, about $129 a month

Break-even at $180 is $30,590 / $440.25 = 69.5 stays, or 208.4 nights, or **57.1%** occupancy. In the stress case you're booking 60%, so you're only about three points above break-even.

**Step 6: stress plus a rent increase.** Suppose the landlord asks for $2,150 at renewal, about 10% more. At the base case, net drops to **$9,818** a year ($818 a month), and break-even rises to 53.8%. Combine that rent with the stress case and net is **-$852** a year (about -$71 a month), with break-even at 61.6% (assumption).

**Step 7: new competition.** Say new listings nearby pull occupancy from 70% to 64% at the same $205 rate (assumption). You'd book 234 nights and 78 stays. Net falls to **$8,693** a year, about $724 a month. The deal still works. Your cushion shrinks, though, and a rate cut on top would push you toward the stress case.

One more what-if: hand the unit to a local manager charging 20% of gross booking revenue. That's $61,625 x 20% = $12,325 a year, and the $12,218 base case becomes a loss of about $107. Lesson 6.5 covers when a manager makes sense.

**Step 8: one slow month.** Say your weakest month books 12 of 30 nights at the stress rate of $180 (assumption: 40% occupancy). That's 4 stays.

- 4 x $440.25 = $1,761 of contribution
- Monthly fixed costs are about $2,549
- Net for the month: about **-$788**

The operating reserve in this example is $4,420 (two months of rent plus utilities). That covers about 5.6 months like this one.

**Step 9: startup cash and payback.**

| Cash to start | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Sunk setup (furniture, linens, safety gear, photos, permit application) | $12,050 |
| Tied up (security deposit $1,950, first month's rent $1,950, operating reserve $4,420) | $8,320 |
| **Total cash on day one** | **$20,370** |

The $250 permit application in setup is the one-time filing. The $250 permit / registration in fixed costs is the yearly renewal (assumption). Your city's fee schedule decides both.

- Base-case payback: $12,050 / $1,018 = **11.8 months**. The lease runs 24 months.
- Over 24 months at the base case, net totals about $24,432. After recovering setup, that leaves about $12,382.
- Stress-case payback: $12,050 / $129 = about **93 months**. That's almost four times the lease term.
- Over 24 months at the stress case, net totals about $3,096. You'd still be about $8,954 short of your setup cost.

**Step 10: run the gates.**

- Gate 1, address-level permission: unknown. The city is unnamed on purpose. Lesson 3.1 tests this same unit against the official New York City and Los Angeles rules. It fails both, for different reasons, and you'll see why in 3.1. That's the point: the math above is worthless until Gate 1 passes at the real address.
- Gate 2, written landlord permission: unknown. The landlord has offered a lease. The lease or a signed addendum still has to say short-term rental use is allowed. It should also name the channels (Airbnb, Vrbo, direct bookings) and any conditions.
- Gate 3, conservative math, row by row from the sheet in 1.3.11:
  - 3a, stress case net is positive: pass (+$1,548 a year).
  - 3b, stress case with rent up 10% is not negative: fail (-$852 a year at $2,150 rent).
  - 3c, expected occupancy at least 10 points above break-even: pass (70% against 49.9%, about 20 points).
  - 3d, base payback under half the lease term: pass, just (11.8 months against a 12-month half-lease).

One row fails and one only just passes, so Gate 3 is marginal.

**Decision for Alder Street:** investigate. The math is good enough to keep going, but it depends on holding a 70% base case. Before signing, I'd want two things. First, a written cap on the rent at renewal, or a longer term. Second, market data from Sam's 4.1 to 4.5 lessons showing that 60% at $180 is a floor, not a typical month. If Gate 1 fails, the answer becomes walk, no matter what the numbers say.

### 1.3.12 Who confirms what

**Confirm with:** the city or county licensing office, in writing. They confirm that the planned use (unhosted, whole unit, short stays) is allowed at this exact address, and which permit it needs.

**Confirm with:** the landlord, in a signed lease clause or addendum. It should say short-term rental use is permitted, on which channels, and that you may use the address on a permit application. An attorney should review the addendum.

**Confirm with:** an insurer, in a written quote. It should show that the policy covers short-term rental guests at this address, at the premium you used in the model.

**Confirm with:** a CPA, before you rely on any after-tax number. The net figures in this lesson are before income tax.

**Confirm with:** the platform's current fee page, on the day you model the deal, which fee structure applies to your account.
