Students often ask me whether a city is Airbnb-friendly. That label doesn't help you. Permission attaches to an address, a use, and a person.

Picture a ladder. Each rung is a separate authority that can say no. The state. The zoning. The permit program. The building. The HOA. Your lease. You need a yes on every rung, for your exact use. One no anywhere ends the deal, even when every other rung says yes.

Airbnb's own regulations overview says many cities require you to register, get a permit, or get a license before you list or take guests. It also flags zoning and taxes. That page is a fine starting point. It isn't the final word for any address. The final word is the official ordinance, the city office that runs it, and the documents that bind your building and your lease.

Slide 3 · Six layers can each say no, and each has its own source document

Work from the widest authority down to the narrowest. Each layer has its own document.

The state may set rules that apply everywhere, like lodging tax registration or limits on what cities can regulate. Many states leave most of this to local governments, but check before you assume.

Zoning decides what uses a parcel may have. Some residential zones treat short stays as commercial lodging and ban them. Others allow them only as an accessory use to someone's home.

Many cities then run a separate permit program on top of zoning. That's where the category, eligibility, caps, fees, and your display number live.

Building and fire codes can set occupancy limits and alarm rules, and some programs exclude certain building types.

HOA, condo, or co-op rules can ban short rentals even where the city allows them.

And for arbitrage, the lease is the rung you control least. Lesson 3.5 and the updated 5.3 addendum lesson cover it.

3.1 · How STR regulation works§ 3.1.34 / 19
Slide 4 · Arbitrage is unhosted, so the host-present clause often settles the question

A hosted stay means the host lives in the unit and is there during the stay. Usually the guest rents a room while the host is home. An unhosted stay means guests have the whole place and nobody lives there.

Cities treat these two very differently. A hosted stay looks like a resident earning some income from a spare room. An unhosted stay looks like a hotel room carved out of the housing supply. So many cities allow the first and restrict or ban the second.

Arbitrage is almost always unhosted. You lease a whole unit and you live somewhere else.

So here's a habit I want you to build. When you open an ordinance, don't start at the top and read every word. Search for the clause that decides whether the host must be present. That one clause often answers the question for an arbitrage plan before you read anything else.

  • Cities usually define it by time, such as more than half the year
  • Some also require it to be the only property where you host
  • Cities may ask for proof: driver's license, voter registration, or tax records at that address
  • Signing a lease on a unit while you live elsewhere doesn't make it your primary residence
3.1 · How STR regulation works§ 3.1.45 / 19
Slide 5 · A primary-residence rule is usually a hard stop for arbitrage

A primary-residence rule says you may only host in the home where you actually live. Cities usually define that by time, like living there more than half the year. Some add that it has to be the only property where you host.

For an arbitrage operator, this is usually a hard stop. You can't turn a unit into your primary residence just by signing a lease on it while you live across town. And cities that use this rule often check. They may ask for a driver's license, voter registration, or tax records showing that address.

So when you read a city's rules, look for this test right after the hosted or unhosted clause. If the city has a primary-residence rule and you don't live in the unit, the arbitrage plan fails at this rung. You don't need to go further down the ladder.

3.1 · How STR regulation works§ 3.1.56 / 19
Slide 6 · Night caps hit arbitrage hardest because rent is due all 12 months

Caps come in three forms.

A night cap limits how many nights a year you may host. Some cities cap only unhosted nights and leave hosted nights alone.

A permit cap limits how many permits the city hands out, sometimes by neighborhood or zoning district. If the cap is full, new applicants may go on a waitlist.

A unit cap limits how many permits one person or one building can hold.

Of the three, the night cap hurts arbitrage the most. Your rent is due twelve months a year. If the city lets you host only part of the year, the math has to work on those nights alone, and the rest of the year is pure cost. In the worked example you'll see how fast that fails. At a 120-night cap, Alder Street loses $10,445 a year.

3.1 · How STR regulation works§ 3.1.67 / 19
Slide 7 · In New York City a 29-night stay is short-term and a 30-night stay is not

Many cities define a short-term rental by how long the guest stays, often under 30 nights. At or above that line, the stay is treated as an ordinary residential tenancy, with different rules.

Count carefully. New York City explains that under state law the check-in date is day zero. So a 29-night stay is a short-term rental, and a 30-night stay is permanent occupancy. Your city may count differently, so read its own definition.

Now, I know what some of you are thinking. Just take 30-night bookings and skip the rules. That isn't a loophole. Once a guest stays past the threshold, tenant protections can apply. Depending on the state and city, that can mean eviction procedures and rent rules if a guest won't leave.

So treat month-plus furnished rentals as a separate business, with its own rules. Have an attorney review it before you try it.

Maps to roadmap-2-complete-new-content.md § 3.1.6

Never assume a permit transfers: ask who holds it, and get the answer in writing

Some cities issue the permit to the property owner, some to the host, and some tie it to both. Three situations expose the difference:

  • You take over a unit that "already has a permit." If it belongs to the previous host, it may not cover you.
  • The landlord sells the building. A permit issued to the owner may end with the sale.
  • You add a partner or form an LLC. Some programs require a natural person, so an entity may not qualify.
3.1 · How STR regulation works§ 3.1.78 / 19
Slide 8 · Never assume a permit transfers: ask who holds it, and get the answer in writing

Here's a question students rarely ask: who does the permit belong to? Some cities issue it to the property owner. Some issue it to the host, meaning the person who lives there or runs the rental. Some tie it to both.

That matters in three situations.

First, a landlord tells you the unit already has a permit. If that permit belongs to the previous host, it may not cover you at all.

Second, the landlord sells the building. If the permit was issued to the owner, it may end with the sale, and you're left with a lease and no permission.

Third, you bring in a partner or set up an LLC. Some programs require the host to be a natural person. An entity may not qualify.

So never assume a permit transfers. Ask the licensing office in writing, and keep the reply in your deal folder.

New York City · Local Law 18 registration

New York City requires the host to live there, be present, and take two guests

  • Hosts must register with the city. The application fee is $145, non-refundable.
  • The host must be a natural person and the permanent occupant, and certify the lease allows STRs
  • The host stays in the unit with guests. No more than two paying guests at a time.
  • No entire apartment or home for less than 30 days, in any permanent residential building
  • Platforms must verify the registration number, legal name, and address before collecting a fee
  • Owners can put a building on the Prohibited Buildings List; OSE must then deny registration there
3.1 · How STR regulation works§ 3.1.89 / 19
Slide 9 · New York City requires the host to live there, be present, and take two guests

Let's look at two big cities with very different rules. I'm using them to show how much the answer changes with the address. Check the official pages on the day you research, because rules change.

New York City's program is Local Law 18, run by the Office of Special Enforcement. Hosts must register, or the rental is illegal. The fee is $145 and it isn't refundable. The host has to be a natural person and the permanent occupant, and must certify that the lease doesn't prohibit short-term rentals.

The host has to stay in the unit with the guests. No more than two paying guests at once, sharing a common household. You cannot rent an entire apartment for less than 30 days.

The registration number goes in every ad. Booking platforms can't collect a fee unless they've checked the city's verification system. And building owners can put a building on the Prohibited Buildings List, which means OSE must deny registration there. Airbnb's New York page says hosts awaiting approval can set a 30-night minimum.

Los Angeles · Home-Sharing Ordinance

Los Angeles allows home-sharing only in your own primary residence, 120 days a year

  • Primary residence only: more than six months a year, and your only home-share
  • Up to 120 days a year; Extended Home-Sharing adds criteria, like six months registered
  • RSO units excluded: most multifamily built before October 1, 1978 (check ZIMAS, Housing tab)
  • Renters submit the landlord's written, notarized approval
  • TOT registration, and the number on every listing
  • FAQ fees: $89 registration; Extended review $850, or $5,660 if discretionary
3.1 · How STR regulation works§ 3.1.810 / 19
Slide 10 · Los Angeles allows home-sharing only in your own primary residence, 120 days a year

Los Angeles runs a Home-Sharing Ordinance. City Planning handles registration and the housing department explains who's eligible.

Home-sharing is limited to your primary residence, where you live more than six months of the year. It must also be the only property where you home-share. The standard limit is 120 days per calendar year. Going beyond that takes an Extended Home-Sharing registration, with extra criteria, including holding a regular registration for at least six months.

Units under the Rent Stabilization Ordinance aren't eligible. That's most multifamily units built before October 1, 1978. You can check RSO status by address in the city's ZIMAS parcel tool, under the Housing tab.

A renter has to submit the landlord's written, notarized approval. Hosts register for Transient Occupancy Tax and post the number on every listing. The FAQ lists an $89 fee, with extended review at $850 or $5,660. Confirm current fees with City Planning, since FAQ documents can lag.

So both cities allow some short-term renting. Neither allows our model.

Slide 14 · Work every address down the same chain, and stop at the first no

Here's the flow I want you to follow for every address. Nine steps, grouped into five boxes.

Write down the exact plan first: address, unit, parcel number, hosted or unhosted, maximum guests, stay length, nights per year. Then confirm which jurisdictions apply. Many students check the wrong city because of a mailing address. Use the parcel lookup.

Find the official ordinance and the program's own page. Airbnb's city pages can point you there. A blog or a Facebook group is not a source. If the ordinance bans your model, stop.

Check the parcel and building: zoning, building type, rent-regulation status, any list like New York's. Then private rules: HOA and the lease. Then permit mechanics. Each has a stop exit.

If anything is unclear, ask the licensing office a specific yes-or-no question in writing. Save a dated PDF of every page and reply, and put them in the deal folder with the SR.1 checklist. Record permitted, prohibited, or unresolved. Unresolved means you don't sign yet.

This is the exercise, the permission ladder. Fill it in for one real address. If you want the full version, use the SR.1 regulatory compliance checklist.

Slide 16 · The last seven rungs cover stay length, building, private rules, and permit holder

The last seven rungs are where the private documents and the permit's fine print come in.

Minimum stay: what length counts as short-term, and how the city counts the check-in day. Building type: whether the building is excluded because it's rent-regulated, affordable, or on a list. Safety and inspection: what the city requires before it issues a permit.

Then the private layers. Do the HOA, condo, or co-op documents allow it? Does the lease or a signed addendum allow it, and does the city require the landlord's consent on the application, the way Los Angeles does?

Transferability: who holds the permit, and does it survive a sale or a change of operator? And number display: where the number has to appear, and whether the platform checks it.

At the bottom, write your decision, a recheck date, and who reviewed it. Permitted, prohibited, or unresolved. I'll say it once more because it's the whole point of this lesson: don't sign a lease on an unresolved address.

These are the main sources behind the lesson. All were checked on September 25, 2026. The full list in the lesson also includes the New York registration rules page, the Local Law 18 text, Airbnb's New York page, the Los Angeles landlord affidavit, and the earlier Los Angeles FAQ.

Notice the pattern. Airbnb's regulations overview is the starting point. The rules themselves come from the city: the Office of Special Enforcement in New York, and City Planning and the housing department in Los Angeles.

When you work your own address, your sources list should look like this. Official pages, each with the date you checked. If you can only find a blog post or a forum thread, write unresolved and send the licensing office a question.

The Alder Street figures, including the 120-night scenario and the $308 break-even rate, were computed from the canonical inputs. They're assumptions for teaching.
