Run your rental on what Airbnb actually publishes about search, not on rumors

Slide 1 · Run your rental on what Airbnb actually publishes about search, not on rumors

After this lesson you can tell what Airbnb actually says affects search apart from claims about "the algorithm." You'll map each factor to something you control, set up guest checkpoints, write a compliant review request, and start a monthly tracker.

So what's the "AI ranking era"? Airbnb doesn't publish whether or how AI changes its ranking. So I stick to what Airbnb does publish, and to the guest experience that shows up in reviews. I'm not promising a rank boost from any AI trick that Airbnb doesn't document.

Quick cast list. Cedar Ridge is a hypothetical lake house owned by Maya and Chris. Maya answers guest messages, so she's the message owner. Chris is her backup and gets the repair quotes. When I say "the owners," I mean the two of them.

Sam is the other instructor in this course. He teaches the live demos, including listing setup in OP.1 and OP.2, and pricing in OP.5. This lesson covers the operating side: the parts guests experience and then write about.

Slide 2 · Airbnb's search page names four main factors, plus variety and availability

This is what Airbnb's search page says. I checked it in September 2026, and the details are in the sources.

Airbnb names four main factors. Quality, popularity, price, and location heavily influence how a listing appears. Quality looks at listing content like photos and videos, guest ratings and reviews, communication between hosts and guests, and listing characteristics.

Popularity looks at how guests engage: how often they save a listing to a wishlist, book it, and message the host.

Price looks at the total price and how it compares with other listings in the area. Listings priced below comparable listings with similar characteristics tend to rank higher.

Location matters a lot. Listings in places guests like to stay tend to rank higher.

Then the page adds two more points. The system encourages variety, so guests see different hosts and prices. And availability matters. The more dates a listing is open, the more likely it shows up. More flexibility on length of stay helps a listing fit more trips.

That's the published list. When someone tells you about a factor that isn't on it, ask them for the Airbnb page.

Airbnb's four ground rules cover what guests tend to write about in reviews

  1. Accuracy: describe the home truly; change a booking only with the guest's consent
  2. Commitment: honor bookings, give a reliable check-in, cancel only for valid reasons
  3. Communication: the host or co-host (a helper on the listing) is available before and during stays
  4. Cleanliness: clean between every stay to a high standard

Airbnb enforces these. Actions can include education, warnings, and removing content that misrepresents the listing.

Slide 4 · Airbnb's four ground rules cover what guests tend to write about in reviews

Airbnb's ground rules for home hosts focus on four things.

Accuracy means the listing describes the home and the amenities available from check-in to checkout. You change an accepted booking's details only with the guest's consent.

Reservation commitment means you honor accepted reservations and provide a reliable check-in. You don't cancel confirmed reservations except for valid reasons outside your control.

Communication means the host or co-host, someone who helps run the listing, is available to respond to inquiries and unexpected issues, before and during stays.

Cleanliness means you clean between every stay to a high standard.

The same page says Airbnb enforces these rules. That can mean education, warnings, and requiring hosts to remove content that misrepresents the listing.

Now look at the list again. Accuracy, check-in, communication, cleanliness. Those are the same things guests tend to write about in reviews. So the ground rules are the minimum, and reviews measure how far above it you land. OP.6 and OP.8 were built to clear this floor.

A question guests ask twice means the house needs a fix, and many bad reviews start as a repeat nobody fixed.

Slide 7 ·

The friction log is the simplest tool in this lesson.

Every time a guest asks a question, mark it. One question is harmless. "Where's the water shutoff?" asked once is fine. By the second time, it goes on the fix list, and the house manual needs a line.

"How do the bunk lights work?" asked twice means the lights need a label. That's the house being unclear.

Many negative reviews start as a repeated question nobody fixed. Something confused the first guest, and they asked. The second guest asked too. That's your moment to fix it. The third guest didn't ask. They wrote it in the review.

So the log is a weekly habit. The owners read it every week and look for repeats. A repeat gets a fix, with a name and a date. That same twice-in-a-quarter rule shows up in the tracker and the exercise. It's also what drives the Cedar Ridge bunk room decision in the worked example.

Airbnb's review policy bans any trade, incentive, or pressure around a review

What the policy forbids

  • Coordinating with, manipulating, extorting, rewarding (incentivizing), or pressuring anyone to shape a review
  • Giving or withholding a review for something of value: a discount, refund, reciprocal review, or a promise not to take negative action
  • Threatening a negative review to get compensation, a refund, or another incentive

What that means for your request

  • It's neutral: it invites a review and asks for honest feedback
  • It offers nothing and hints at no trade
  • It doesn't tell the guest what rating to give
  • My rule, beyond the policy text: don't ask for "five stars" at all
Slide 8 · Airbnb's review policy bans any trade, incentive, or pressure around a review

After checkout, Airbnb prompts both guest and host to leave a review. According to Airbnb's review pages, each side has 14 days after checkout to submit one.

Airbnb's policy on authentic and trustworthy reviews sets the limits on how you ask. Hosts and guests may not coordinate with, manipulate, extort, incentivize, or pressure another person to influence a review. Reviews may not be given or withheld in exchange for something of value. That includes a discount, a refund, a reciprocal positive review, or a promise not to take negative action. And neither side may threaten a negative review to get compensation or a refund.

So the review request has to be neutral. It invites a review. It asks for honest feedback. It doesn't offer anything, hint at a trade, or tell the guest what rating to give.

I'd go a step further than the policy text. Don't ask for five stars at all. Naming the rating reads as pressure, and the policy prohibits pressure.

Maps to roadmap-2-complete-new-content.md § OP.9.5

A compliant request invites an honest review and asks to hear about problems directly

Send this, 2 or 3 days after checkout

  • "Hi [first name], thanks for staying at Cedar Ridge. If you have a few minutes, we'd appreciate an honest review on Airbnb. If anything wasn't right, please tell us directly too, so we can fix it for the next guests."
  • Private feedback is in addition to the review, never instead of it

Never write this

  • "Leave us 5 stars and we'll give you a discount next time." (Incentive)
  • "We'll return your deposit once you've left a review." (Trade)
  • "We gave you a great review; we hope you'll do the same." (Reciprocal pressure)
  • Any goodwill gesture tied to review timing, such as a credit in the same message as the request
Slide 9 · A compliant request invites an honest review and asks to hear about problems directly

Here's a request that works within the rules. It goes out two or three days after checkout. That gap gives the cleaner's notes time to come in, so you know about any problem before you ask.

It thanks them, invites an honest review, and asks them to tell you directly if anything wasn't right. That last line gives an unhappy guest a place to say it, and it gives you something to fix. Private feedback is in addition to the review, never instead of it.

The right column shows what never to write. The discount for five stars is an incentive. The deposit returned after a review is a trade. "We gave you a great review, we hope you'll do the same" is reciprocal pressure.

The fourth one is easy to miss. Don't tie any goodwill gesture to review timing. In OP.8, Maya gave a $100 credit when the internet went down. She sent it during the stay, with no mention of reviews. Keep it that way.

Your own review of the guest follows the same rules. Write it on the facts.

Answer criticism for future guests: check the facts, fix what you can, then respond

  1. Wait a day (house rule, not Airbnb's); don't reply while you're annoyed
  2. Check the facts: turnover photos, the message thread, the friction log for that stay
  3. Sort it: fair and fixable, fair but a known trade-off, or factually wrong
  4. Fix what you can before you respond; never claim a fix that isn't done
  5. Respond briefly: thank them, name the point, state the fix or fact; keep details private

Hosts can dispute a review that breaks the policy; a dispute doesn't ensure removal. A retaliatory or extortion review may qualify. A fair three-star about a hot bunk room doesn't.

Slide 10 · Answer criticism for future guests: check the facts, fix what you can, then respond

A host's public response shows on the listing with the review. So you're writing for future guests, not arguing with the past one.

Wait a day. That's my house rule, not Airbnb's. Check the facts with the turnover photos, the message thread, and the friction log. Then sort the complaint.

Fair and fixable is the hot bunk room: respond with what you've fixed. Fair but a known trade-off: say a guest writes that the house is far from town. Thank them and point to where the listing already says so. Factually wrong: say a guest writes that an amenity wasn't listed when it was. State the fact calmly.

Fix what you can before you respond, and never claim a fix that isn't done. Then respond briefly.

Save disputes for policy violations. A review stays up unless its author asks for removal within 30 days, or it breaks the policy. A dispute doesn't guarantee removal. Disputing fair criticism wastes time, and it doesn't fix the house.

In the first 90 days, that's 61 times 90 over 365. That's 15.04, so about 15 stays. Real launch months may be slower, so treat 15 as the base-case pace, not a promise.

Why does this matter? Because ten reviews is a small sample. One three-star review is one in every ten reviews a new guest reads. That's why the tracker counts the distribution, not just the average. And it's why the complaint log matters so much early. With ten reviews, two people mentioning the same problem is a pattern.

Slide 15 · Say a fix is done in public only once it's really done

Suppose the three-star review reads: "Great lake access and clean house, but the bunk room was really hot and the kids didn't sleep well."

Slide 18 · Four rules turn the tracker into decisions instead of a monthly report

These are the decision rules from the exercise, written as four questions. If the answer is no, do what the box on the right says.

First: has every complaint reason that showed up twice this quarter got a fix, an owner, and a date? If not, put it on the fix list now. At Cedar Ridge, that's the bunk room.

Second: have you read the turnover photos and message thread for every review below four stars, within 48 hours? The 48 hours is my house rule. Pick a window and keep to it. If not, read them before you do anything else.

Third: are the facts checked, and is every fix you mention really done? If not, wait, or say only what's already done.

Fourth: does the review break Airbnb's reviews policy? If not, don't dispute it. Fix the house instead. If you do dispute one, save the policy section you're relying on.

Every month, look at whether the fix list changed. If the tracker never moves the fix list, it isn't doing its job.
