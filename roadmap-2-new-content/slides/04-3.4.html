3.4 · Red Flags to Avoid in 2026§ 3.4.01 / 19
Slide 1 · Look for the fastest reason to say no before you research a deal

The title says cities to avoid. Here's why you won't get a list. A list of cities goes stale fast. What you get instead is a test you can run on any address, which is more useful.

A quick definition first. Arbitrage, in this course, means renting an apartment from a landlord and re-renting it to short-stay guests.

The screen gives every address one of three labels: reject, investigate, or advance. The first pass takes about an hour of desk work. Written confirmations and the lease review take longer.

The screen has two stages. Stage one is six hard stops. Any one of them ends the deal by itself, and no amount of revenue fixes it. Stage two is thirteen commercial and lease red flags. You score each one with fixed point rules, so two people looking at the same address land on the same answer.

We'll run Alder Street through the whole thing. Then we'll drop the same apartment into New York City and Los Angeles to see what changes.

Slide 2 · Screen for a reason to reject before you get attached to the deal

Most students do this in the wrong order. They find a nice apartment, run revenue numbers, pitch the landlord, and only then check whether the city allows the plan. By then they're attached. The rules feel like an obstacle instead of an answer.

I want you to flip that. Look for the fastest reason to say no. If you find one, stop and move to the next address. If you can't find one, you've earned the right to spend real time on the deal.

Rules attach to the exact address, not the city. Lesson 3.1 walked through the layers: address, building, zoning district, permit category, and the signed lease. A zoning district is the area the city's zoning map puts your address in. A permit category is the type of short-stay license the city issues. This lesson turns those layers into a quick screen.

That's also why there's no city blacklist here. A list like that goes stale within months. A condition that's true at your address puts the deal in trouble, whatever the city.

Slide 3 · H1 to H3: the city can end an arbitrage deal before the landlord says a word

A hard stop is a condition that ends the deal by itself. The first three come from the city.

H1 is the big one. Many cities separate hosted stays, where you live there and rent part of it, from unhosted stays, where the whole unit goes to guests. Arbitrage is almost always unhosted. New York City is the clearest example. Its Office of Special Enforcement, the OSE, says you can't rent an entire apartment for fewer than 30 days. Short stays need the host present with no more than two guests.

H2 is the primary-residence rule. Los Angeles limits home-sharing to the host's primary residence, and the host has to live there more than six months a year. If you won't live in the unit, that address is a reject for arbitrage.

H3 is the permit itself. Does the city issue it, is there a cap or a waitlist, and can it move to you? LA says a registration can't be transferred. NYC must deny registration in buildings on its Prohibited Buildings List. A previous tenant's permit is not your permit.

3.4 · Red Flags to Avoid in 2026§ 3.4.24 / 19
Slide 4 · H4 to H6: the building, the lease, or the insurer can end the deal too

The last three hard stops come from the building, the lease, and the insurer.

H4 is a ban from a homeowners association, called an HOA, or a condo or co-op board. The city might allow your use and the building can still block it. Airbnb itself tells hosts to check HOA and co-op rules before they start. If the governing documents ban rentals under a set number of days, stop there.

H5 is the one people talk themselves past. A lease that bans subletting, bans transient use, or just says nothing about short stays is not permission. A friendly text from the landlord isn't permission either. If the landlord won't sign a written short-term-rental addendum, the answer is reject. Some cities tie this to the permit too. NYC makes applicants certify the lease doesn't prohibit short-term renting, and Los Angeles requires written landlord approval for renters.

H6 is insurance. STR just means short-term rental. If nobody will write an STR liability policy for this unit and this use, you carry guest-injury risk yourself. You also have nothing to show a landlord who asks for proof of coverage. Lesson 2.4 covers insurance in detail.

3.4 · Red Flags to Avoid in 2026§ 3.4.35 / 19
Slide 5 · The same apartment fails in both New York City and Los Angeles, for different reasons

Here's why friendliness rankings fail. Take one apartment and drop it into two big travel markets.

In New York City, an operator renting the whole unit for three-night stays hits H1 straight away. Short stays are allowed only when the host is present with up to two guests. Whole-unit stays under 30 days are out. The OSE says New York State counts the check-in date as day zero. So check how the city counts a stay before you assume a long booking clears the 30-day line.

In Los Angeles, the same operator hits H2. Home-sharing is limited to the host's primary residence. You also can't take over a previous host's registration, because it doesn't transfer. That's H3. The Housing Department says home-sharing isn't allowed in units under the Rent Stabilization Ordinance. That covers most multi-family units built before October 1, 1978. In those buildings the use itself is prohibited, which is H1. So the age of the building can end the deal before you even get to the residence rule.

These rules can change. I've dated them to 25 September 2026. Recheck the official page. Sam, who records our live research demos, shows the search process in 3.2.

3.4 · Red Flags to Avoid in 2026§ 3.4.46 / 19
Slide 6 · Eight commercial flags ask whether the deal survives an ordinary slow stretch

Once an address clears the hard stops, the question changes. Does the deal survive an ordinary slow stretch? Lesson 1.3, the revenue and cost model, built the math. Here I turn that math into eight flags.

C1 and C2 are about cushion. Break-even occupancy is where revenue exactly covers every cost, rent included. Compare it with your base case. Your base case is your expected year. Your stress case is a deliberately bad year. This course's default stress case cuts the nightly rate 12% and occupancy by 10 percentage points. See if it loses money every month.

C3 is where your numbers come from. Comps are similar active listings nearby. Use comps with a full year of data, not the best listing in the neighborhood. Sam's 4.2 and 4.3 show you how to pull them.

C4 is concentration. If one stadium or one employer drives your bookings, your occupancy moves when they move.

C5, C6, and C7 are about money you've committed: rent you owe regardless, setup cost you've sunk, and the reserve that keeps one bad month from becoming a default. C8 is new supply, and Sam's 4.2 covers the oversupply screen.

3.4 · Red Flags to Avoid in 2026§ 3.4.57 / 19
Slide 7 · Five lease flags decide whether you get to keep the business you build

The lease decides whether you get to keep what you build. These are the five terms I'd look for first.

L1 is termination. If the landlord can end the lease without cause on short notice, your setup money is at risk every month. You want termination only for a defined breach, with written notice and a chance to fix it. That chance to fix it is called a cure period.

L2 is guest rights. The lease or addendum should say guests are allowed, how many, how they get in, and which building areas they can use. Silence invites a fight later.

L3 is insurance. Some leases demand a standard renter's policy that excludes business use. Others want the landlord named on a policy you haven't even quoted. If the lease and your policy don't match, flag it.

L4 is anything verbal. If you're counting on it, it has to be on paper.

L5 ties back to the math. If your stress-case payback runs past the lease term, you could hand back the keys before you've earned your setup cost back.

Slide 8 · Commercial rows score 0, 1, or 2 on fixed thresholds

Here are the point rules for the commercial side. The bands are my teaching defaults, an assumption, not industry rules. The reason they're fixed is simple. If you and a partner score the same address, you should get the same answer. Nobody gets to round up because they like the apartment.

A few of these need a word. C1 is measured in percentage points of occupancy, not scorecard points. If your base case is 70% and break-even is 60%, the cushion is 10 percentage points. That scores a 1. C2 looks at the stress case per month. Anything below zero is a 2. C4 counts demand sources. Two sources with neither dominant score the same as three.

C3 and C8 have a rule I want you to keep: if you haven't done the work yet, it's a 1, not a 0. Comps not pulled scores 1. Supply not checked scores 1. You score the numbers you have, not the ones you hope for.

C5 is annual rent divided by base-case gross. Under 35% scores zero. Over 45% scores 2.

C7 counts months of rent plus utilities you can cover from cash after setup. Two months or more scores zero.

Slide 9 · Lease rows can force investigate on their own: any 2 blocks an advance

The lease rows use the same 0, 1, 2 scale, with one extra rule. Any lease row scored 2 means the label can't be advance, even if the total is low. A lease that lets the landlord walk away without cause is a problem no revenue number fixes.

Apply the labels in order. First, any hard stop answered yes is reject. Second, ten points or more is reject. Third, any hard stop still unknown, any lease row at 2, or five to nine points is investigate. Otherwise it's advance.

Advance sends you to lease negotiation and full underwriting, the complete numbers review from lesson 1.3. A commercial row at 2 doesn't block advance on its own, but it goes on the to-do list. The cut-offs are my teaching defaults, not industry rules.

Treat investigate as a to-do list, not a maybe. Every row scored 1 or 2 gets an owner and a date. That's what we'll build for Alder Street next.
