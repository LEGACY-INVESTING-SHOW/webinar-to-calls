Slide 1 · Arbitrage works when one lease survives an ordinary bad month

People keep asking me whether arbitrage still works in 2026. I understand why. You read that Airbnb is growing, and then you read that the average listing is flat.

In this lesson I'm going to change the question. We won't judge the whole market. We'll judge one apartment, at one rent, under one city's rules.

By the end you'll be able to take a real unit and work out four numbers. What each stay leaves behind. The occupancy where you break even. What happens in a stress case. And how many months it takes to earn back your setup cash. Then you'll run the unit through three gates and write down a decision: sign, investigate, or walk.

Our example the whole way through is Alder Street, a hypothetical two-bedroom. Every figure on it is an assumption from the course's canonical example. None of this is legal, tax, lending, or insurance advice.

Here are two sets of numbers that seem to disagree. Airbnb reported that in the second quarter of 2026, revenue rose 17% to $3.6 billion, and nights and seats booked rose 10%. AirDNA, looking at the average listing, expected supply to grow a little faster than demand. It projected occupancy down about 1% and rates up about 1.5%.

Both can be true at once. When the platform grows but more listings open, the new demand gets spread across more units.

So I read both as weather reports. They tell you the climate. They don't tell you the rent your landlord wants, what's opening in your building, or whether your city lets you host at all.

A lease is a promise to pay a fixed rent at one address for a fixed term. So the question is always about this unit. Can it win its share of demand, and does the rent leave a margin? Sam's 4.1 through 4.5 show you how to pull the market data. This lesson is what you do with it.

1.3 · Arbitrage market reality check§ 1.3.23 / 20
Slide 3 · Rent arrives every month, so a travel boom can't rescue a weak lease

Every arbitrage deal has two kinds of cost. Some rise and fall with bookings. The platform fee, the cleaner, supplies. If nobody books, you don't pay them.

The others show up every month whether anyone books or not. Rent, utilities, insurance, software, a repair reserve, your permit fee. On most leased units, rent is the largest line on the page.

Here's why that matters. A strong travel season can nudge your nightly rate up. It can't touch your rent. So if the fixed costs are too high for what the unit can realistically earn, more visitors in the city won't close the gap. Those visitors book the listings with better locations, better reviews, or lower prices first.

That's why I want you thinking in contribution margin. It's the idea on the next slide, and it's the lens for the rest of this lesson.

1.3 · Arbitrage market reality check§ 1.3.34 / 20
Slide 4 · Each stay leaves a contribution, and contributions must cover fixed costs first

Contribution is what one stay leaves behind after the costs that come with that stay. Here's the chain.

Booked nights times the nightly rate gives nightly revenue. Add the cleaning fee the guest pays, and that's the stay's gross revenue. Take off the platform fee. Airbnb's service fee page describes a single fee where most hosts pay 15.5%, and a split fee where the host pays 3% and the guest pays a separate fee. Our example uses the 15.5% single fee on nightly plus cleaning. Then take off the cleaner and the supplies. What's left is the contribution.

Add up every stay's contribution for the year, subtract fixed costs, and you have net before income tax.

Two notes. A manager's percentage fee moves with bookings, so it's a variable cost. On Alder Street a 20% manager fee turns a positive year into a $107 loss. Lesson 6.5 covers when a manager makes sense. And occupancy taxes aren't your money. Lesson 3.3 covers who collects them.

Break-even occupancy is where contributions exactly cover fixed costs

  • Break-even stays = annual fixed costs / contribution per stay
  • Break-even nights = break-even stays x average stay length
  • Break-even occupancy = break-even nights / 365
  • Your cushion is the gap between break-even and expected occupancy
  • Expect 70%, break even at 50%: you can lose a lot of bookings. Expect 62%, break even at 57%: one soft quarter wipes you out.
Price moves it

Cut your rate to fill nights and each stay contributes less, so you need more stays. Check break-even at your slow-month rate, not only your average.

1.3 · Arbitrage market reality check§ 1.3.45 / 20
Slide 5 · Break-even occupancy is where contributions exactly cover fixed costs

Break-even occupancy is the share of nights you have to book, at a given rate, for contributions to exactly cover fixed costs. Below it you lose money. Above it you keep something.

The formula has three steps. Divide annual fixed costs by the contribution per stay. That's how many stays you need. Multiply by your average stay length to get nights. Divide by 365 to get occupancy.

The number on its own isn't what I care about. I care about the gap between break-even and what you expect to book. That gap is your cushion. Seventy percent expected against a 50% break-even is a lot of room. Sixty-two against 57 is almost none.

One more thing. Break-even moves with price. When you drop your rate in a slow month, each stay leaves less behind, so you need more of them to cover the same rent. So run the break-even at the rate you'd actually charge in a slow month.

Slide 6 · A deal can make money over a year and still run out of cash in February

An annual average hides the months when you lose money. Most markets have a slow season. In that season your fixed costs don't move, while bookings and rates both fall.

This is a cash problem, not only a profit problem. You pay rent on the first of the month. The platform pays you after guests check in, on its payout schedule. If you walk into a slow stretch with no reserve, you end up paying rent out of your personal account.

On Alder Street, say the weakest month books 12 of 30 nights at $180. That's 4 stays at $440.25 each, about $1,761, against roughly $2,549 of fixed costs. You're down about $788 that month. The canonical reserve is $4,420, two months of rent plus utilities. It covers about 5.6 months like that. Three in a row use about $2,365 of it.

Sam's 4.3 AirDNA tutorial shows where to find monthly history for comparable listings. Bring those monthly figures back to your worksheet.

1.3 · Arbitrage market reality check§ 1.3.67 / 20
Slide 7 · New listings nearby take occupancy, so put a number on that loss before you sign

Your unit competes with every similar listing a guest sees in the same search. If five new two-bedrooms open a few blocks away, some guests who would have booked yours will book theirs. You feel that as lower occupancy, lower rates, or both.

So before you sign, check two things. First, how many comparable listings sit near the address today, and how their occupancy and rates look over the last 12 months. Second, whether new supply is coming. Look for new apartment buildings, listings that opened in the last few months, and demand that hangs on a single employer or a single event.

AirDNA's outlook has supply growing a bit faster than demand in 2026. Whether that happens on your block is what Sam's 4.2 lesson on supply, revenue, and oversupply screening teaches you to check. For this lesson, I want you to turn it into one number: how much occupancy can new competition take from you before you reach break-even?

1.3 · Arbitrage market reality check§ 1.3.78 / 20
Slide 8 · A base case is what you expect, and a stress case is what you can survive

I want you to build two versions of every deal before you sign anything. The base case is what you expect. The stress case is what you can survive.

A useful stress case changes the two inputs you control least. That's the nightly rate and the occupancy. You can also raise the rent to test what happens if the landlord asks for more at renewal. Keep every other line exactly the same. That way the only thing you're looking at is the effect of the stress.

Then read the result. If the stress case is still positive, the deal has room. If it's barely positive, you're betting that the base case comes true. If it's negative, the lease has no margin for an ordinary slow year.

In a few slides you'll see Alder Street land in the middle row. That's a common place to land, and it's why the decision there isn't a clean yes.

1.3 · Arbitrage market reality check§ 1.3.89 / 20
Slide 9 · Payback is sunk setup divided by monthly net, and it has to fit inside the lease

Setting up a unit costs money you don't get back. Furniture, linens, safety gear, photos, the permit application. That's sunk setup cost.

Other cash is tied up but not spent. The security deposit, prepaid rent, and an operating reserve. You need that cash on day one, but it isn't gone in the same way.

Payback is sunk setup divided by monthly net. It tells you how many months until the business returns the money you put in.

Then compare payback with the lease term. This is the step people skip. If payback takes longer than the lease, you're relying on a renewal you don't control. A landlord who declines to renew leaves you holding used furniture and a loss. So the question is simple: does payback fit inside the lease with room to spare?

1.3 · Arbitrage market reality check§ 1.3.910 / 20
Slide 10 · Three gates must pass before you sign, and permission comes first

I use three gates, and a deal has to pass all three before you sign a lease.

Gate one is address-level permission. The city, county, and state rules for this exact address have to allow what you plan: unhosted, whole unit, short stays. So do the zoning, the building, and any HOA or condo rules. Module 3 teaches this gate.

Gate two is written landlord permission. In the lease or a signed addendum. A verbal okay isn't permission. Airbnb's own hosting guidance tells hosts to check their lease and talk to their landlord and HOA before hosting. Lessons 3.5, 5.3, and 5.7 cover the documents and the conversation.

Gate three is the math. The stress case survives, break-even sits well below expected occupancy, and payback fits the lease.

The order matters. Permission is the cheapest thing to check and the most absolute. Great math on a unit you can't legally operate is worth nothing.

None of the numbers we built count as real until the right person confirms them.

The city or county licensing office confirms, in writing, that your planned use is allowed at this exact address and which permit it needs. Unhosted, whole unit, short stays. Say it in those words.
