3.1 · How STR regulation works§ 3.1.01 / 20
Slide 1 · Permission attaches to one address, one use, and one person

Before any numbers matter, you need permission to run a short-term rental at one exact address. It's the check most students skip, and it's the one that can end a deal outright.

Two terms first. A short-term rental, or STR, is a furnished stay of less than about a month, the kind booked on Airbnb or Vrbo. Rental arbitrage means you lease a unit from a landlord, you don't live in it, and you rent it to short-stay guests. Most of this lesson asks whether that model is allowed at one address.

By the end you'll be able to list every layer of permission for that address, from the state down to your lease. You'll know which answers have to come in writing, and from whom. What you walk away with is a permission ladder with a result: permitted, prohibited, or unresolved.

We'll use a hypothetical unit called Alder Street, and we'll drop it into two real rulebooks, New York City and Los Angeles. Rules change often, so treat this as education, not legal advice, and check the official pages on the day you research.

Students often ask me whether a city is Airbnb-friendly. That label doesn't help you. Permission attaches to an address, a use, and a person.

Picture a ladder. Each rung is a separate question that can end the deal. Some rungs come from an authority: the state, the zoning, the permit program, the building, the HOA, your lease. That page is a fine starting point. It isn't the final word for any address. The final word is the official ordinance, the city office that runs it, and the documents that bind your building and your lease.

Slide 3 · Six layers can each say no, and each has its own source document

Work from the widest authority down to the narrowest. Each layer has its own document. Later, the ladder breaks these six layers into 15 specific questions.

The state may set rules that apply everywhere, like lodging tax registration or limits on what cities can regulate. Many states leave most of this to local governments, but check before you assume.

Zoning decides what uses a parcel may have. Some residential zones treat short stays as commercial lodging and ban them. Others allow them only as an accessory use, meaning a side use of someone's home.

Many cities then run a separate permit program on top of zoning. That's where the category, eligibility, caps, fees, and your display number live.

Building and fire codes can set occupancy limits and alarm rules, and some programs exclude certain building types.

A homeowners association, condo, or co-op can have rules filed in the property records that ban short rentals, even where the city allows them.

And for arbitrage, the lease is the layer you control least. Some cities also want the landlord's signed consent on the permit application. Lessons 3.5 and 5.3 cover those documents.

3.1 · How STR regulation works§ 3.1.34 / 20
Slide 4 · Arbitrage is unhosted, so the host-present clause often settles the question

A hosted stay means the host lives in the unit and is there during the stay. Usually the guest rents a room while the host is home. An unhosted stay means guests have the whole place and nobody lives there.

Cities treat these two very differently. A hosted stay looks like a resident earning some income from a spare room. An unhosted stay looks like a hotel room carved out of the housing supply. So many cities allow the first and restrict or ban the second.

Arbitrage is almost always unhosted. You lease a whole unit and you live somewhere else.

So here's a habit I want you to build. When you open an ordinance, don't start at the top and read every word. Search for the clause that decides whether the host must be present. That one clause often answers the question for an arbitrage plan before you read anything else.

3.1 · How STR regulation works§ 3.1.45 / 20
Slide 5 · A primary-residence rule is usually a hard stop for arbitrage

A primary-residence rule says you may only host in the home where you actually live. Cities usually define that by time, like living there more than half the year. Some add that it has to be the only property where you host.

For an arbitrage operator, this is usually a hard stop. You can't turn a unit into your primary residence just by signing a lease on it while you live across town. And cities that use this rule often check. They may ask for a driver's license, voter registration, or tax records showing that address.

So when you read a city's rules, look for this test right after the hosted or unhosted clause. If the city has a primary-residence rule and you don't live in the unit, the arbitrage plan fails at this rung. You don't need to go further down the ladder.

3.1 · How STR regulation works§ 3.1.56 / 20
Slide 6 · Night caps hit arbitrage hardest because rent is due all 12 months

Caps come in three forms.

A night cap limits how many nights a year you may host. Some cities cap only unhosted nights and leave hosted nights alone.

A permit cap limits how many permits the city hands out, sometimes by neighborhood or zoning district. If the cap is full, new applicants may go on a waitlist.

A unit cap limits how many permits one person or one building can hold.

Of the three, the night cap hurts arbitrage the most. Your rent is due twelve months a year. If the city lets you host only part of the year, the math has to work on those nights alone. The rest of the year is pure cost. In the worked example you'll see how fast that fails. At a 120-night cap, our example unit loses $10,445 a year.

3.1 · How STR regulation works§ 3.1.67 / 20
Slide 7 · Many cities draw the short-term line at 30 nights, so count the way they do

Many cities define a short-term rental by how long the guest stays, often under 30 nights. At or above that line, the stay is often treated as an ordinary residential tenancy, depending on the state and city. Those follow different rules.

Check how your city counts the length of a stay. New York City's FAQ spells out its rule. It treats the check-in date as day zero, under state law. By its count, a 29-night stay is short-term and a 30-night stay is not. Your city may count differently, so read its own definition.

Now, I know what some of you are thinking. Just take 30-night bookings and skip the rules. That isn't a loophole. Once a guest stays past the threshold, tenant protections can apply. That can mean eviction procedures and rent rules if a guest won't leave.

So treat month-plus furnished rentals as a separate business, with its own rules. Have an attorney review it before you try it.

Maps to roadmap-2-complete-new-content.md § 3.1.6

Never assume a permit transfers: ask who holds it, and get the answer in writing

Some cities issue the permit to the property owner, some to the host, and some tie it to both. Three situations expose the difference:

  • You take over a unit that "already has a permit." If it belongs to the previous host, it may not cover you.
  • The landlord sells the building. A permit issued to the owner may end with the sale.
  • You add a partner or form an LLC. Some programs require a natural person (an individual, not a company), so an entity may not qualify.
3.1 · How STR regulation works§ 3.1.78 / 20
Slide 8 · Never assume a permit transfers: ask who holds it, and get the answer in writing

Here's a question students rarely ask: who does the permit belong to? Some cities issue it to the property owner. Some issue it to the host, meaning the person who lives there or runs the rental. Some tie it to both.

That matters in three situations.

First, a landlord tells you the unit already has a permit. If that permit belongs to the previous host, it may not cover you at all.

Second, the landlord sells the building. If the permit was issued to the owner, it may end with the sale, and you're left with a lease and no permission.

Third, you bring in a partner or set up an LLC. Some programs require the host to be a natural person, meaning an individual, not a company. An LLC may not qualify.

So never assume a permit transfers. Ask the licensing office in writing, and keep the reply in your deal folder.

3.1 · How STR regulation works§ 3.1.89 / 20
Slide 9 · New York City: the host must live there, stay home, and host two guests at most

Let's look at two big cities with very different rules. I'm using them to show how much the answer changes with the address. Rules change, so check the official pages on the day you research.

New York City's program is Local Law 18, run by the Office of Special Enforcement, or OSE. Hosts must register, or the rental is illegal. The host has to be a natural person who lives there, and must certify that the lease doesn't prohibit short-term rentals.

The host has to stay in the unit with the guests. No more than two paying guests. Guests share the home with the host, and they must be able to use the whole unit. You cannot rent an entire apartment for less than 30 days.

The registration number goes in every ad. Booking platforms can't collect a fee unless they've checked the city's verification system. And building owners can list a building on the Prohibited Buildings List, which means OSE must deny registration there.

Also on the city's pages: the fee is $145, non-refundable. Airbnb says hosts awaiting approval can set a 30-night minimum. That's a holding pattern while you wait. It doesn't get you around the rules.

Los Angeles · Home-Sharing Ordinance

Los Angeles: primary residence only, with a 120-day yearly cap unless Extended

  • Primary residence only: more than six months a year, and your only home-share
  • Up to 120 days a year; Extended Home-Sharing adds criteria, like six months registered
  • Rent-stabilized (RSO) units excluded: most multifamily built before Oct 1, 1978; check the city's ZIMAS parcel tool, Housing tab
  • Renters submit the landlord's written, notarized approval
  • Register for the city's hotel tax (Transient Occupancy Tax, TOT) and show the number on every listing
3.1 · How STR regulation works§ 3.1.810 / 20
Slide 10 · Los Angeles: primary residence only, with a 120-day yearly cap unless Extended

Los Angeles runs a Home-Sharing Ordinance. City Planning handles registration and the housing department explains who's eligible.

Home-sharing is limited to your primary residence, where you live more than six months of the year. It must also be the only property where you home-share. The standard limit is 120 days per calendar year. Going beyond that takes an Extended Home-Sharing registration, with extra criteria, including holding a regular registration for at least six months.

Units under the Rent Stabilization Ordinance aren't eligible. That's most multifamily units built before October 1, 1978. You can check RSO status by address in the city's ZIMAS parcel tool, under the Housing tab.

A renter has to submit the landlord's written, notarized approval. Hosts register for the city's hotel tax, the Transient Occupancy Tax, and post the number on every listing.

Also on the city's pages: the FAQ lists an $89 fee, with extended review at $850, or $5,660 when a case-by-case review is required. Confirm current fees with City Planning, since FAQ documents can lag.

So both cities allow some short-term renting. Neither allows the arbitrage model we'll test next.

Suppose someone tried to fix the Los Angeles problem. Move in, make it your primary residence, and host within the 120-day cap. I'll treat that as 120 booked nights and run it as a business test, keeping every other assumption the same.

One hundred twenty nights at $205 is $24,600. At three nights a stay, that's 40 stays, and $110 cleaning each adds $4,400. That's $29,000 of gross bookings. Slide 15 · Work every address down the same chain, and stop at the first no

Here's the flow I want you to follow for every address. Nine steps, grouped into five boxes.

Write down the exact plan first: address, unit, parcel number, hosted or unhosted, maximum guests, stay length, nights per year. Then confirm which jurisdictions apply. Many students check the wrong city because of a mailing address. Use the city or county parcel lookup to get the parcel number, the ID the county gives each lot.

Find the official ordinance and the program's own page. Airbnb's city pages can point you there. A blog or a Facebook group is not a source. If the ordinance bans your model, stop.

Check the parcel and building: zoning, building type, rent-regulation status, any list like New York's. Then private rules: HOA and the lease. Then permit mechanics. Each has a stop exit.

If anything is unclear, ask the licensing office a specific yes-or-no question in writing. Save a dated PDF of every page and reply. Put them in the deal folder with SR.1, the regulatory compliance checklist in the course resources. Record permitted, prohibited, or unresolved. Unresolved means you don't sign yet.

Slide 17 · The last eight rungs test the building, private rules, insurance, and the permit

The last eight rungs are where the private documents and the permit's fine print come in. Same rule: Yes is always the good answer.

Minimum stay: is your typical stay allowed, counted the way the city counts? Building type: is the building eligible, meaning not rent-regulated, affordable, or on a prohibited list? Safety and inspection: can you meet what the city requires before it issues a permit?

Then the private layers. Do the HOA, condo, or co-op documents allow it? Does the lease or a signed addendum allow it, and can you get any landlord consent the city requires, the way Los Angeles does?

Insurance: will an insurer cover short-term guests at this address, under your permit category? Transferability: will the permit be in your name, and survive a sale or a change of operator? And number display: can you show the number where required and pass the platform's check?

At the bottom, write your decision, a recheck date, and who reviewed it. Don't sign a lease on an unresolved address.

Here's who confirms what.

The city or county licensing office confirms, in writing, whether your exact plan fits a permit category at this address. It also confirms who the permit holder has to be.

The zoning or planning department confirms the parcel's district allows the use.

An attorney licensed in your state confirms that the lease and any addendum allow the use. They also tell you whether stays of 30 nights or more would create a tenancy.

If there's an HOA, condo, or co-op board, get their answer in writing too.

The landlord signs a document that lets you use the address on a permit application. That's separate from the lease or addendum that allows short stays. Some cities require a specific form. Los Angeles, for example, uses a notarized affidavit.

And your insurer confirms the policy covers short-term guests at this address, under the permit category you'll actually hold. Save every one of these in the deal folder.
