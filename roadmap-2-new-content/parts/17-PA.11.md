### PA.11.0 Learning outcome

After this lesson you can check your final closing figures line by line against your accepted offer and your latest loan terms. You'll know which document does what and how to move your closing funds without handing them to a scammer. You'll also know what to collect when the keys change hands. You'll leave with a filled closing-figures comparison, a closing-day checklist, and an unresolved items log that carries into PA.12.

### PA.11.1 Three things change hands

Closing looks like a pile of signatures. Underneath, three things move.

Money moves from you and your lender to the seller, the seller's lender, the title company, and a list of service providers. Documents move ownership to you and give your lender a claim on the house. Control moves too: keys, codes, accounts, records, and the right to occupy.

Most closing problems come from one of those three getting ahead of the others. You wire money before you've checked the figures. You sign before the walkthrough. You get the deed but not the lock codes. This lesson puts them in order.

### PA.11.2 Which closing disclosure will you get?

For most consumer mortgages, the lender must give you a Closing Disclosure. The CFPB says you must receive it at least three business days before closing. The window lets you compare final terms and costs with your Loan Estimate and ask questions first ([CFPB, Review documents before closing](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/close/review-documents-before-closing/)). It also says not to close until you've received and reviewed it ([CFPB, What should I do if I don't get a Closing Disclosure three days before closing](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-should-i-do-if-i-do-not-get-a-closing-disclosure-three-days-before-my-mortgage-closing-en-1911/)).

Investment property adds a wrinkle. The CFPB's Regulation Z commentary treats credit to buy a non-owner-occupied rental as business-purpose credit ([CFPB, Comment for 1026.3](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/interp-3/)). Business-purpose credit falls outside many of those consumer disclosure rules. So a DSCR lender may give you a settlement statement from the title company instead of a Closing Disclosure. The same commentary says a vacation home the owner will occupy for more than 14 days in the coming year doesn't get that non-owner-occupied treatment. That's one more reason to give your lender an honest personal-use number.

What does that mean for you? Ask your lender, in writing, two questions early. Which closing document will I receive? When will I receive final figures? If your loan doesn't come with the three-day rule, ask for the final figures at least three business days ahead anyway. Put that request in writing to both the lender and the title company. You're asking for time to check. On your loan it may not be a legal right.

Whatever the form is called, the review is the same. Compare it with the accepted offer, any amendments from PA.9, and your latest loan terms.

### PA.11.3 What to compare, line by line

The CFPB says to check that cash to close matches your latest Loan Estimate, and to ask the lender why if it doesn't ([CFPB, Closing Disclosure explainer](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/closing-disclosure/)). It also notes some costs can change, and there are legal rules about what can change and by how much. Those limits depend on the loan type and the fee. Your lender and closing agent explain which ones apply to you.

Work through four groups.

The first group is the deal terms from your contract. Check the purchase price, the earnest money credit, any seller credit, any repair escrow, and any items the seller agreed to leave. For Cedar Ridge, the PA.9 amendment has the seller fix the septic and treat the well before closing. The seller also pays the $3,100 lien from their proceeds. The lien payoff should show on the seller's side, not yours.

The second group is the loan terms. Check the loan amount, rate, points, monthly principal and interest, loan term, and any prepayment penalty. Compare each against the written quote you accepted in PA.7. A rate or point change needs an explanation. The CFPB notes a rate can change if something important changes, such as an appraisal coming in low ([CFPB, rate or fees changed](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/my-rate-or-the-fees-changed-between-my-loan-estimate-and-my-closing-disclosure-what-do-i-do-en-184/)).

The third group is closing costs. Check each fee against the estimate. On a Closing Disclosure, services you couldn't shop for, services you did shop for, and other costs sit in separate sections. Your title charges should match the title company's quote. The owner's title policy should be there if you chose it in PA.9.

The fourth group is prepaids and escrow. This is where the insurance premium, prepaid interest, and the starting balance of your escrow account appear. The CFPB explains that an escrow account lets you pay insurance and property taxes monthly with your loan payment ([CFPB, Closing Disclosure explainer](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/closing-disclosure/)). Check that the insurance premium is for the STR policy from PA.9, not a generic homeowners estimate.

Finally, check the names. The buyer, borrower, and insured names must match your PA.10 worksheet exactly.

### PA.11.4 Worked example: Cedar Ridge closing figures

In PA.7, Maya and Chris pursued conventional first and kept DSCR as the backup. For this walkthrough, assume the conventional file stalled on documentation and they switched to the backup, which is the switch PA.7 planned for (assumption). So they are closing on the DSCR quote from the canonical example: $385,000 price, $288,750 loan at 7.25% with 1 point, 30-year fixed. Down payment is $96,250. Total closing costs including points are $14,438, and cash to close is $110,688. Monthly P&I is $1,970, and monthly PITIA is $2,557.

The canonical file only gives the total: 3% of price plus 1 point. Below is one plausible way that $14,438 breaks down. Every line item is an assumption. Real fees vary by state, lender, and title company. Transfer taxes and property tax prorations are left out to keep the example simple. In this hypothetical, the seller pays any transfer tax.

The final column adds to the canonical figures. Points of $2,888 plus $11,550 of other costs equals $14,438. The $11,550 is 3% of $385,000. Adding the $96,250 down payment gives $110,688.

The prepaid interest line uses the daily rate. $288,750 × 7.25% ÷ 365 = about $57.35 a day. Ten days is about $574. Fifteen days is about $860. The amount depends on the day of the month you close, which is why it moved.

Now read the changes the way you would at your kitchen table.

1. Insurance up $600, and its escrow line up $100. The estimate used a generic homeowners premium of $2,600 (assumption). The final figures use the actual STR quote of $3,200 from PA.9. That's expected, and it's the right policy. Two months at $3,200 a year is $533. The $3,200 prepaid here is the same premium that appears in the B1 operating budget. Count it once in year-one cash, not in both closing costs and operating costs.
2. Appraisal up $100. The STR rent analysis add-on cost more than estimated. Ask the lender whether that increase is allowed under the rules that apply to your loan.
3. Lender's title policy and settlement fee up $95 combined, and survey up $50. These are services they shopped for. Ask the title company and surveyor to explain each change.
4. Prepaid interest down $286. The closing date moved later in the month.

Net effect: $600 + $100 + $100 + $50 + $45 + $50 - $286 = $659. That matches the difference between the two totals.

Next, the wire amount. Suppose Maya and Chris paid $7,700 of earnest money at contract, which is 2% of price (assumption). That deposit is credited at closing. The funds they still owe are $110,688 - $7,700 = $102,988. That's the number they'll wire, and it's the number a scammer would love to redirect.

Here are the checks that aren't in the fee table. The seller's septic and well invoices are in the file, per PA.9. The seller's side shows the $3,100 lien payoff. The borrower names match the PA.10 decision. The monthly payment reads $1,970 P&I and about $2,557 with escrow ($3,850 tax plus $3,200 insurance, divided by 12, is $587.50 a month).

### PA.11.5 The final walkthrough

The CFPB says to use the final walkthrough to confirm agreed repairs were made and that items the seller agreed to leave are still there. If something's wrong, contact the seller right away ([CFPB, What should I do before, during, and after closing](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-should-i-do-before-during-and-after-the-mortgage-closing-process-en-1907/)).

For an STR, the walkthrough is also your first look at the house as an operator. Bring the PA.9 tracker and walk it row by row.

1. Seller repairs. Look at the septic work and the new well UV system, and get both invoices. Check anything else the seller agreed to fix.
2. Included items. Check furniture, appliances, the dock equipment, and anything else in the contract.
3. New damage. Look for anything that wasn't there at inspection: water stains, broken windows, storm damage.
4. Systems. Run water at every tap, flush toilets, test the HVAC in both modes, and check the water heater.
5. Photos and meter readings. Photograph each room and write down the utility meter readings with the time. You'll use them for final bills and for the PA.12 handoff.

If the walkthrough turns up a real problem, don't sign and hope. Call your agent and attorney. Common fixes include a repair escrow, a credit, or a short delay. The CFPB notes you can walk away at closing, but you might lose your deposit and face other consequences under your contract ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-should-i-do-before-during-and-after-the-mortgage-closing-process-en-1907/)). Let your attorney use that lever. Don't try it alone at the table.

### PA.11.6 What you sign and what each document does

You'll sign a stack. Know what the main pieces do, and read the names on each one against your PA.10 worksheet.

The deed transfers ownership from the seller to the title owner you chose. The seller signs it, and it gets recorded in the county records. Ask the title company when you'll receive the recorded copy.

The promissory note is the borrower's promise to repay the loan on its terms. Read the rate, payment, maturity, and any prepayment section.

The mortgage or deed of trust gives the lender a claim on the house if the loan isn't repaid. It also holds clauses you should read, including the due-on-sale clause discussed in PA.10.

A personal guaranty appears if your lender requires one for an entity loan. Read it with your attorney before closing day, not at the table.

The final closing figures and settlement statement show every dollar in and out. Initial every page only after it matches your comparison.

The insurance binder or declarations page isn't signed at closing, but the lender needs it. The effective date must be the closing date or earlier. Named insured and mortgagee must match the loan documents.

The CFPB says to request the rest of your closing documents in advance. If documents at the table differ from what you reviewed, don't sign until you're comfortable ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-should-i-do-before-during-and-after-the-mortgage-closing-process-en-1907/)).

### PA.11.7 Wire fraud: the protocol

Closing-day wire fraud targets exactly your situation: a known closing date, a large transfer, and several parties emailing each other. The CFPB warns that scammers send emails posing as your agent or settlement agent with last-minute changes to wiring instructions ([CFPB, Buying a home? Beware of mortgage closing scams](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_mortgages-scams_one-pager.pdf)). Its guidance comes down to a handful of steps. Here's how I'd turn them into a protocol.

1. Set it up early. At contract, meet or call your agent and settlement agent. Identify two trusted people who can confirm payment instructions, as the CFPB suggests. Write down their phone numbers from a source you trust. Use a card handed to you in person, or the company website you typed in yourself.
2. Agree on a code phrase. The CFPB suggests a code phrase only your trusted contacts will know ([CFPB, Beware of mortgage closing scams](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/beware-mortgage-closing-scams/)).
3. Verify every instruction by voice. Before you send any money, call the settlement agent at the saved number and read back the bank name, account name, account number, and amount. The CFPB says to verify in person or by the phone number you previously agreed to. It says to avoid phone numbers or links in an email ([CFPB one-pager](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_mortgages-scams_one-pager.pdf)).
4. Treat any change as fraud until proven otherwise. A "new account" email is the classic move. Stop and call the saved number.
5. Don't email financial information. The CFPB says email is never a secure way to send it.
6. Don't trust caller ID. The CFPB notes scammers can spoof phone numbers. If a call feels wrong, hang up and call the saved number.
7. Confirm receipt. After the wire, call the settlement agent at the saved number and confirm they received the exact amount.
8. If something goes wrong, act in minutes. The CFPB says to contact your bank or wire service immediately to try to stop or reverse the transfer. It also says to file a complaint with the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center ([CFPB, Mortgage closing scams](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/archive/blog/mortgage-closing-scams-how-protect-yourself-and-your-closing-funds/)).

For Cedar Ridge, the wire is $102,988. Maya and Chris saved the settlement agent's number from the title company's website at contract. They agreed on a code phrase with their agent. Their bank's wire form gets filled in only after the voice readback.

### PA.11.8 Possession: keys, codes, records, and proof

Ownership transfers when the deed records, but control transfers when you have every way into the house and every record about it. Your contract says when possession passes. Collect these before you leave the closing or the house that day.

1. Physical keys, garage remotes, mailbox keys, and gate or boat-launch passes.
2. Door codes, alarm codes, and the admin login for any smart lock, camera, thermostat, or Wi-Fi router. Ask the seller to remove their accounts from each device, then reset every code yourself.
3. Warranties and manuals: roof, HVAC, water heater, appliances, septic components, and the well UV system the seller installed.
4. Vendor records: septic pump and repair invoices, well test results, pest treatment, HVAC service, and the names of the seller's plow and trash services.
5. Permits and approvals: the county's STR permit status for your new application, septic permit record, and any building permits for past work.
6. Proof of payment: final figures, wire confirmation, title policy commitments, and the insurance declarations.

Anything you don't get goes on the unresolved items log. That log is the bridge to PA.12, and every row needs an owner and a date.

### PA.11.10 Who confirms what

**Confirm with:** your lender. It confirms which closing document you'll get and when, why any rate, point, or fee changed, and the exact wire amount.

**Confirm with:** the title company or closing attorney. They confirm the settlement figures, lien payoffs, recording, title policies, and wire instructions, verified by voice at a saved number.

**Confirm with:** your real estate attorney or agent. They confirm possession terms, walkthrough remedies, and anything in the documents that differs from your contract.

**Confirm with:** your insurer. It confirms the policy effective date, named insured, and mortgagee clause.

**Confirm with:** your bank. It confirms its wire process, cutoff times, and who to call immediately if a wire needs to be stopped.
