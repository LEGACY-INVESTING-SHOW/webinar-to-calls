### OP.6.0 Learning outcome

By the end of this lesson you can turn a furnishing budget into a room-by-room purchase plan for a specific house. The plan will match a named guest, a sleeping layout the listing can state truthfully, and a safety list your insurer can read. You'll also leave with a delivery schedule, a linen cycle, an owner storage plan, a replacement estimate, and a room inventory Sam can use in OP.1 to build the listing.

### OP.6.1 Start with the guest, not the catalog

Most furnishing mistakes start in a store. Someone falls for a sofa, then tries to make the house fit it. Reverse that. Write down who is booking this house and what they'll do during the stay. Then buy for that person.

A guest profile has four parts. Who comes (families, two couples, a work team, a fishing group). How many sleep over (the real number, not the maximum you could squeeze in). What they do during the day (lake, hiking, remote work, cooking in). And what they complain about at comparable houses. You can read that last part in the reviews on nearby listings. Sam's PA.4 underwriting package already names the comparable set, so start there.

For each part of the profile, ask what it demands from the house. Two families with small children need a place to eat together, a crib that meets current standards, and furniture that can't tip. A work group needs a real desk, a chair that survives four hours, and reliable internet. A lake crowd needs towels you don't mind seeing on a dock, a place to rinse off, and hard floors near the door.

Then write down what this house is *not* for. If you won't allow parties, don't buy a bar cart and a sound system. If the lower level has stairs only, don't imply step-free access. Every accessibility claim in the listing has to be true on the day a guest arrives. Airbnb's [ground rules for hosts](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/2895) say the listing page should accurately describe the home and the amenities available from check-in to checkout.

### OP.6.2 Sleeping layout: the one number guests check twice

The sleeping layout drives more guest decisions than any throw pillow. It also drives your linen count, your cleaning time, and your insurance conversation. So set it before you buy anything.

Count sleeping spots by bed, not by room. A king sleeps two. A queen sleeps two. A twin sleeps one. A sofa bed sleeps whoever is willing, and reviews tend to say so. If your advertised guest count depends on a sofa bed or an air mattress, write that plainly in the listing.

Airbnb lets you list the number and type of beds in each room. Its [sleeping arrangements article](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/1224) says a bunk bed counts as 2 beds in the bed count shown on the listing. That matters when you compare your bed count with a competitor's. Six beds can mean six people or twelve, depending on the bed types.

Check three things before you settle the layout:

1. Local occupancy limits and any permit condition on guest count. Some permits cap occupancy per bedroom or per septic capacity. PA.9 should have captured this during diligence.
2. Egress. Every sleeping room needs a way out in a fire that a guest can find in the dark. Your local building or fire official decides what counts.
3. Insurance. Some STR policies ask for maximum occupancy and bed types. A number that differs from the listing can create a claim problem.

**Confirm with:** the local licensing office or building department on maximum occupancy and bedroom egress for this address; the insurer that the listed guest count matches the policy.

### OP.6.3 Work space, storage, outdoor use

Three areas get skipped in most furnishing budgets, then show up in reviews.

A work space is a desk, a chair, a lamp, and outlets within reach. If you claim a "dedicated workspace," a guest will expect exactly that, not a dining chair near a window. If the internet can't hold a video call, don't advertise remote work. Test the speed on the laptop and phone you actually own, in the room where the desk sits, and write the result in the handoff packet.

Storage means two different things. Guests need somewhere to put a suitcase and hang clothes for a four-night stay. The owner needs a locked place for personal items, spare inventory, and cleaning stock. Mixing the two produces the classic review: "closets were full of the owner's stuff."

Outdoor use is where lake and mountain houses earn their rate and where many injuries happen. Decide which outdoor features you'll offer and which you'll maintain. A grill needs a cleaning step, fuel checks, and a placement your insurer and local fire rules accept. A fire pit, dock, kayak, or hot tub each carries its own insurance question. If you can't maintain it every turnover, don't list it.

### OP.6.4 Safety and insurability before style

Safety equipment is the first line in the budget, not the last. Here's what the primary sources say, and what they leave to you.

Airbnb's [safety requirements for homes](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/2904) say hosts must state whether smoke and carbon monoxide alarms are present. If present, they must work and hosts must test them regularly. The same page says hosts must address fire and electrical hazards, including blocked exits, inoperable smoke detectors, and exposed wiring. It also covers poorly maintained appliances such as hot tubs, gas appliances, and furnaces, and physical risks like unsafe walls or windows. Hosts are responsible for any area guests can use during the stay.

The Consumer Product Safety Commission publishes a one-page [vacation rental safety tips sheet](https://www.cpsc.gov/s3fs-public/VacationRentalSafetytips002_0.pdf?VersionId=MKcrFCYmuih6xmpoX59wcdmexPwzAXrO) and related [news releases](https://www.cpsc.gov/Newsroom/News-Releases/2022/Make-Safety-a-Priority-When-Staying-in-a-Vacation-Rental-Home). Its recommendations include:

- Smoke alarms on every level, outside each sleeping area, and inside every bedroom.
- Carbon monoxide alarms on every level, outside sleeping areas.
- At least one fire extinguisher.
- TVs and furniture anchored to the wall, and no dangling window-covering cords.
- Pools and spas with compliant drain covers and a fence at least four feet high with self-closing, self-latching gates.
- Cribs that meet CPSC standards, with a bare sleep space (fitted sheet only).
- Cleaning supplies locked or out of children's reach, and a recall check on child products at [SaferProducts.gov](https://www.saferproducts.gov).

Those are consumer guidance, not your local code. Your city, county, or state may require more, such as hard-wired or interconnected alarms. Treat the CPSC list as a floor for planning, then ask the fire marshal what applies at this address.

Security devices come next, because they affect privacy and listing accuracy. Airbnb's [device rules](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/3061) ban security cameras and recording devices that monitor indoor spaces, even if they're turned off. Exterior cameras are allowed if the listing discloses their location. They can't point into the house or cover areas with a greater privacy expectation, such as an enclosed outdoor shower or sauna. Noise decibel monitors that measure only sound level are allowed in common spaces. They must be disclosed and kept out of bedrooms, bathrooms, and sleeping areas. Hidden cameras are prohibited. So buy a doorbell camera if you want one, place it outside, and write its location into the listing.

**Confirm with:** the local fire marshal or building department on alarm type, placement, and extinguisher rules; the insurer on grills, fire features, water access, and any pool or hot tub; Airbnb's current device page before you mount anything.

### OP.6.5 Durability, cleaning time, and replacement cost

Every item you buy has three prices: the purchase price, the minutes it adds to each turnover, and the cost of replacing it. The cheapest sofa is often the most expensive one by year three.

A few buying rules that hold up:

- Buy performance or washable fabrics in the living room. Removable covers let the cleaner handle a spill without a service call.
- Put waterproof encasements on every mattress on day one. A stained mattress is a replacement; a stained encasement is a wash.
- Buy white or one-color bedding in one line, so sets mix and match. A cleaner shouldn't need a chart to make a bed.
- Choose dishware you can rebuy by the piece for years. Save the breakage replacements on a spares shelf.
- Avoid glass tabletops, delicate lamps, and anything that needs assembly tools after a move.
- Choose lighting that works without apps. A guest shouldn't need a login to turn on a lamp.

Think in cleaning minutes too. Twelve decorative pillows add time to every turnover. A bunk room with fitted-sheet-friendly mattresses saves time. Your cleaner is paid per turnover, so extra minutes become either rushed work or a higher rate later.

### OP.6.6 The budget: purchase plan, spares, linen cycle, and a reserve

The furnishing budget is a fixed input from the underwriting. Maya and Chris set $32,000 for Cedar Ridge, and the financing table counts it in total cash in ($142,688 on the DSCR quote). Your job is to spend it well, not to find more.

Build the plan in five layers, in this order:

1. Safety and access items. These are fixed; nothing gets cut here.
2. Beds and mattresses, because they carry the listing and the reviews.
3. Linens and towels, sized to a cycle.
4. Kitchen, dining, living, work space, outdoor.
5. Delivery, assembly, spares, owner storage, and a contingency line.

The linen cycle sets how much bedding you buy. A common setup is three sets per bed: one on the bed, one in the wash, one clean on the shelf. Three sets let a same-day turnover happen even if the laundry falls behind. Towels follow the guest count, not the bed count: three sets per guest.

A breakage reserve covers what guests break and what wears out. In year one, it's mostly glasses, a lamp, a stained towel, a scratched table. You can fund it two ways. Carry forward any unspent contingency from the purchase plan. Then earmark part of the monthly repairs line for replacements, so the money is there before the next break.

Two notes before the numbers. First, the plan spends the whole $32,000 on purchases, delivery, and a contingency line. B6 treats the $32,000 as 5-year property for the depreciation illustration. Some items, like linens and consumables, may be treated differently. Second, how each item is classified is the CPA's call, not yours.

**Confirm with:** the CPA on which purchases are capitalized furnishings, which are supplies, and how delivery and assembly costs are treated.

### OP.6.7 Worked example: Cedar Ridge

Cedar Ridge is a three-bedroom, two-bath house in a drive-to lake market, sleeps 8, no HOA. Maya and Chris plan about 10 personal-use days a year. The furnishing budget is $32,000. The base case expects 61 stays a year, averaging 3.5 nights.

**Guest profile (assumption).** Two related families or two couples with kids, arriving Friday, doing lake days, cooking most dinners. Midweek bookings in shoulder season lean toward remote workers and small groups. No parties; no pets at launch.

**Floor plan (assumption).** A main level with the kitchen, dining area, living room, primary bedroom, and bath 1. A walkout lower level with bedroom 2, a bunk room, and bath 2. A deck faces the lake.

**Sleeping layout.** Every bed type is listed exactly as installed.

**Linen cycle, step by step.**

- Beds: 1 king + 1 queen + 4 twins = 6 beds.
- Sheet sets at 3 per bed: 6 × 3 = 18 sets. Cost: (1 × 3 × $90) + (1 × 3 × $75) + (4 × 3 × $45) = $270 + $225 + $540 = $1,035.
- Duvet covers at 3 per bed: (3 × $70) + (3 × $60) + (12 × $40) = $210 + $180 + $480 = $870.
- Duvet inserts, one per bed plus a spare king: $120 + $100 + (4 × $60) + $120 = $580.
- Pillows: 4 on the king, 4 on the queen, 1 per twin = 12, plus 4 spares = 16 at $25 = $400. Protectors: 16 at $10 = $160.
- Bath towel sets at 3 per guest: 8 × 3 = 24 sets at $25 = $600.
- Lake towels: 16 at $15 = $240. Kitchen towels and extra bath mats: $120.
- Linen total: $1,035 + $870 + $580 + $400 + $160 + $600 + $240 + $120 = **$4,005**.

With 61 stays and all 6 beds stripped each turnover, the house runs 61 × 6 = 366 sheet-set changes a year. Spread across 18 sets, each set gets washed about 20 times a year. Plan on replacing linens on a rolling basis, not all at once.

The listing states that one closet is locked and not available to guests. Accurate listings cover what guests can't use as well as what they can.

### OP.6.8 Photo-ready checklist and room inventory for OP.1

Airbnb's [search explanation](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/39) says quality is a major factor in how listings appear. It says quality assessment includes listing content like photos and videos, ratings and reviews, host communication, and listing characteristics. Airbnb's resource center also notes that guests can [filter by amenities](https://www.airbnb.com/resources/hosting-homes/a/the-amenities-guests-want-25). An amenity you have but don't list can't show in a filtered search. An amenity you list but don't have becomes an accuracy problem. Airbnb can also ask hosts to [confirm photo accuracy](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/2881) by taking new photos of the space in person through the app. So the photos have to show the house as a guest will find it.

Photo-ready checklist (Day 20, the evening before photos):

- Every bed made in the standard set, the way the cleaner will make it every turnover.
- Every lamp and bulb working; blinds open and even.
- Every surface cleared except items the guest will actually find there.
- Safety equipment in place and visible where required (extinguisher, alarm locations noted).
- Doorbell camera location photographed for the disclosure; no camera anywhere indoors.
- Owner closet locked; no personal items in guest areas.
- Outdoor furniture set up as guests will use it; grill clean and covered.
- One shot of each bed type so the sleeping arrangement is visible.
- Work nook with the chair and lamp in place; internet speed test result saved.

Room inventory for Sam's OP.1 (a sample row per room; the full sheet lists every room):

### OP.6.10 Who confirms what

**Confirm with:** the local licensing office or building department on maximum occupancy, bedroom egress, and any permit condition tied to beds or guest count.
