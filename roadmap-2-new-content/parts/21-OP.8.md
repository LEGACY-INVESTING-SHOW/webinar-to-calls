### OP.8.0 Learning outcome

After this lesson you'll have a short operating playbook for your house. A backup cleaner or a new co-host can follow it without calling you. It has three parts:

- A turnover checklist with photo proof, supply levels, and a plan for a missed clean. The turnover is the clean between one guest's checkout and the next check-in.
- A guest message sequence, with a named person checking each message.
- An emergency matrix for seven kinds of incident.

You'll see all three run at Cedar Ridge: a normal turnover, a guest problem, and a real emergency.

Cedar Ridge is the course's hypothetical three-bedroom lake house. It sleeps eight in six beds and hosts about 61 stays a year in the base-case forecast. Maya and Chris own it and live about two hours away. Maya runs guest messaging and orders supplies. Chris handles approvals and bookkeeping, backs Maya up, and makes the owner calls in an emergency. A local turnover cleaner and an on-call handyman do the on-site work. The handyman also takes the after-hours calls. These roles come from the OP.7 role map.

One word needs care. In this lesson, "owner" on its own means Maya or Chris, the people who own the house. The one role responsible for an SOP is its "SOP owner".

### OP.8.1 What makes an SOP usable

A standard operating procedure (SOP) is a written routine that produces the same result no matter who follows it. Most short-term rental (STR) checklists fail that test. They say "clean kitchen" and leave the standard to the cleaner's mood.

Every SOP in this lesson has the same seven parts:

1. Trigger: what starts it (a checkout, a message, an alarm).
2. SOP owner: the one named role responsible for this SOP, taken from the OP.7 role map.
3. Steps, in order.
4. Standard: what "done" looks like, specifically.
5. Proof: a photo, a timestamp, or a reading.
6. Escalation: who to call, by when, if a step fails.
7. Record: where the result gets written down.

Keep each SOP to one page. If it runs longer, split it. The backup cleaner reads it in the driveway, not at a desk.

### OP.8.2 Turnover SOP with photo proof

The turnover is the SOP that runs most often. At Cedar Ridge it runs about 61 times a year, once for each base-case stay. Airbnb's [ground rules for hosts](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/2895) set cleanliness as an expectation and name the basics. Hosts should clean between every stay: laundry, trash, vacuuming or sweeping, and wiping surfaces. Your SOP turns that into a standard.

Trigger: guest checkout in the calendar. SOP owner: turnover cleaner (calendar access only, per OP.7). Window: checkout 10 a.m., check-in 4 p.m. (assumption), so six hours. The cleaner logs each step in the task app, the shared app where turnovers are scheduled (Sam's OP.4 shows where it fits).

| Step | Standard | Photo proof |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Arrive and log start | Start time recorded in the task app | Front door on arrival |
| 2. Walk-through before touching anything | Note damage, missing items, smoke smell, extra guests' signs, anything left behind | Any damage, before cleaning |
| 3. Strip all beds, start laundry | All 6 beds stripped whether used or not | None |
| 4. Kitchen | Dishes clean and put away, appliances wiped inside and out, fridge emptied of guest food, trash and recycling out | Counters, open fridge |
| 5. Bathrooms | Toilets, showers, sinks, mirrors, floors; hair check on every surface | Each bathroom, wide |
| 6. Bedrooms | Beds made with clean sets from the shelf, the same way every time; surfaces dusted, under beds checked | Each bed |
| 7. Living, dining, work nook | Surfaces, cushions, remotes, floors vacuumed or mopped | Each room, wide |
| 8. Deck and grill | Furniture set, grill grates clean, cover on, no guest trash | Deck, grill |
| 9. Restock | Consumables to par (OP.8.3) | Supply shelf |
| 10. Safety check | Alarms present, not disabled, status light normal; extinguishers in place; no indoor device added; noise monitor in its disclosed spot; doorbell camera aimed only at the entry; owner closet (the owners' locked storage) locked | Each alarm's status light; check logged |
| 11. Final walk and lock | Lights off, thermostat to the vacancy setting written in the SOP, doors locked | Front door on exit |
| 12. Report | Checklist complete, damage and low-stock items flagged | All photos uploaded |

Photos serve two purposes. Maya and Chris live two hours away, so the photos let Maya check the work without driving over. And the photos are evidence if a guest later reports a problem. The retained lesson 9.3 Dealing With Damages covers how to use damage photos in a claim.

Airbnb's [safety requirements](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/2904) say that if smoke and carbon monoxide (CO) alarms are present, hosts must keep them working and test them regularly. At every turnover, the cleaner looks at each alarm. It must be in place, not disabled, and showing a normal status light. The cleaner logs that visual check. The full button test goes in the monthly inspection, which the handyman does. Set the test schedule with the alarm manufacturer's instructions and your local fire department. Ask the fire department, too, whether this house must have smoke and CO alarms, and where (OP.8.10).

### OP.8.3 Linen and consumable inventory

Par level means the amount you keep on hand. When stock falls to the reorder point, someone orders more. Write both numbers on the shelf label so nobody has to guess.

Linens follow the OP.6 cycle. Cedar Ridge owns 18 sheet sets (three per bed for six beds) and 24 towel sets (three per guest for eight guests). After a turnover, one set per bed is on the bed, one is in the wash, and one is on the shelf. So the shelf should hold 6 clean sheet sets and 8 clean towel sets once the beds are made. The cleaner counts the shelf at every turnover.

For towels, a count of 7 is a flag, not an order. A set may still be in the dryer or pulled for a stain. At 6 towel sets (assumption), Maya orders replacements. At that count, sets are wearing out or going missing faster than laundry refills the shelf. Sheets have no buffer at this par. A count of 5 sheet sets (assumption) is both a flag and an order.

Consumables run about $25 per stay at Cedar Ridge ($1,525 a year across 61 stays). Keep stock for four stays on site (assumption), about $100 worth, and reorder at two stays. That leaves room for a back-to-back weekend without a supply run. On each shelf label, write what one stay uses (for example, how many rolls of toilet paper), so the cleaner can count stays.

| Item | Par | Reorder at | Counted by | Ordered by |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Toilet paper, paper towels | 4 stays | 2 stays | Cleaner | Maya |
| Soap, shampoo, conditioner | 4 stays | 2 stays | Cleaner | Maya |
| Dish soap, dishwasher pods, sponges | 4 stays | 2 stays | Cleaner | Maya |
| Trash bags | 4 stays | 2 stays | Cleaner | Maya |
| Coffee, tea, sugar, oil, salt, pepper | 4 stays | 2 stays | Cleaner | Maya |
| Laundry detergent | 4 stays | 2 stays | Cleaner | Maya |
| Sheet sets on shelf | 6 sets (1 per bed) | 5 sets | Cleaner | Maya |
| Towel sets on shelf | 8 sets | 6 sets | Cleaner | Maya |

Replacements follow the approval limits from OP.6 and OP.7. The cleaner replaces a broken glass or a stained towel from the spares shelf and notes it. Anything over $200 (assumption) needs approval from Maya or Chris before purchase. The cleaner never pays for replacements out of pocket without a written OK.

### OP.8.4 Missed-clean escalation

The most visible everyday failure is a guest arriving to a dirty house. Often it starts with a cleaner who doesn't show up and a host who finds out at 3:30 p.m. The escalation ladder catches it by late morning.

| Time on turnover day | Check | If it fails | Who acts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8 p.m. the night before | Cleaner confirms tomorrow's turnover in the task app | Maya calls the cleaner | Maya |
| 9 p.m. the night before | Cleaner still not confirmed | Maya alerts the backup cleaner to hold the slot | Maya |
| 11:00 a.m. | Arrival photo received | Call the cleaner; if no answer in 15 minutes, send the backup | Maya |
| 11:30 a.m. | Backup cleaner en route | Tell the arriving guest there may be a short delay; offer a later check-in time | Maya |
| 2:30 p.m. | Turnover more than half done (photo count) | Decide: delay check-in, or send a second cleaner | Maya |
| 3:45 p.m. | All photos in, report complete | Maya reviews photos; if anything is missing, she messages the guest a delay before 4 p.m. | Maya |
| After | Missed clean recorded | Second miss in 90 days triggers a review of whether to keep this cleaner | Chris |

The 11:00 a.m. row is a deadline, not a start time. In the Sunday example below, the arrival photo comes in at 10:25. A backup sent at 11:15 may not finish by 4 p.m. The ladder's job is to find out early enough to warn the guest and offer a later check-in.

The backup cleaner is paid a same-day premium, $200 instead of $150 (assumption). If that happens four times a year (assumption), it costs 4 × $50 = $200 more. That's cheap next to a refund and a one-star cleanliness review.

### OP.8.5 Message sequence from booking to checkout

Every guest gets the same core messages at the same moments. Automation sends most of them. At Cedar Ridge, Maya checks every one.

Airbnb lets hosts send [scheduled quick replies](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/2897) automatically, triggered by events like a new booking, check-in, or checkout. The same help page says hosts can skip, send early, or edit an upcoming scheduled message without changing the template. Airbnb also offers suggested quick replies, which use AI to suggest one of your existing replies for common questions.

| When | Message | Sent by | What must be right |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 minutes after booking | Thanks, what to expect, house rules summary, and a request to confirm how many people are coming | Scheduled | Names the correct guest count and no-party rule |
| 7 days before | Trip prep: directions, parking, lake access rules, what to bring | Scheduled | Seasonal details current |
| 1 day before, 10 a.m. | Check-in details: address, door code, Wi-Fi, where the house manual is | Scheduled; checked in the Monday review (edit or skip if wrong) | Correct unique code for this stay |
| Check-in day, evening | Short check-in follow-up: "Anything missing?" | Manual | Sent by a person who can act on the answer |
| Mid-stay (stays of 4+ nights) | Offer a restock or answer questions | Manual | Optional; skip for short stays |
| 1 day before checkout | Checkout steps: dishes, trash, thermostat, lock | Scheduled | Steps match the cleaner's expectations |
| After checkout | Thanks for staying | Scheduled | Neutral wording: nothing offered in exchange for a review (OP.9) |

If the guest's count doesn't match the booking, Maya sorts it out with the guest before arrival. The door code goes out the day before, in the check-in details message. On turnover day, Maya reviews the cleaner's final photos before the 4 p.m. check-in (OP.8.4).

Human review is a written weekly step, not a hope. Every Monday, Maya opens the list of upcoming scheduled messages for the week. She checks each one against the booking: guest count, dates, the lock code, and any special arrangement. A booking made after Monday for an arrival before next Monday gets the same check the day it's booked.

Automation needs limits. Write these into the SOP and into any AI messaging tool you use:

- No automated or AI reply goes to a message about smoke, fire, gas, alarms, leaks, injury, police, lockouts, or refunds. A person answers those immediately.
- The scheduled replies above are triggered by events like a booking or checkout. So this first limit matters most for AI-suggested replies and any outside tool that answers what a guest writes (SR.5, OP.4).
- No automated message sends a door code that changed after the check-in message went out (for example, a code reset during a lockout). A person sends it after checking the lock.
- A person replies within 30 minutes (assumption) to any guest message, even if an automated acknowledgment went first.
- An AI suggestion is a draft. A person approves it before it sends, unless it's an approved template for a routine question.
- Any exception granted (early check-in, late checkout, extra guest) gets written into the booking notes so the cleaner sees it.

SR.5 compares AI messaging tools on these criteria. Sam's OP.4 shows where messages and tasks live in the tech stack.

### OP.8.6 Emergency matrix

An emergency matrix tells the person on the spot what to do first and who to call. It also says who can stop work and what to record. Write it for the guest and the team together, and put the guest's part in the house manual.

Airbnb's help center says to contact [local emergency services](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/3063) first if there's an emergency in progress. Airbnb also describes an [Urgent Support Line](https://news.airbnb.com/expanding-the-urgent-support-line) in its app for hosts and guests during active reservations. Neither replaces 911, and neither replaces your own on-call person. So the house manual tells guests the order: get out, call 911, then call the on-call number.

Stop-work authority means the right to halt a turnover, a repair, or the next booking because something isn't safe. At Cedar Ridge, anyone on the team can stop work for safety. Only an owner (Maya or Chris) can reopen the calendar, and only after the right professional signs off.

At Cedar Ridge, "owner" in the matrix means Maya or Chris. The "messaging owner" is Maya. The "after-hours contact" and the "on-call number" are the handyman (OP.7).

| Incident | First action (guest or first person on site) | Who responds | Stop-work authority | Record |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fire or smoke | Get everyone out, then call 911 from outside | 911; after-hours contact goes to site; owner notified | Anyone; owner blocks the calendar | Incident report, fire department report number, photos, insurer notice |
| CO alarm | Get everyone out to fresh air, then call 911 | 911 or fire department; gas supplier; licensed heating and cooling (HVAC) tech | Anyone; no one re-enters until the fire department clears the scene; the house stays closed until the HVAC tech and gas supplier clear it in writing | Incident report, alarm location, readings if given, technician report |
| Water leak | Shut off water at the main (location marked in manual); move away from electrical | Handyman; plumber; owner | Handyman or owner | Photos, shutoff time, plumber invoice, insurer notice if damage |
| Lockout | Guest messages or calls the on-call number | Messaging owner resends or resets code; handyman on site if lock fails | n/a | Time locked out, cause, fix |
| Injury | Call 911 for anything serious; first aid kit location in manual | 911; owner notified immediately | Owner decides whether the area stays closed | Incident report (facts only), photos of the area, insurer notice |
| Power, water, or internet outage | Check the utility's outage page or house manual steps | Messaging owner; handyman if on-site check needed | Owner decides whether the stay continues | Outage times, utility reference, any guest credit |
| Severe weather | Follow official local warnings; shelter location in manual | Messaging owner sends a warning message; handyman after the storm | Owner decides on arrivals and relocations | Warning time, messages sent, damage photos |

Keep a laminated contact sheet inside the owner closet door and a copy in the house manual. It lists 911, non-emergency police and fire, the utilities, and the water shutoff location. It also lists the on-call phone, handyman, plumber, HVAC company, and insurer's claim line. Fill in the real local numbers before the first booking.

### OP.8.7 Guest privacy and safety devices in the SOPs

Privacy rules belong in the SOPs, because the people who break them are often well-meaning. A cleaner might want to set up a "nanny cam" to watch for parties. That's not allowed.

Airbnb's [device rules](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/3061) prohibit security cameras and recording devices that monitor indoor spaces, even if they're off. Exterior cameras must be disclosed in the listing and can't watch indoor spaces or areas like an enclosed outdoor shower. Noise decibel monitors that don't record sound are allowed in common spaces if disclosed, and not in bedrooms, bathrooms, or sleeping areas.

Cedar Ridge has a doorbell camera and a noise monitor (OP.6). Step 10 of the turnover SOP carries three device checks. The cleaner confirms no new device has appeared indoors. The noise monitor is still in its disclosed common-area spot. The doorbell camera still points only at the entry.

Add one line to the messaging SOP (OP.8.5) too: camera footage and guest details stay in the platform or the device's own app. Nobody downloads them to a personal phone or shares them outside the team. The retained lesson 9.4 How to Avoid Parties & Smoking covers the party-prevention side.

### OP.8.8 Worked example: three walk-throughs at Cedar Ridge

**Normal turnover (Sunday).** A family of seven checks out at 10:00 a.m. after a three-night stay. The next group of eight arrives at 4:00 p.m. Their check-in details, with the door code, went out Saturday at 10 a.m.

- 8:05 p.m. Saturday: the cleaner confirms in the task app.
- 10:25 a.m.: arrival photo received. The walk-through finds one chipped mug and a lake towel with a stain.
- 10:40 a.m.: all six beds stripped and laundry started. Six used sets go to the wash. When the beds are made in step 6, six clean sets come off the shelf. That leaves 18 - 6 - 6 = 6 clean sets on the shelf, one per bed, so no flag.
- 10:45 a.m. to 1:50 p.m.: kitchen, bathrooms, bedrooms, living areas, deck (about 3 hours, assumption).
- 1:55 p.m.: restock. Consumables for one stay cost about $25; the shelf drops from four stays to three, above the reorder point of two.
- 2:00 p.m.: the mug is replaced from the spares shelf and the towel goes to the rag bin (worn linens kept for cleaning). Both are logged. Total under $200, so no approval is needed.
- 2:10 p.m.: safety check. All alarms in place, extinguishers mounted, no indoor devices, noise monitor and doorbell camera in place, owner closet locked.
- 2:20 p.m.: final photos uploaded. Maya reviews them at 2:45 p.m. The house is ready for the 4 p.m. check-in.
- Money: the guest's $175 cleaning fee covers the $150 cleaner plus about $25 of supplies for the stay.

**Guest problem (Tuesday).** A guest on a four-night midweek stay messages at 9:05 a.m.: "Internet is down. I have work calls today." The listing advertises a dedicated workspace.

- 9:05 a.m.: the message mentions no safety words, so an automated acknowledgment is allowed. The SOP still requires a person within 30 minutes (assumption). Maya replies at 9:15 a.m. with the house manual's reset steps: unplug the router for 30 seconds, wait five minutes.
- 9:35 a.m.: still down. Maya checks the internet provider's outage page. It shows an area outage with an estimated fix by 6 p.m.
- 9:40 a.m.: Maya tells the guest what she knows and when she'll update them. She doesn't guess at a restore time the provider hasn't given.
- 10:00 a.m.: Maya offers a $100 goodwill credit. That's her OP.7 goodwill limit (assumption). She pays it through the platform, not in cash, and doesn't tie it to a review. Airbnb's review policy (covered and sourced in OP.9) prohibits offering something of value in exchange for a review.
- 6:20 p.m.: service returns. Maya confirms with the guest.
- Record: outage times, provider reference, the credit, and one root-cause note (what failed and how to prevent it). An advertised workspace with one internet line has no backup. Chris gets a quote for a cellular backup router. It's above $200 (assumption), so it goes on the list of purchases Maya and Chris decide at their month-end review.

**Emergency (Saturday, 1:40 a.m.).** A guest calls the after-hours number: the CO alarm on the lower level is sounding.

- 1:40 a.m.: the handyman answers, per the OP.7 after-hours row. His first words follow the matrix: "Get everyone outside now, then call 911." He stays on the line until all eight guests are outside.
- 1:44 a.m.: the guest calls 911. The handyman texts Chris. The guest called the after-hours number before 911, and that cost four minutes. The house manual should tell guests to get out and call 911 first, then call the on-call number.
- 1:48 a.m.: the handyman leaves for the house (about 35 minutes away, assumption). He doesn't go inside; the fire department controls the scene.
- 2:05 a.m.: the fire department arrives. They find elevated CO near the furnace (a propane furnace, assumption) and shut off the gas supply. Nobody goes back in tonight.
- 2:15 a.m.: Chris contacts Airbnb's urgent support line and messages the guest. Relocation costs more than any team member's spending limit, so Chris, as an owner, approves it himself. He books a nearby hotel.
- 2:30 a.m.: stop-work. The handyman tapes a "do not occupy" notice to the front door. Chris blocks the open dates so no new bookings come in, and flags Sunday afternoon's booking for a decision.
- 8:00 a.m.: a licensed HVAC technician inspects the furnace. The house stays closed until the technician and the gas supplier clear it in writing.
- Record: incident report with times, alarm location, fire department report number, HVAC findings, hotel receipt, and messages sent. Chris notifies the insurer the same day.
- Follow-up: Chris decides whether to cancel or relocate Sunday's guests based on the technician's timeline. He adds a furnace service check before each heating season to the handyman's inspection list.

**Decision rules**

- If a step has no named owner, the SOP isn't finished.
- If the backup hasn't done the SOP once for real or in a paid trial, they aren't a backup yet. A paid trial means the backup does one paid turnover alongside you or the regular cleaner.
- If an automated message can send a changed door code or reply to a safety message, change the setting before the next booking.
- Review every SOP after any incident and at least every six months (assumption).

### OP.8.10 Who confirms what

**Confirm with:** the local fire department on the first actions for fire and CO alarms at this house. Ask whether and where this house must have smoke and CO alarms, and how often to test them.

**Confirm with:** the insurer on incident notice deadlines, required records, and whether relocation costs are covered.

**Confirm with:** the utilities (gas or propane, electric, water) on emergency numbers and shutoff procedures.

**Confirm with:** Airbnb's current help pages on scheduled messages, safety requirements, and device rules before you rely on them. These pages change.
