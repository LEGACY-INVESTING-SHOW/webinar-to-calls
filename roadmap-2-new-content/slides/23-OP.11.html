This lesson is about buying a second property. But we're not going to open a single listing. We're going to open the books on the first one.

If property one earned about what you forecast, a second similar house repeats a plan you've already tested. If it didn't, a second house doubles a plan that hasn't worked yet, and it adds a second loan.

By the end of this lesson you'll do three things. You'll build a trailing-12-month report and set it beside your original underwriting. What you walk away with is a dated yes, not yet, or no, with the numbers that produced it. We'll use Cedar Ridge at the end of year 2. Everything here is education only. Your lender, CPA, and insurer confirm the real answers.

Slide 2 · Property two repeats a tested plan only if property one earned what you forecast

Most people start the property-two conversation with a listing. I want you to start it with a bank statement.

There are two jobs here. The first is proof. You need to know what property one actually earned, what it actually cost, and how much cash is left after both. The underwriting you did with Sam in PA.4 was a forecast. Twelve months of operating records are evidence. This lesson is about reading the evidence before you commit more money.

The second job is liquidity. Buying property two usually means pulling cash from somewhere. That's savings, a refinance of property one, or a loan against the house you live in. Each of those changes your monthly obligations and what you can survive in a slow season.

Keep one rule in mind for the rest of the lesson. A second property funded with borrowed money needs a wider margin than one funded with savings.

Sam's OP.10 lesson walks you through the 30-day and 90-day review. Bring that report here. It gives you the categories you track and the way you pull the data. The trailing 12 is the same review stretched to a full year, so every season shows up in it.

Build it from records, not memory. Payouts and fees come from each platform's earnings reports. Expenses come from the property's bank account and card. Booked nights, stays, and your average nightly rate come from your property management software or the platform calendar.

Slide 4 · Four checks explain the gap between your underwriting and the actual year

Once you have the variance column, you're looking for four things.

First, split any revenue miss into rate and occupancy. They point to different fixes. A rate problem often traces to pricing settings or competition. An occupancy problem can trace to calendar gaps, minimum stays, reviews, or demand. Sam's OP.5 lesson covers the pricing tools. Here you only need to know which lever moved.

Second, find the expense lines that grew. Utilities, insurance renewals, and repairs are the usual ones. One large repair in the year may be a one-off. A pattern of repairs is a budget problem.

Third, check whether you actually funded the capital reserve. If that 5% stayed in the operating account and got spent, your cash flow looks better than it really is.

Fourth, compare debt service. The number that matters most is NOI. If trailing NOI is well under your base case, property two waits until you know why.

A cash-out refinance is a new, bigger loan, priced in five steps from a written quote

  1. New loan = appraised value × the lender's maximum loan-to-value (LTV).
  2. Equity removed = new loan - current loan balance.
  3. Cash out = equity removed - refinance closing costs.
  4. New payment = principal and interest on the new loan at the new rate and term.
  5. New cash flow = NOI - new annual principal and interest.
Watch three things

The 30-year clock restarts. Closing costs can eat a large share of a small cash-out. A bigger loan makes a weak year worse, so run weak-case NOI too.

OP.11 · Refinancing, HELOCs & Property #2§ OP.11.35 / 20
Slide 5 · A cash-out refinance is a new, bigger loan, priced in five steps from a written quote

A cash-out refinance can feel like free money from appreciation. In practice you're replacing your loan with a bigger one, with a new payment, a new term, and new closing costs.

Walk through the math in this order, every time. New loan, equity removed, cash out, new payment, new cash flow.

Every input comes from a written lender quote, not a rule of thumb. The appraisal may come in lower than your estimate. The lender may cap loan-to-value lower than you expect for a short-term rental. It may also apply its own coverage ratio, the way we saw in PA.6. Compare quotes the way PA.7 taught, using the CFPB's guide to comparing Loan Estimates where the lender issues one.

Then watch three things. The term resets, so you pay down principal more slowly. Closing costs can eat a big share when the equity you remove is small. And because the loan is bigger, a weak year gets worse.

A HELOC is secured by your own home, and the lender can freeze it when you need it

How it works (CFPB)

  • A revolving line secured by a home you own, usually your primary residence
  • A draw period (10 years in the CFPB example), then repayment
  • At the end of the draw you may repay over time or owe it all at once, depending on the plan
  • A variable rate tied to a public index, such as a prime rate

Two risks in plain words

  • Your home is the collateral. If you can't repay, you could lose it.
  • The lender can generally freeze or cut the line if your home's value drops significantly or your finances change materially.
Do not do this

Don't count undrawn HELOC capacity as a reserve. A market dip can remove it at the worst time.

OP.11 · Refinancing, HELOCs & Property #2§ OP.11.46 / 20
Slide 6 · A HELOC is secured by your own home, and the lender can freeze it when you need it

A home equity line of credit is a revolving line secured by a home you already own. For most people that's the house they live in.

The CFPB describes two phases. During the draw period, and they use 10 years as an example, you can borrow up to your limit. Many plans base the minimum payment on the balance. When the draw period ends, repayment starts. Depending on the plan, you may repay over time or owe the whole balance at once. The rate is usually variable, so price it at today's rate and at a higher stress rate. Read the lender's disclosures before you model anything.

Now the two risks. Your home is the collateral. And the lender can generally freeze or cut the line if your home's value drops a lot.

There's also a tax question for your CPA. IRS Publication 936 limits the mortgage interest deduction when the money doesn't buy, build, or substantially improve the home securing it. A rental down payment doesn't meet that test. Don't assume either answer in your model.

Slide 7 · A second house in the same market shares your team but stacks your risk

Once the numbers work, the operating question starts. Can your team run two properties as well as it runs one?

A second house in the same market shares a lot. The same cleaner, handyman, and supply run might cover both. You already know the permit process, the seasons, and the guest profile. You can reuse your OP.8 playbook with small edits. The cost is concentration. One bad season, one new ordinance, or one storm now hits both houses at once.

A new market spreads that risk. But the learning curve starts over. You'll need a fresh market check using Sam's PA.2 lesson, a new address-level permission review with resource SR.1, a new local team, and new vendor backups.

And your first-year results from property one don't tell you much about a market you've never operated in. Treat a new market like a first property on the operating side.

Slide 18 · Your CPA, lenders, insurer, and licensing office each confirm a different piece

Here's who confirms what.

Your bookkeeper or CPA confirms the trailing 12 matches the books and the bank, and that repairs and improvements are classified correctly.

The refinance lender confirms, in writing, the appraised value, the maximum loan-to-value for a short-term rental, and how they define the coverage ratio. Get the rate, points, costs, reserves, and any prepayment penalty on your current loan too.

The HELOC lender's plan disclosures tell you the limit, the draw and repayment periods, the index and margin, the minimum payment, and the conditions that let them freeze or reduce the line.

Your CPA answers the interest question from Publication 936, and how a second property affects your depreciation and loss position. Resource SR.3 helps with that conversation.

The licensing office or an STR attorney checks property two's exact address. And your insurer confirms the house can be covered for short-term rental use.

These are the sources behind this lesson.

The three CFPB pages cover how a HELOC works: the draw and repayment periods, the variable rate tied to an index, the disclosures the lender must give you, and the conditions that let a lender freeze or reduce the line. IRS Publication 936 covers the limit on deducting home equity interest as mortgage interest.

The CFPB Loan Estimate guide is how you compare written refinance quotes. Fannie Mae's reserve rules show that a lender can require reserves on top of the down payment. Airbnb's help page covers co-host permission levels.

The Cedar Ridge numbers come from our recurring example file, cases B1, B2, B4, and B7. The trailing-12, HELOC, and pass-rule figures are new assumptions I built for teaching.

Check each rule against the current source before you rely on it. Lender terms and plan disclosures vary, so your own written quotes come first.
