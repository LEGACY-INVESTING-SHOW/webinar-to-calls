This lesson is about reading a DSCR loan quote the way the lender reads it. DSCR stands for debt service coverage ratio.

By the end you'll know which income the lender puts on top of the ratio and which payment goes underneath. You'll see why a gross-based ratio well above 1.0 can still leave you losing money every month. And you'll leave with two tools for this week. One is a written question list for two lenders. The other is a table for comparing their written offers on the same house.

We'll use Cedar Ridge the whole way through. Maya and Chris are buying it for $385,000 with 25% down. Every number you see on Cedar Ridge is an assumption from our course example. Your job is to get your own quote in writing.

Slide 2 · A DSCR lender swaps your pay stubs for the property's income, but it still checks you

The title, "How They Qualify the Property, Not You," is shorthand. It points at the main difference from a regular mortgage. A DSCR lender sizes the loan mostly on the property's rental income, not your pay stubs.

But the lender still looks at you. Kiavi is a lender that publishes its DSCR terms online. Its guide to DSCR rental loans says it considers the borrower's credit score, the down payment available, and the property's coverage ratio. So expect the list on this slide.

Here's the fair way to say it. The property's income replaces your salary in the math. It doesn't mean nobody asks about you. One term on the list: loan-to-value, or LTV, is the loan as a percent of the price. Put 25% down and your LTV is 75%.

One more thing. Kiavi's terms are one lender's terms. Other lenders publish different minimums, and the same lender can change its terms. I use Kiavi's pages as examples because they're public. That isn't an endorsement. Treat them as examples, then get your own written quote.

Slide 3 · Your DSCR (debt service coverage ratio) moves with three choices the lender makes

DSCR stands for debt service coverage ratio. In plain words, it's how many dollars of property income the lender counts for every dollar of loan payment.

So DSCR is the income the lender counts, divided by the loan payment. The top number is some version of income. The bottom number is some version of the payment. Kiavi describes a common version: gross rent divided by PITIA. PITIA is principal, interest, property taxes, insurance, and any HOA dues. Kiavi's material also says lenders calculate it with different inputs. Some use gross rent. Some use a vacancy-adjusted figure, meaning income cut down for empty time. Some put flood insurance in the payment, while others leave it out.

That flexibility is the whole problem. The same house can produce very different ratios depending on these three choices.

So before you compare any two quotes, ask each lender for its definition in writing. Pricing often changes with the ratio too, so the definition can move your rate, not just your approval.

Slide 5 · The same house scores 2.42, 1.94, or 1.18, depending on the lender's definition

Now three different incomes on top of the same $30,684.

Ratio 1 uses gross booking revenue. Base-case gross is $74,275: 212 booked nights at a $300 ADR, which is average daily rate, plus $10,675 of cleaning fees. Divide by $30,684 and you get 2.42.

Ratio 2 haircuts that revenue by 20%. Some lenders discount projected STR income. The 20% here is my assumption, not any lender's rule. That gives $59,420, and a ratio of 1.94.

Ratio 3 uses net operating income, which is revenue after operating costs. Operating costs are $45,066, so NOI is $29,209. Tax and insurance are already subtracted in NOI, and PITIA includes them. So I add them back, or they'd be subtracted twice. That's $36,259, and a ratio of 1.18.

Same house, same loan, same year. The ratio runs from 1.18 to 2.42. The lender's written definition decides which one counts.

Now run the weak case. The course example's weak case drops ADR from $300 to $265. Occupancy falls from about 58%, which is the base case's 212 nights, to 50%. Gross revenue falls to $57,330, and NOI falls to $18,162.

Look at the spread. On the gross definition, the weak case still shows 1.87. On the haircut version, 1.49. On the NOI version, 0.82. That last one means the income the lender counts doesn't even cover the payment.

Three lenders could look at the same weak year and reach three different answers. One would say this loan is strong. One would say it fails.

That's why I keep saying: get the definition in writing. And it's why the next slide matters. A high number on the gross definition can hide a real problem for you as the owner.

Slide 7 · A lender ratio of 1.87 can sit next to a loss of $456 a month

Look at the weak case on the gross definition: 1.87. That sounds comfortable. It says the house brings in almost twice its loan payment.

Here's what Maya and Chris actually keep. Weak-case NOI is $18,162. Annual principal and interest on the DSCR loan is $23,637. Tax and insurance are already subtracted in NOI, so cash flow is NOI minus principal and interest. That's minus $5,475 a year, about minus $456 a month.

A lender ratio of 1.87 and an owner losing $456 a month. Both are true at once.

The reason is simple. A gross ratio ignores the platform fee, the cleaner, utilities, supplies, software, repairs, and the capital reserve. An STR carries far more operating cost than a long-term rental where the tenant pays utilities. On Cedar Ridge, base-case operating costs are $45,066. The gross ratio never sees them.

And even the base case is thin: $5,572 a year, about $464 a month.

Notice the NOI-based ratio doesn't have this problem. It's 1.18 in the base case, when they make money, and 0.82 in the weak case, when they lose it. It's the gross ratios that can look healthy while you lose money.

So keep two tools for two questions. And remember, it's the gross-based ratios that can pass while you lose money.

The lender's ratio answers one question only: will this lender make this loan at this size? It's built to protect the loan. It isn't built to protect your return.

Your own NOI and cash flow answer the other question: should you buy this house? That's where every operating cost shows up, in both the base case and the weak case.

The definition does more than pass or fail you. It can shrink the loan.

Say a lender uses the NOI-based definition and requires a minimum of 1.20. That minimum is my assumption. The most PITIA it will accept is $36,259 divided by 1.20, which is $30,216 a year. Take out $7,050 of tax and insurance. The most principal and interest it allows is $23,166 a year. At 7.25% over 30 years, that supports a loan of about $282,989. Any mortgage calculator, or a spreadsheet PV function, does that last step.

Maya and Chris asked for $288,750. This lender would cut the loan by about $5,761. They'd bring that much more cash to closing.

Now flip it. Under the 80% gross definition with a 1.25 minimum, also an assumption, the ratio supports roughly $494,600 of loan. That's more than the house is worth. So that lender's cap is its maximum LTV, say 75%, or $288,750. The LTV sets the loan, not the ratio.

So ask each lender for the maximum loan its definition supports on your numbers. And this used Maya and Chris's own income. The lender may use a lower number, which shrinks the loan further.

Slide 10 · With no booking history, the lender picks the income source, and it usually leans low

Cedar Ridge has no booking history under Maya and Chris. Neither will most houses you buy to convert. So where does the lender's income number come from?

Each lender picks its own source. Kiavi's rental loan page says the qualifying rent is the lower of 110% of appraised market rent or a valid lease rent. That structure fits a long-term lease, so ask how it applies to nightly bookings. Kiavi also publishes that it finances STRs in some markets and lets borrowers qualify on nightly revenue. That page doesn't say which data source or haircut it applies. Ask.

Some lenders accept a report from an STR data provider. On an existing STR, a lender may want 12 or 24 months of statements.

The lender's number will rarely match your pro forma. It shouldn't. The lender is protecting a loan, so it usually leans lower on income. When the lender's income is lower than yours, don't argue the loan up. Ask whether your pro forma is the one that's too high.

Slide 12 · A 3-2-1 penalty on a year-two refinance would give up $5,659 of the cash-out

Here's why the prepayment penalty matters for Cedar Ridge. The course example assumes Maya and Chris refinance at month 24 to pull cash back out. OP.11 covers how. The DSCR balance then is $282,951.

This is a what-if. The Cedar Ridge quote doesn't state a penalty, so suppose it carried a 3-2-1 structure. That's 3% of the balance in year one, 2% in year two, 1% in year three, then nothing. It's one common shape, not a standard.

A payoff in month 24 is still in year two. So the penalty is 2% of $282,951, which is $5,659.

The planned refinance shows $15,725 of cash out. That's what's left after the new, larger loan pays off the old one and the refinance costs. Subtract the penalty and it drops to $10,066. Wait one more month, into year three, and the penalty falls to 1%.

That's a big swing for a one-month timing choice. State law and loan type can also limit these penalties. Ask the lender how its penalty applies in your state, and write the schedule down by year.

Slide 14 · Send the same 12 written questions to two lenders, on the same house, on the same day

This is the first tool you take away. Send these same questions, in writing, to at least two lenders. Same house. Same day if you can.

Why the same day? Rates change daily. Quotes from different days can differ for reasons that have nothing to do with the lender.

The first four questions are the ones people skip. The formula. The income source. The minimum ratio. The maximum loan on your numbers. Those four decide whether the loan even happens at the size you need.

The rest decide the cost and the strings. Points and fees. The prepayment schedule. Reserves. The LLC and who guarantees. Permit proof. The appraiser's STR experience. And how long the rate lock lasts.

Three terms to define. Your credit tier is the score band the lender prices you in. A Loan Estimate is the standard cost form on consumer mortgages; more on that in a minute. A rate lock holds your quoted rate for a set number of days while you close.

If a lender won't answer in writing, that's an answer too.
