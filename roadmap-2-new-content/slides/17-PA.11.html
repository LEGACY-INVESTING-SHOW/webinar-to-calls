Slide 2 · Money, documents, and control change hands at closing, and they have to move in order

Closing looks like a pile of signatures. Underneath, three things move.

Money moves from you and your lender to the seller, the seller's lender, the title company, and a list of service providers.

Documents move ownership to you and give your lender a claim on the house.

Control moves too. Keys, codes, accounts, records, and the right to occupy.

A title company or a closing attorney runs the closing. I'll call the person handling your file there the settlement agent.

Most closing problems come from one of those three getting ahead of the others. You wire money before you've checked the figures. You sign before the walkthrough. You get the deed but not the lock codes.

This lesson puts them in order: check the figures, walk the house, review the documents, verify the wire by voice, and sign what matches. Then collect control before you leave. Ask your settlement agent early whether your funds must arrive before or after you sign.

Slide 3 · A DSCR loan may not come with a Closing Disclosure, so ask which document you'll get

For most consumer mortgages, the lender must give you a Closing Disclosure. That's its final form listing your loan terms and costs. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, the CFPB, says you must receive it at least three business days before closing. That window lets you compare it with your Loan Estimate, the lender's early estimate. Don't close until you've received and reviewed it.

Investment property adds a wrinkle. The CFPB's official commentary treats credit to buy a non-owner-occupied rental as business-purpose credit. That falls outside many of the consumer disclosure rules. A debt service coverage ratio, or DSCR, loan is sized mostly on the property's income. It's the usual case here. So its lender may give you a title company settlement statement instead.

A vacation home the owner occupies more than 14 days in the coming year doesn't get that treatment. So give your lender an honest personal-use number. Our example buyers, Maya and Chris, plan about 10 days.

Ask your lender early, in writing: which document will I get, and when? Ask for final figures three business days ahead anyway.

Walk the house against your PA.9 tracker before you sign anything

  • Seller repairs: see the septic work and the new well UV (ultraviolet) system, and get both invoices.
  • Included items: furniture, appliances, dock equipment, anything else in the contract.
  • New damage: water stains, broken windows, storm damage that wasn't there at inspection.
  • Systems: run every tap, flush toilets, test HVAC in both modes, check the water heater.
  • Photos and meters: photograph each room; write down utility meter readings with the time.

Don't sign and hope. Call your agent, and your attorney if you have one. Common fixes are a repair escrow, a credit, or a short delay.

Slide 9 · Walk the house against your PA.9 tracker before you sign anything

The CFPB says to use the final walkthrough to confirm agreed repairs were made. It also says to check that items the seller agreed to leave are still there. If something's wrong, contact the seller right away.

For an STR, the walkthrough is also your first look at the house as an operator. So bring the PA.9 tracker and walk it row by row.

Look at the seller's repairs, and get the invoices. Check the included items. Look for new damage. Run the systems. Then photograph every room and write down the meter readings with the time. You'll use those for the final bills and in the PA.12 handoff.

If you find a real problem, don't sign and hope. Call your agent, and your attorney if you have one. The CFPB notes you can walk away at closing, but you might lose your deposit and face other consequences under your contract. Let your attorney or agent use that lever. Don't try it alone at the table.

Slide 10 · Know what each closing document does before you initial a single page

You'll sign a stack. Know what the main pieces do, and read the names on each one against your PA.10 worksheet.

The deed transfers ownership to the title owner you chose. It gets recorded with the county. Ask the title company when you'll get the recorded copy.

The promissory note is the promise to repay. Read the rate, payment, maturity, and any prepayment section.

The mortgage or deed of trust gives the lender a claim on the house. It holds the due-on-sale clause from PA.10, which lets the lender demand full payment if the property moves without its consent.

An entity loan is made to your LLC, not to you. If the lender wants a personal guaranty, you promise to repay if the LLC doesn't. Read it with your attorney before closing day.

Initial the settlement statement only after it matches your comparison. And check that the insurance binder starts on or before closing, with the right names.

Slide 11 · Set up trusted contacts at contract, then verify every wire by voice at a saved number

Closing-day wire fraud targets exactly your situation. A known closing date, a large transfer, and several parties emailing each other. The CFPB warns that scammers send emails posing as your agent or settlement agent with last-minute changes to wiring instructions.

Here's how I'd turn the CFPB's guidance into a protocol.

Set it up early. At contract, identify two trusted people who can confirm payment instructions. Your agent and your settlement agent are the natural pair. Write down their numbers from a source you trust: a card handed to you, or a company website you typed in yourself.

Agree on a code phrase only they know. Ask for it on every call about money. If the caller can't give it, hang up and call the saved number.

Before you send anything, call the settlement agent at the saved number and read back the bank name, account name, account number, and amount.

After the wire, call the saved number again and confirm they received the exact amount.

Treat any change to wiring instructions as fraud until a saved number proves otherwise

  • A 'new account' email is the classic move. Stop and call the saved number.
  • Don't email financial information. The CFPB says email is never a secure way to send it.
  • Don't trust caller ID. Scammers can spoof numbers. Hang up and call the saved number.
  • If a wire goes wrong, act in minutes. Call your bank or wire service right away to try to stop or reverse it.
  • File a complaint with the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3).
Slide 12 · Treat any change to wiring instructions as fraud until a saved number proves otherwise

The rest of the protocol is about what to do when something feels off.

Treat any change as fraud until proven otherwise. A 'new account' email is the classic move. Don't reply to it. Stop and call the saved number.

Don't email financial information. The CFPB says email is never a secure way to send it.

Don't trust caller ID. The CFPB notes scammers can spoof phone numbers. If a call feels wrong, hang up and call the saved number yourself.

And if something does go wrong, minutes matter. The CFPB says to contact your bank or wire service immediately to try to stop or reverse the transfer. It also says to file a complaint with the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center.

For Cedar Ridge, the wire is $102,988. Ask your bank this week who to call, and what their cutoff times are, so you aren't looking it up in a panic.

Collect six things before you leave the closing or the house that day.

Slide 17 · Five parties confirm the figures, the wire, the documents, and the coverage

Here's who confirms what on closing day.

Your lender confirms which closing document you'll get and when. It explains any change to the rate, points, or fees. It confirms the loan figures behind the wire amount.

The title company or closing attorney confirms the settlement figures, the lien payoffs, recording, and the title policies. It also confirms the exact wire amount and the wire instructions. Always by voice, at the number you saved.

Your attorney or agent confirms possession terms and what you can do if the walkthrough turns something up. They also flag anything in the documents that differs from your contract.

Your insurer confirms the effective date, the named insured, and the mortgagee clause.

And your bank confirms how its wires work, its cutoff times, and who you call right away if a wire has to be stopped.
