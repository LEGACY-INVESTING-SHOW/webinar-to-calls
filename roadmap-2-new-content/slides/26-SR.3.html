Slide 1 · A cost-seg estimate means something only after your CPA confirms every input

This deck teaches you how to use one tool: the cost segregation and tax-impact estimator. It's a scenario workbook for one property.

It estimates how much depreciation a cost-segregation study might move into year one, and what that could mean for your tax. I built it for the conversation with your CPA. It doesn't replace that conversation.

We'll walk through the whole thing. What it's for, each tab, each section of inputs, the formulas, the rules built into it, and a filled example using Cedar Ridge. Then the common mistakes, the evidence to ask a study provider for, and how to hand it to your CPA.

One warning up front. Whether any of this applies depends on the property, the dates, and how you use it. It also depends on your participation, your income, and the tax year. The workbook makes those inputs visible. Your CPA decides them.

The workbook does four jobs.

First, it collects every input a CPA will ask for, with a source and a status beside each one. Second, it separates ordinary depreciation from accelerated depreciation. That way you can see what the study actually changes, compared with doing nothing.

Third, and this is the one people skip, it shows the tax effect only after the loss limits you enter. A big deduction you can't use this year is not cash this year. The workbook won't let it look like cash.

Build it as a spreadsheet with five tabs. The formulas later in this deck use these tab names and cell addresses, so keep them exactly.

Inputs holds every assumption. Calc holds formulas only, and nobody types there. Outputs holds the separated results you bring to your CPA. Scenarios puts low, base, and high cases side by side. And Sources lists every rule the workbook relies on, with a link and a date.

Six mistakes turn an honest estimate into a number that looks like cash

  • Adding the tax effect to operating cash flow or to a lender's math. Keep it on its own line.
  • Treating the estimated deduction as cash before the CPA sets the usable share.
  • Typing into the Calc tab. Only the shaded input cells take typing.
  • Guessing a CPA cell instead of leaving it blank and marking its status.
  • Applying bonus to land or to the whole building. Land never depreciates.
  • Counting furniture twice: once in B14 and again inside the study.
SR.3 · Cost segregation & tax-impact estimator§ SR.3.410 / 22
Slide 10 · Six mistakes turn an honest estimate into a number that looks like cash

Here are the mistakes this workbook is built to catch.

The biggest is mixing the tax effect into cash flow. Never add it to operating cash flow in another model. And keep it out of lender math. A lender underwrites cash flow, not deductions.

Second, treating the deduction as cash before your CPA sets the usable share. If the activity is passive, that share can be zero.

Third, typing into the Calc tab. Once someone overwrites a formula, nobody can trust the outputs.

Fourth, guessing. If a cell is your CPA's call, leave it blank and mark it CPA to supply. A guess dressed up as a number is worse than an empty cell.

Fifth, bonus on land or on the whole building. Bonus applies to the short-life pieces only.

Sixth, counting furniture twice. If your furnishings are already on your books in B14, ask the study provider how they treated them.

Slide 11 · Five built-in rules decide whether the deduction can count this year

The workbook builds in eight rules. Five of them work like gates.

Gate one is dates. The IRS says 100% bonus is now permanent for eligible property acquired after January 19, 2025. A binding contract signed earlier changes that, which is why B6 exists. Publication 946 also describes 40% for certain earlier property, and an election to take 40% instead. The screen only checks dates. Your CPA confirms eligibility.

Gate two: bonus needs a recovery period of 20 years or less. The building's 27.5 or 39 years is too long. Land isn't depreciable at all.

Gate three is the Publication 527 home-use test. Cedar Ridge's 10 personal days sit under 21.2.

Gate four is Publication 925's average-stay rule. Cedar Ridge averages 3.48 days. Passing that doesn't make the loss usable by itself. It sends your CPA to the material participation tests, like more than 500 hours, or more than 100 hours and at least as much as anyone else. A cleaner who logs more hours than you can matter here.

Four more rules keep the building life, loss limits, and basis honest

  • Building life is a CPA call. At 27.5 years, Cedar Ridge's remaining building is $8,872 a year instead of $6,256.
  • The $25,000 allowance phases out between $100,000 and $150,000 of MAGI. At about $185,000 it's gone either way.
  • The excess business loss limit was $313,000, or $626,000 joint, for 2025. Your CPA supplies the current year.
  • A study on property placed in service in an earlier year can need Form 3115 and a section 481(a) adjustment.
  • Depreciation reduces basis whether or not you claim it. The Outputs tab carries both pools to OP.12.
SR.3 · Cost segregation & tax-impact estimator§ SR.3.512 / 22
Slide 12 · Four more rules keep the building life, loss limits, and basis honest

Here are the other rules.

The building life. Publication 946 defines residential rental property by where the income comes from, and it excludes hotel, motel, or inn units used mostly on a transient basis. How that applies to a single house rented for short stays is a question for your CPA. B6 uses 39 years. At 27.5, Cedar Ridge's line would be $8,872 a year.

Loss limits come before the tax effect. Publication 925 describes a special allowance of up to $25,000 for active participation. It phases out between $100,000 and $150,000 of MAGI. Ask whether it can apply at all. At Maya and Chris's assumed $185,000, it's fully phased out anyway. There's also an excess business loss limit. For 2025 it was $313,000, or $626,000 joint.

A late study can need Form 3115. Your CPA handles that filing.

And depreciation reduces basis whether or not you claim it. That's why the pools carry forward.

Ask a study provider for twelve pieces of evidence before you pay

IRS Pub 5653 says there are no standards for preparing studies. The first four requests come from it; the rest make the study usable here.

  • Preparer's name, credentials, and experience
  • Every participant and their role
  • Name and description of the method
  • Reconciliation to price and basis
  • Source of plans, or site-visit notes and photos
  • Asset-by-asset schedule with class and life
  • Legal reasoning for each classification
  • Land allocation method and support
  • How furnishings already on your books were treated
  • Placed-in-service date the study assumed
  • Discussion of recapture at sale
  • Fee, delivery date, and support if the IRS asks

Slide 16 · Ask a study provider for twelve pieces of evidence before you pay

The IRS publishes a Cost Segregation Audit Techniques Guide for its own examiners. It's Publication 5653, and the current version is dated February 2025.

It says there are no standards for preparing these studies. It says they vary widely in method, documentation, depth, and preparer expertise. It describes approaches from a detailed engineering approach built on actual cost records, down to survey, letter, and rule-of-thumb approaches.

Its principal elements of a quality study include a preparer with expertise and experience, and a reconciliation of allocated costs to actual costs. Its information requests ask who prepared the study, what role each person played, and where the blueprints came from.

So use that guide to shape what you ask for before you pay. The first four requests on this list come straight from it. The rest are my recommendations for making the study fit this workbook. The asset schedule fills B17 to B19. The land method ties to B11. The placed-in-service date must match B8.

  1. Before the meeting, fill every input you can document. Leave CPA cells blank and mark status honestly.

  5. Rerun the Scenarios tab with the CPA's inputs. Save a dated copy and log the CPA on the Sources tab.
