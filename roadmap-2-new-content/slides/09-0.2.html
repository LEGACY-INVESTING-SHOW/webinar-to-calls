Ownership wins only on specific facts

Slide 1 · Ownership wins only on specific facts

The title of this lesson makes a claim: ownership wins. Sometimes it does. Sometimes it loses badly. So I treat that title as a question you answer for one property, one loan, and one household.

By the end you'll split an ownership return into four streams and know which one you can spend this year. You'll compare owning with leasing a similar house. And you'll have a simple test for whether ownership wins on your deal.

We'll use Cedar Ridge the whole way. It's the hypothetical three-bedroom lake house Maya and Chris are buying for $385,000 on a DSCR loan quote. Every number comes from the course example file.

Slide 2 · Ownership pays four ways, and you can spend only one

The usual pitch for owning lists three benefits: cash flow, tax savings, and equity. I split equity in two, because the parts behave differently. That gives four streams.

Cash flow is what's left after operating costs and the loan payment. Principal paydown is the part of each payment that reduces what you owe. Change in value is what the house is worth later compared with what you paid. Tax timing is depreciation that may lower your tax now. Some of it may be taxed back when you sell, so I call it timing, not savings.

Only one of the four lands in your bank account in a normal month. So when someone adds all four and calls the total income, stop them.

Slide 3 · The only spendable stream is $5,572 a year

Cash flow is the only stream you can spend this year.

Cedar Ridge books 212 nights at $300, plus 61 cleaning fees, for $74,275. Operating costs are $45,066. That includes a 5% repairs reserve and a 5% capital reserve for things like a roof. The loan payment is $23,637 a year. That leaves $5,572, about $464 a month.

Don't drop those reserves to make the number look bigger. You'd be borrowing from the next roof.

Against $142,688 of cash in, that's a 3.9% cash-on-cash return. In the weak case, at $265 a night and 50% occupancy, the same house loses $5,475 a year. Small in a good year. Negative in a bad one. Both are pre-diligence figures, and PA.9 revisits them.

  • Already paid out of your cash flow
  • Back only when you sell or refinance
  • If the value falls, equity can shrink
Slide 4 · Paydown of $2,795 is equity you can't spend

Each month, part of the loan payment reduces the balance. That part becomes equity, the share of the house you own outright. On Cedar Ridge it's $2,795 in year one, and it grows a little each year.

Here's the catch. You already paid that $2,795 out of your cash flow. It's inside the $23,637 loan payment. You can't spend it. You get it back when you sell or refinance.

A refinance can turn some of it into cash. In the course example, one at the end of year two frees $15,725 after costs. But the bigger loan cuts base-case cash flow from $5,572 to $4,752.

And if the value falls, you can pay the loan down every month and still have less equity than you started with.

Slide 5 · If the value stays flat, a sale returns $85,531

Change in value is the stream people talk about most and control least. The course example assumes 3% growth a year, which is $11,550 in year one. That's an assumption, not a forecast. Don't count it as $960 a month you can use. You collect it only by selling or borrowing, and both cost money.

So run this test on every deal. What if the value stays flat for five years? Cedar Ridge sells at $385,000. Take off 7% selling costs, $26,950, and the $272,519 loan balance. You walk away with $85,531 before tax. You put in $142,688. That's a $57,157 gap, and only cash flow and tax timing can close it.

Slide 6 · Year-one deductions could reach $107,072

Tax timing comes from depreciation, a paper write-off for wear on the building. Your CPA sets every input: how much of the price is land, the date the house was ready to rent, and whether it's on the 27.5-year or 39-year schedule.

In the course example, a cost segregation study moves $68,816 of the building into short-life property. Add $32,000 of furnishings, and that $100,816 may qualify for 100% bonus depreciation. Add a full year on the rest of the building, $6,256. That's about $107,072. At an assumed 32% tax rate, that could cut tax by about $34,263.

Read the word could. Some of it can come back as taxable gain when you sell. That's why I call it timing.

Slide 7 · Three tests decide if the loss is usable

Here's what has to be true before that loss lowers your tax this year. Passive losses generally offset only passive income.

Test one. If the average stay is 7 days or less, the IRS doesn't treat it as a rental activity under these rules. Cedar Ridge averages 3.5 nights, so yes.

Test two. The loss offsets other income, like your paycheck, only if you materially participate. One way is more than 100 hours and at least as much as anyone else. Miss it and the loss stays passive. It may be suspended and carried forward.

Test three is personal use. Above the greater of 14 days or 10% of rented days, the house counts as a home. For Cedar Ridge that's 21.2 days. Maya and Chris plan 10.

Your CPA confirms each test for your household.

Slide 8 · At $185,000, hours matter and the $25,000 allowance doesn't

Two traps catch high-earning households.

First, one participation test compares your hours with everyone else's, owner or not. A cleaner who turns the house over 61 times a year may log more hours than you. A full-service co-host's hours may exceed yours by a wide margin. Keep a real log of your hours. Your CPA will ask.

Second, the $25,000 allowance for rental real estate. It shrinks by 50% of modified adjusted gross income above $100,000 and is gone at $150,000. Maya and Chris earn $185,000 combined. If their modified AGI is near that, it gives them nothing. At a short average stay it may not apply at all.

Slide 9 · Owning takes $98,188 more cash for similar cash flow

Part One is about leasing a place and renting it to guests. So why not lease a house like Cedar Ridge instead of buying it?

I kept the operations identical: same revenue, same cleaner, same utilities. The lease is hypothetical, $2,900 a month with written permission to host. The landlord carries property tax, insurance, and capital repairs.

Base-case cash flow comes out almost even. Leasing is $315 a year higher. In the weak case the lease loses more, because rent is fixed. If you own, though, you carry capital repairs the reserve may not fully cover. And owning takes $98,188 more cash. That extra cash buys streams two, three, and four.

For some households, $44,500 in a lease beats $142,688 in a house. For others, the long hold and the equity matter more. And in some lake markets, owners may not lease for short-term rental at all.

Slide 10 · Return reaches 14.0% only by counting money you can't spend

Now the same year as a return on the $142,688 you put in, one layer at a time.

Cash flow alone is 3.9%. Add principal paydown and it's 5.9%. Add the assumed 3% appreciation and it's 14.0%.

Those are three different things. Only the first one pays a bill. The second is money you already paid and can't get back without selling or refinancing. The third is an assumption you can't check until you sell.

I left tax timing out on purpose. It depends on your CPA's answers, and it can be zero in year one.

So when someone shows you one total return number, ask which layer it is. Then ask for the four streams, each on its own row.

Slide 11 · Cedar Ridge ends ahead in one of four cases

Now five years, using the course example's sale model. Cash flow stays flat, the original loan stays, and the house sells after year five with 7% selling costs. Everything is before income tax.

Case A works. Self-managed, base case, 3% growth. Five years of cash flow plus sale proceeds puts them $27,732 ahead of the $142,688 they put in. Modest, but positive.

Case B hires a full-service co-host at 18%. That's $13,370 a year, and they pay about $650 a month to own the house. They end $39,118 behind.

Case C is the weak case with growth: $27,503 behind. Case D is the base case with flat value: $29,297 behind.

So Cedar Ridge wins only if revenue hits the base case, they self-manage, and the value holds. Change any one and it falls behind.

Slide 12 · Your deal wins only if all three pass

Here's the test for your own deal. Ownership wins only if all three are true.

One, the base case comes out ahead after five years. Cedar Ridge passes at $27,732.

Two, the weak case is behind by no more than you could accept losing. Put a real number on that before you look at listings. Cedar Ridge's weak case ends $27,503 behind. Only Maya and Chris can say if that's acceptable.

Three, the extra cash for owning buys something you want: a long hold, personal use, or equity. If a lease gets you the same cash flow for far less money, you should know why you're buying anyway.

Sam's PA.4 gives you the NOI, weak case, and loan payment you need to run this on a real deal.
