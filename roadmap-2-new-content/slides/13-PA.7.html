Pick one loan to chase and keep a backup

Slide 1 · Pick one loan to chase and keep a backup

In PA.6 we priced one financing path. Now we put two side by side on the same house and the same buyer.

A DSCR loan is sized mostly on the property's income. A conventional loan is sized mostly on you: your documented income, your debts, your credit, and your assets. That one difference changes the paperwork, whether you can close in an LLC, and what happens when you go for the next property.

By the end you'll compare cash to close, the monthly payment, and five-year cost. Then you'll use a decision tree to pick which lender to call first. The tree orders your calls. The lender makes the eligibility call.

We stay with Cedar Ridge: Maya and Chris, $385,000, 25% down. Both quotes are hypothetical.

Here's Cedar Ridge with both quotes. Same $288,750 loan, same 25% down, both 30-year fixed. DSCR is 7.25% with one point, which is 1% of the loan paid at closing. Conventional is 6.875% with no points.

The conventional payment is $1,897 against $1,970. P&I here means principal and interest. Closing costs are lower because there's no point, so cash to close is $107,800 against $110,688. Total cash in adds the $32,000 furnishing budget. Cash-on-cash, first-year cash flow divided by total cash in, is 4.6% against 3.9%.

The conventional quote wins on every line. One thing the table leaves out is reserves. Either lender may want months of payments sitting in your account on top of cash to close, so ask both.

The base case assumes a $300 average nightly rate (ADR) and 58% occupancy. DSCR leaves $5,572 a year and conventional leaves $6,446. That's $874 better on conventional.

A conventional lender weighs four things

  • Debt-to-income: 36%, 45%, or 50% max
  • Documented income: W-2s, returns, pay stubs
  • Occupancy type: investment, up to 85% LTV
  • Reserves, which rise with financed properties
Slide 4 · A conventional lender weighs four things

On a conventional loan, the lender underwrites your household. I'm using Fannie Mae's rules as the example because they're public. Your loan may follow a different guide, so ask which one applies.

First, debt-to-income, your monthly debt payments divided by gross monthly income. The limit is 36% when a person underwrites the file by hand. It can reach 45% with the required credit score and reserves, and 50% through Fannie Mae's automated system.

Second, documented income. Third, occupancy type. On a one-unit investment purchase you can borrow up to 85% of the price, so at least 15% down. Fourth, reserves, which grow with the number of financed properties you have.

Maya and Chris plan about 10 personal-use days a year. Don't guess the occupancy type from that. Tell the lender the real plan and let it classify.

Here's a rough DTI check. The lender runs the real one.

Maya and Chris earn $185,000 a year, which is $15,417 a month. I've made up two numbers the example doesn't give: a $2,400 home payment and $600 of car and card payments. That's $3,000 a month, and a DTI today of 19.5%.

Now add the full Cedar Ridge conventional payment of $2,484, with zero rental income counted. Debts go to $5,484 a month. That's a DTI of 35.6%.

That's well under the 45% and 50% limits. On a hand-underwritten file, though, it's only 0.4 points under 36%. That's what "near the limit" means in the decision tree coming up.

Fannie Mae added an STR income section in September 2026

  • Required for applications from November 1, 2026
  • Second home: rental income generally can't count
  • Your lender confirms whether your deal fits
Slide 6 · Fannie Mae added an STR income section in September 2026

You'll hear people say conventional loans can't use Airbnb income. That's out of date.

On September 2, 2026, Fannie Mae added a section on short-term rental income from the property being financed. Lenders are encouraged to use it now and must use it for applications dated November 1, 2026 or later. Before that date, ask your loan officer whether they've adopted it.

I'll be straight with you. The exact conditions in that section weren't confirmed for this lesson. If the lender says your deal fits, it may count documented STR income. If the house is classified as a second home, rental income generally can't be used. And if it doesn't fit, the lender may still approve you on W-2 income alone.

Ask the lender whether the section applies to your transaction and which documents it accepts, in writing.

Slide 7 · Over five years, DSCR costs about $8,342 more

The monthly payments differ by $73. That looks small. Over five years it adds up, and the point adds more on day one.

I ran both loans for 60 months. The DSCR loan costs $2,888 in points at closing and $5,454 more in interest. Together that's about $8,342 more over five years.

They'd also owe $1,080 more at the end, because less of each DSCR payment went to principal. That's already inside the extra interest, so don't count it twice. Same with the $4,370 base-case cash flow gap over five years. It's the same payment difference seen from the operating side.

Build this table for your own deal. A DSCR term sheet may not show an APR, so five-year interest plus points plus closing costs is the fairest single number you have.

Slide 8 · A year-five penalty could add $2,725

Now the exit. In the Cedar Ridge example, Maya and Chris sell at the end of year five.

Say the DSCR loan has a 5-4-3-2-1 step-down penalty. That's an example structure, not a standard. You'd pay 5% of the balance if you paid off in year one, stepping down to 1% in year five. Month 60 is still year five, so it's 1% of $272,519. That's $2,725, on top of the $8,342, or about $11,067 in total.

With a 3-2-1 schedule, there'd be no penalty at year five. So the schedule matters as much as the rate. Ask the conventional lender to confirm in writing whether any prepayment penalty applies to its loan too.

Compare the same lines on same-day quotes

  • Rate, lock, and APR
  • Points and lender credits
  • Cash to close and reserves
  • Monthly P&I and PITIA
  • Prepayment penalty by year
  • Refinance terms
Slide 9 · Compare the same lines on same-day quotes

Rates change daily, so get both quotes on the same day if you can. Then compare these lines.

The APR includes the rate plus points and certain other charges, so it's useful when two loans have different fees. PITIA is the full payment: principal, interest, taxes, insurance, and association dues.

The last line is the one people forget: what the lender will require at refinance. How long you must own the property first. How much cash you can pull out. And how it will count STR income then.

One wrinkle. A loan on a rental you won't live in counts as business-purpose credit, so a lender may send a short term sheet instead of a standard Loan Estimate. Either way, ask for every rate, fee, and penalty in writing, in the same layout.

Slide 10 · DSCR usually means less paperwork, more cost

Price isn't the only difference. Here's what each route tends to give and take. Every line varies by lender, so confirm it with the one you're talking to.

DSCR lenders often let you close in an LLC, and the paperwork is shorter. Conventional usually means full income and asset documents, and often personal names on title.

One thing people get wrong: a DSCR loan doesn't keep the property off your record. A future conventional lender will count Cedar Ridge under its own rules when Maya and Chris go for property two.

So keep clean records from day one. Booking statements, the loan payment history, and the Schedule E your CPA files, which is the tax form that reports rental income and expenses. OP.11 picks this up.

Slide 11 · On W-2 income, call conventional first

This is Branch A of the decision tree: your first rental, with documented W-2 income. It orders your calls. The lender decides eligibility.

Is your DTI, with the full new payment and no rental income, under the limit with some room? If yes, get a conventional quote first. If you're over or within a point or two, also follow Branch C. That's for thin or complex income, and it starts with asking a lender to pre-underwrite your income.

Will the lender classify it as an investment property? Can you close the way you need to, in an LLC if you need one? If not, get a DSCR quote. And until the conventional approval is in writing, keep a DSCR quote live.

Refinancing a rental you already own? That's Branch B, and it's in the lesson.
