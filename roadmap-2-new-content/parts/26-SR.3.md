### SR.3.0 What this resource does

This is a scenario workbook for one property. It estimates how much depreciation a cost-segregation study might move into year one, and what that could mean for tax. It's built for the conversation with your CPA, not to replace it.

In plain words: when you buy a rental, you can't deduct the building's cost all at once. You deduct it a slice at a time over 27.5 or 39 years. That paper write-off for wear on the building is depreciation. Land is never depreciated. A cost-segregation study splits the building into parts. Some parts, such as land improvements, qualify for 5-, 7-, or 15-year lives. When bonus depreciation (an extra first-year deduction) applies, those short-life parts, plus your furniture, can be deducted in full in year one.

The running example is Cedar Ridge, a hypothetical three-bedroom lake house. Maya and Chris, a two-earner household with about $185,000 of W-2 income, bought it for $385,000 and furnished it for $32,000. In the base case, a study adds about $67,000 of year-one deduction. At a 32% tax rate (assumption), that's worth about $21,000 before a $4,500 study fee (assumption). That holds only if the loss is usable this year, and SR.3.5 shows why that's the biggest open question.

The workbook does four jobs. It collects every input a CPA will ask for, with a source and a status beside each. It separates ordinary depreciation from accelerated depreciation, so you can see what the study actually changes. It shows the illustrative tax effect only after the loss limits you enter, so a big deduction you can't use doesn't look like cash. And it lists every assumption that is still unverified, so nothing gets treated as settled by accident.

Sam's PA.5 lesson walks through a study and a year-one example in more depth. Use this workbook after that lesson, when you have a real property and a real quote from a study provider. No property yet? Build the workbook anyway and enter the Cedar Ridge inputs as practice. If you'd like to see the numbers before the build, read the worked example in SR.3.6 first.

One warning up front. Whether any of this applies depends on the property, the dates, and how you use it. It also depends on your participation, your income, and the tax year. The workbook makes those inputs visible. Your CPA decides them.

### SR.3.1 Workbook layout

Build it as a spreadsheet with five tabs, using the tables in SR.3.2 to SR.3.4. The formulas use these tab names and cell addresses. Shaded input cells are the only cells anyone types into.

| Tab | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Inputs | Every assumption, with what to enter, source, status, and date checked |
| Calc | Formulas only; no typing |
| Outputs | The separated results you bring to the CPA, including the open items pulled from the Inputs status column |
| Scenarios | Low / base / high side by side |
| Sources | Every rule the workbook relies on, with link and date |

Five status values are used everywhere:

- **Verified**: document in hand.
- **CPA to supply**: your CPA's call.
- **Unverified**: an estimate, a placeholder, or a document you don't have yet.
- **Study**: from the cost-segregation report.
- **Example input**: a teaching figure, used only in the Cedar Ridge tables.

A real owner would replace every Example input with a figure from reports and mark it Verified. Two kinds of cells don't take a status: the model switches (B35, B36), and the formula in B21, which reads "Formula (CPA confirms)".

- Calc B20 is the rental-use fraction (not Inputs B20, the building life). It applies only if the CPA says expenses must be divided between rental and personal use. The Cedar Ridge base case does not divide, so it uses 1.

Recapture, in plain words: when you sell, part of the depreciation you took can be taxed. That's why the Outputs tab keeps the two pools. OP.12 works through it.

Put this line at the top of the Outputs tab in bold: "Illustrative only. Every figure depends on inputs your CPA has not yet confirmed. Not tax advice."

Six mistakes turn an honest estimate into a number that looks like cash:

1. Adding the tax effect to operating cash flow or to a lender's math.
2. Treating the estimated deduction as cash before the CPA sets the usable share.
3. Typing into the Calc tab. Only the shaded input cells take typing.
4. Guessing a CPA cell instead of leaving it blank and marking its status.
5. Applying bonus to land or to the whole building. Land never depreciates.
6. Counting furniture twice: once in Inputs B14 and again inside the study.

### SR.3.5 Decision rules built into the workbook

The workbook builds in eight rules. Rules 1, 2, 4, 5, and 6 work like yes-or-no gates. Rules 3, 7, and 8 cover the building life, late-study filings, and basis.

**Rule 1. Bonus rate depends on dates and eligibility.** The IRS says the One, Big, Beautiful Bill, a tax law, made the 100% additional first-year depreciation deduction permanent. It covers eligible property acquired after January 19, 2025 ([IRS news release](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/treasury-irs-issue-guidance-on-the-additional-first-year-depreciation-deduction-amended-as-part-of-the-one-big-beautiful-bill); [Notice 2026-11](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/n-26-11.pdf)). For this rule, property is treated as acquired no later than the date a binding written contract for it was signed. So a contract signed on or before January 19, 2025 can rule out 100% bonus, even if you closed later. That's why Inputs B6 asks for the contract date as well as the closing date. Publication 946 describes 100% for qualified property acquired and placed in service after January 19, 2025. It also describes 40% for certain property placed in service after December 31, 2024 and before January 20, 2025 ([IRS Publication 946](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p946)). An election to use 40% instead also exists for some property. The screen in Inputs B21 only checks dates. The CPA confirms eligibility and enters any election in Inputs B22.

**Rule 2. Bonus applies to short-life property, never to land or the whole building.** Qualified property must be tangible property depreciated under MACRS, the IRS's standard depreciation system. Its recovery period must be 20 years or less ([IRS Publication 946](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p946)). The 27.5-year and 39-year building lives are longer, so Calc B13 applies bonus only to Calc B11 (reclassified parts plus furnishings). Land is not depreciable at all ([IRS Publication 946](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p946)).

**Rule 3. The building life is a CPA decision.** Publication 946 defines residential rental property as a building earning 80% or more of gross rental income from dwelling units. It excludes hotel, motel, or inn units where more than half the units are used on a transient basis ([IRS Publication 946](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p946)). How that applies to a single house rented for short stays is a question for your CPA. The Cedar Ridge base case uses 39 years in Inputs B20, the cautious choice if short stays count as transient use. A longer life means a smaller yearly deduction. With 27.5 years, Cedar Ridge's remaining-building line would be $8,872 per full year instead of $6,256.

**Rule 4. The personal-use day test.** Publication 527 treats a dwelling unit as used as a home in one case. That's when personal use exceeds the greater of 14 days or 10% of the days rented at a fair rental price ([IRS Publication 527](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p527)). Calc B24 runs that test. Publication 527 also says that with any personal use, expenses are divided between rental and personal use. Set Inputs B36 to 1 if your CPA applies that division to depreciation.

**Rule 5. Average stay and material participation.** Publication 925 says an activity with an average customer stay of 7 days or less is not a rental activity. You figure the average by dividing total days in all rental periods by the number of rentals ([IRS Publication 925](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p925)). Calc B22 does that math. "Not a rental activity" isn't bad news. It means the usual rental-loss rules don't settle the question on their own. Passing that test does not make the loss usable by itself. The CPA then applies the material participation tests. Two examples are more than 500 hours, or more than 100 hours and at least as much as anyone else ([IRS Publication 925](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p925)). That's why Inputs B27 and B28 ask for your hours and everyone else's. A cleaner or co-host who logs more hours than you can make you fail that 100-hour test.

**Rule 6. Loss limits come before the tax effect.** If the activity is passive, the loss may be suspended and carried forward instead of reducing this year's tax. Then Inputs B32 would be 0%. Passive is Publication 925's term. Losses from passive activities generally offset only income from other passive activities, not your paycheck (lesson 0.2 covers this). For Cedar Ridge, Rule 5's tests decide whether it's passive. Publication 925 describes a special allowance of up to $25,000 for active participation in rental real estate. It phases out between $100,000 and $150,000 of MAGI ([IRS Publication 925](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p925)). Ask the CPA whether that allowance can apply to your activity at all, given Rule 5. At Maya and Chris's assumed MAGI of about $185,000, the allowance would be fully phased out, so it doesn't help them either way. A separate excess business loss limit can also apply. For 2025 the IRS threshold was $313,000, or $626,000 for joint filers. Disallowed amounts carry forward as a net operating loss (NOL) ([Form 461 instructions](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i461)). Cedar Ridge's $107,072 sits far below that 2025 figure. The CPA supplies the current-year figure.

That's why the base case's 100% usable share in Inputs B32 is an assumption, not a finding. It rests on Rule 5's tests going Maya and Chris's way, and their hours aren't logged yet.

**Rule 7. Late studies need a method change.** Suppose the property was placed in service in an earlier year. Reclassifying it can then require Form 3115, a change in accounting method, with a section 481(a) catch-up adjustment ([About Form 3115](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-3115); [IRS Publication 946](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p946)). Add a note in Inputs B16 if this applies. The CPA handles the filing.

**Rule 8. Depreciation reduces basis whether or not you claim it.** Publication 527 says basis goes down by the depreciation you could have deducted, even if you didn't claim it ([IRS Publication 527](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p527)). The Outputs tab carries the accelerated and straight-line pools forward so the recapture conversation in OP.12 starts with real numbers.

Here is the base column worked by hand, so you can follow each formula:

1. Basis: $385,000 + $6,000 = $391,000.
2. Land: $391,000 × 20% = $78,200. Building: $391,000 × (1 − 20%) = $312,800.
3. The study moves 22% of the building to short lives: $312,800 × 22% = $68,816. Remaining building: $312,800 − $68,816 = $243,984.
4. Accelerated: ($68,816 + $32,000) × 100% = $100,816.
5. Ordinary, a full first year for simplicity: $243,984 ÷ 39 = $6,256.
6. Year-1 deduction: $100,816 + $6,256 = $107,072.
7. Illustrative tax effect: $107,072 × 32% = $34,263, only if all of it is usable this year.

Read the table this way. The study's real contribution, compared with no study, is $67,051 more deduction in year one ($107,072 − $40,021). At 32% that's $21,456, or $16,956 after a $4,500 fee, if fully usable. The furnishings bonus appears in both the study and no-study rows, because furniture is short-life property whether or not you order a study.

The study mostly changes timing. It doesn't change your basis: $391,000 plus $32,000 of furnishings either way. It moves deductions earlier, and it lowers your adjusted basis faster. That shows up again at sale (OP.12).

The passive row is the one to fear. In that case, the year-one tax effect is zero and the deduction waits. Remember that the base column assumes the whole loss is usable this year (Inputs B32 = 100%). With the $25,000 allowance phased out at their income, that depends on the participation tests in Rule 5.

Two more checks from the day-count inputs:

- Home-use test: the greater of 14 days or 10% × 212 = 21.2 days. Ten personal days is below that, so Cedar Ridge is not "used as a home" under the Publication 527 test, as entered.
- Average stay: 212 ÷ 61 = 3.48 days, which is 7 days or less. The CPA then decides passive status using the participation tests and Maya and Chris's hour logs.

### SR.3.7 Evidence to request from a study provider

The IRS publishes a [Cost Segregation Audit Techniques Guide, Publication 5653](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p5653.pdf), for its examiners. The current version is dated February 2025. It says there are no standards for preparing these studies, and that studies vary widely in method, documentation, depth, and preparer expertise. It contrasts a detailed engineering approach built on actual cost records with survey, letter, and "rule of thumb" approaches. Its principal elements of a quality study include a preparer with expertise and experience. They also include a reconciliation of total allocated costs to total actual costs. Its information document requests ask who took part and in what role, and where the blueprints came from.

Use that guide to shape what you ask for before you pay. The first five rows come straight from the points above. The rest are my recommendations for making the study usable in this workbook. Get all twelve in writing before you engage a provider.

| Every participant and their role (engineer, appraiser, analyst) | Pub 5653 information document requests ask for this | |

| Reconciliation of allocated costs to total purchase price and basis | Pub 5653 lists reconciliation as a principal element | |

### SR.3.8 How to use this with a CPA

1. Before the meeting, fill every input you can document. Leave the CPA cells blank rather than guessing. Mark each status honestly.
2. Send the CPA the workbook, the settlement statement, the furnishing receipts, the study quote, and your calendar of rental and personal days. If you already have the study, send it with the evidence from SR.3.7.
3. Ask the CPA to fill or confirm the dates and basis first. Those are the placed-in-service date (B8), capitalized costs (B10), land method (B11), and building life (B20), all on the Inputs tab. Then the method choices: bonus rate or election (B21, B22), rental-use division (B36), and first-year convention (B35).
4. Ask the CPA to decide participation and limits: which Publication 925 test applies (B29), professional status (B30), MAGI (B31), and the usable share (B32). Bring your hour log.
5. Ask for the marginal federal and state rates the deduction would offset (B33, B34).
6. Rerun the Scenarios tab with the CPA's inputs. Save a dated copy. Record the CPA's name and the date on the Sources tab. An email counts.
7. Decide on the study only after seeing the "added deduction from the study" line, the passive case, and the fee side by side. Keep the dated workbook with your tax records for the OP.12 exit conversation.

A short script for the meeting: "Here is my estimate and every assumption behind it. Which inputs are wrong, which apply to me, and what would you enter instead?"
