What the title leaves out

Expect some mix of these checks:

  • Your credit score and credit history
  • How much cash you put in (the loan-to-value, or LTV)
  • Your reserves after closing, if that lender requires them
  • The property type, condition, and appraisal
  • Whether short-term rental use is legal at that address
  • The entity on title, and who signs a personal guarantee
PA.6 · DSCR Loans 101§ PA.6.12 / 20
Slide 2 · A DSCR lender swaps your pay stubs for the property's income, but it still checks you

The tracker calls this lesson "How They Qualify the Property, Not You." That's shorthand. It points at the main difference from a regular mortgage. A DSCR lender sizes the loan mostly on the property's rental income, not your pay stubs.

But the lender still looks at you. Kiavi's own guide to DSCR rental loans says it considers the borrower's credit score, the down payment available, and the property's coverage ratio. So expect the list on this slide.

Here's the fair way to say it. The property's income replaces your W-2 income in the math. It doesn't mean nobody asks about you.

One more thing. Kiavi's terms are one lender's terms. Other lenders publish different minimums, and the same lender can change its terms. I use Kiavi's pages as examples because they're public. Treat them that way, then get your own written quote.

PA.6 · DSCR Loans 101§ PA.6.23 / 20
Slide 3 · The lender makes three choices, and each one moves the ratio

DSCR stands for debt service coverage ratio. In plain words, it's how many dollars of property income the lender counts for every dollar of loan payment.

The top number is some version of income. The bottom number is some version of the payment. Kiavi describes a common version: gross rent divided by PITIA. PITIA is principal, interest, property taxes, insurance, and any HOA dues. Kiavi's material also says lenders calculate it with different inputs. Some use gross rent, some use a vacancy-adjusted figure, and some put flood insurance in the payment while others leave it out.

That flexibility is the whole problem. The same house can produce very different ratios depending on these three choices.

So before you compare any two quotes, ask each lender for its definition in writing. Pricing often changes with the ratio too, so the definition can move your rate, not just your approval.

Slide 5 · The same house scores 2.42, 1.94, or 1.18, depending on the lender's definition

Now three different incomes on top of the same $30,684.

Ratio 1 uses gross booking revenue. Base-case gross is $74,275: 212 booked nights at a $300 ADR, plus cleaning fees. Divide by $30,684 and you get 2.42.

Ratio 2 haircuts that revenue by 20%. Some lenders discount projected STR income. The 20% here is my assumption, not any lender's rule. That gives $59,420, and a ratio of 1.94.

Ratio 3 uses net operating income, which is revenue after operating costs. Base-case NOI is $29,209, and tax and insurance are already taken out of it. So I add them back, or they'd be counted twice. That's $36,259, and a ratio of 1.18.

Same house, same loan, same year. The ratio runs from 1.18 to 2.42. The lender's written definition decides which one counts.

PA.6 · DSCR Loans 101§ PA.6.47 / 20
Slide 7 · A lender ratio of 1.87 can sit right next to a $456-a-month loss

Look at the weak case on the gross definition: 1.87. That sounds comfortable. It says the house brings in almost twice its loan payment.

Here's what Maya and Chris actually keep. Weak-case NOI is $18,162. Annual principal and interest on the DSCR loan is $23,637. NOI already covers tax and insurance, so cash flow is NOI minus principal and interest. That's minus $5,475 a year, about minus $456 a month.

A lender ratio of 1.87 and an owner losing $456 a month. Both are true at once.

The reason is simple. A gross ratio ignores the platform fee, the cleaner, utilities, supplies, software, repairs, and the capital reserve. An STR carries far more operating cost than a long-term rental where the tenant pays utilities. On Cedar Ridge, base-case operating costs are $45,066. The gross ratio never sees them.

And even the base case is thin: $5,572 a year, about $464 a month.

Slide 8 · Use the lender's ratio to ask if they'll lend, and your own cash flow to ask if you should buy

So keep two tools for two questions.

The lender's ratio answers one question only: will this lender make this loan at this size? It's built to protect the loan. It isn't built to protect your return.

Your own NOI and cash flow answer the other question: should you buy this house? That's where every operating cost shows up, in both the base case and the weak case.

Sam's PA.4 lesson, Reading a Full Underwriting Package, builds that NOI line by line. I'm not going to re-teach it here. What I want you to bring back from PA.4 is his operating-cost total and your base and weak NOI. Bring those to every lender conversation. Slide 9 · An NOI-based 1.20 minimum would shrink Cedar Ridge's loan to about $282,989

The definition does more than pass or fail you. It can shrink the loan.

Say a lender uses the NOI-based definition and requires a minimum of 1.20. That minimum is my assumption. The most PITIA it will accept is $36,259 divided by 1.20, which is $30,216 a year. Take out $7,050 of tax and insurance. The most principal and interest it allows is $23,166 a year. At 7.25% over 30 years, that supports a loan of about $282,989.

Maya and Chris asked for $288,750. This lender would cut the loan by about $5,761. They'd bring that much more cash to closing.

Now flip it. Under the 80% gross definition with a 1.25 minimum, also an assumption, the same house supports a loan well above what they asked for. There, the limit that binds is the lender's maximum LTV, not the ratio.

So ask each lender for the maximum loan its definition supports on your numbers.

No booking history

With no booking history, the lender picks the income source, and it will lean low

  • Appraiser's market rent: Kiavi uses the lower of 110% of appraised market rent or lease rent. Ask how that works with no lease.
  • STR revenue: Kiavi finances STRs in some markets on nightly revenue. The page didn't say which data or haircut. Ask.
  • Third-party estimates: ask which provider, which comparable set, and whether they discount it.
  • Actual history: 12 or 24 months of statements or returns. The seller's history may not fit your pricing or costs.
Confirm with

Each lender, in writing: income source for a no-history STR purchase, any haircut, and whether the appraiser must be STR-experienced.

PA.6 · DSCR Loans 101§ PA.6.610 / 20
Slide 10 · With no booking history, the lender picks the income source, and it will lean low

Cedar Ridge has no booking history under Maya and Chris. Neither will most houses you buy to convert. So where does the lender's income number come from?

Each lender picks its own source. Kiavi's rental loan page says the qualifying rent is the lower of 110% of appraised market rent or a valid lease rent. That structure fits a long-term lease, so ask how it applies to nightly bookings. Kiavi also publishes that it finances STRs in some markets and lets borrowers qualify on nightly revenue. The page I could confirm doesn't say which data source or haircut it applies.

Some lenders accept a report from an STR data provider. On an existing STR, a lender may want 12 or 24 months of statements.

The lender's number will rarely match your pro forma. It shouldn't. When the lender's income is lower than yours, don't argue the loan up. Ask whether your pro forma is the one that's too high.

Slide 12 · A year-two refinance under a 3-2-1 penalty gives up $5,659 of the cash-out

Here's why the prepayment penalty matters for Cedar Ridge. The canonical example refinances at the end of year 2. The DSCR balance then is $282,951.

Take a 3-2-1 structure as the example. That's 3% of the balance in year one, 2% in year two, 1% in year three, then nothing. It's one common shape, not a standard.

A payoff in month 24 is still in year two. So the penalty is 2% of $282,951, which is $5,659.

The canonical refinance shows $15,725 of cash out. Subtract the penalty and it drops to $10,066. Wait one more month, into year three, and the penalty falls to 1%.

That's a big swing for a one-month timing choice. State law and loan type can also limit these penalties. Ask the lender how its penalty applies in your state, and write the schedule down by year.

PA.6 · DSCR Loans 101§ PA.6.914 / 20
Slide 14 · Send the same 12 written questions to two lenders, on the same house, on the same day

This is the first tool you take away. Send these same questions, in writing, to at least two lenders. Same house. Same day if you can.

Why the same day? Rates change daily. Quotes from different days can differ for reasons that have nothing to do with the lender.

The first four questions are the ones people skip. The formula. The income source. The minimum ratio. The maximum loan on your numbers. Those four decide whether the loan even happens at the size you need.

The rest decide the cost and the strings. Points and fees. The prepayment schedule, by year. Reserves. The LLC and who guarantees. What proof of legal STR use they need, and when. Whether the appraiser needs STR experience. And how long the rate lock lasts.

If a lender won't answer in writing, that's an answer too.

Four things this week. Each should take under an hour.

First, pull your base-case and weak-case NOI from your PA.4 package. You need both before you talk to anyone.

Second, send the 12 questions to two DSCR lenders. Same day. Same address.

Third, fill in the worksheet as the answers come back. Formula, maximum loan, points, fees, prepayment schedule.

Fourth, compute your weak-case cash flow and write one line on how you'd cover it and for how long.

You now have one financing path priced out. PA.7 puts that DSCR quote next to a conventional quote on the same house and the same buyers. Bring your filled worksheet. The conventional side will ask for your income documents, not just the property's, so start gathering those too.

Here are the sources behind this lesson.

Most of the lender terms come from Kiavi's public pages. I used Kiavi because its pages are public, not because it's the only choice or the best one. Every Kiavi term here is one lender's terms, and lenders change their terms. The reserve and LLC points came from search summaries of Kiavi's site. The lesson's source log flags that they weren't tied to one exact page.

The CFPB pages cover what a point is, how to compare loan offers, and the Regulation Z commentary on business-purpose credit. That last one explains why a DSCR lender may hand you a term sheet instead of a Loan Estimate.

The Cedar Ridge numbers all come from our canonical example file. They're assumptions for teaching. They are not a quote anyone gave Maya and Chris.
