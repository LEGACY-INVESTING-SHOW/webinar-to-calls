5.7 · The 2026 Landlord Pitch§ 5.7.01 / 17
Slide 1 · Win a clear yes or a clear no, in writing, with evidence the owner can check

This lesson is about the part of the landlord conversation that often decides whether an owner says yes: their worries about legality and liability.

By the end you'll be able to run a landlord conversation that ends in one of two documented outcomes. One is written approval with a signed addendum. The other is a clear pass that you log and move on from. Both are fine results.

You'll build three things. A 60-second opening. An evidence packet that answers regulation and liability objections before they come up. And an accurate way to explain how your own insurance differs from a platform's protection program. That last one is where I hear operators say inaccurate things most often, so we'll spend real time on it.

We'll run it all through Alder Street, with a role play against a fictional landlord named Dana.

Slide 3 · Cover four points in the first 60 seconds, in this order, in plain words

Owners decide in the first minute whether you sound like a business or a risk. So cover four points, in this order.

Who operates the property. Your name, your company, and the fact that you'd be the tenant on the lease.

Who stays. Paying guests for short stays, usually a few nights, booked through named platforms. Give the expected volume. Owners want a number, not "some guests."

How the unit is maintained. Professional cleaning after every stay, a local contact for repairs, and inspections on a schedule.

Why it can benefit the owner. A lease at the asking rent with a professional tenant. The unit kept furnished and cleaned. Problems reported fast.

The sample on the next slide also names your insurance, the permit, and the addendum. Those preview your evidence.

Be careful on that fourth point. Don't promise things you can't control. "Zero wear and tear" and "no complaints ever" are promises you'll break, and the owner will remember them the day you do.

Owners respond to paper more than to reassurance. Your evidence packet is a folder of documents that answer objections before they come up.

The permit status comes from your 3.4 scorecard and your SR.1 research, with the official links. The draft addendum carries the ten topics from lesson 3.5, drafted with lesson 5.3 and reviewed by your attorney.

The operating summary gives stays per year, maximum guests, cleaning, and inspections. House rules come from your listing draft. Missing something? Say so and give a date. "The insurer quoted $1,300 a year; I'll send the declarations page when the policy binds, before the first guest." That's honest and specific, and owners hear the difference.

Slide 6 · Answer the worry behind each objection, then write the answer into the addendum

Every objection has a real worry behind it. Answer it with evidence, then write the answer into the addendum.

"Is this even legal here?" The worry is fines, liability, or being named in enforcement. Show the ordinance link, the permit category, the date you checked, and any written answer from the licensing office. In writing, you take on every permit, and the change-in-law clause, clause 8 of 3.5's sample addendum, covers the day the rules move.

"I don't want parties." Show your house rules, your guest cap, and Airbnb's policy against open-invite parties and disruptive gatherings. In writing: an occupancy cap, no events, and breach and cure. That means written notice and a set number of days to fix a problem.

Noise gets quiet hours and a response time. Damage gets your declarations page, your damage process, and the lease deposit you pay the owner. In writing, you pay for guest damage beyond ordinary wear and report within 24 hours.

For worries about other tenants, show your contact sheet and a neighbor notice with your number. Airbnb's Neighborhood Support page, where neighbors can report parties, is only a backstop. Your number comes first.

Slide 7 · Three more objections are yours; the owner's insurer or lender is theirs to answer

Four more objections. You answer the first three. Only the owner can answer the last one.

"What if you can't fill it and stop paying?" That's a vacancy and default worry. A summary of your stress case, your numbers with a lower rate and occupancy, plus your cash reserve is the evidence. In writing, it's standard rent and default terms plus your lease deposit.

"Who answers at night?" The owner doesn't want a 2 a.m. call. Name your local contact and a backup, with coverage hours, and commit to delivering the name before the first stay.

"What if something happens to a guest?" The owner is afraid of being sued. Show your STR liability declarations page, with the owner named as an additional insured, a person the policy also protects, if your insurer allows it.

The last row is honest about limits. Some objections you can't solve. If the owner's own insurer excludes short stays, or the owner believes the mortgage forbids it, that's for the owner to resolve. You can't answer for their policy or their loan. Offer to wait while they check. If the answer is no, log the pass and move on.

Host damage protection isn't insurance, so never describe it to an owner that way

Airbnb's AirCover for hosts

Two parts with different rules
  • Host damage protection: Airbnb says it isn't an insurance policy (Airbnb Help 3733)
  • Request within 14 days of checkout; wear and tear excluded; can be denied for lack of reasonable care (Airbnb Help 279)
  • Host liability insurance: third-party underwritten, with exclusions; landlord covered only narrowly (Airbnb Help 3145)
  • Host liability insurance applies only to Airbnb bookings, not Vrbo or direct (Airbnb Help 937)

Your own STR liability policy

The document the owner should see
  • A contract between you and your insurer
  • Names you and lists limits and exclusions
  • May name the landlord as an additional insured
  • Can cover bookings from every channel, if the policy says so
5.7 · The 2026 Landlord Pitch§ 5.7.58 / 17
Slide 8 · Host damage protection isn't insurance, so never describe it to an owner that way

This is the objection where operators most often say something inaccurate. Get it right, because the owner may repeat what you said to their own insurer.

Start with what Airbnb says. AirCover for hosts includes Host damage protection and Host liability insurance. Airbnb states that Host damage protection isn't an insurance policy. It also has conditions. You file within 14 days of the guest's checkout. Wear and tear isn't covered. Airbnb can deny a request if you didn't take reasonable care.

Host liability insurance is actual insurance, underwritten by third-party insurers, with terms and exclusions. The program summary lists exclusions for injury the insured intended or should have expected, fungi or bacteria, and liability assumed under a contract. It treats a host's landlord as an insured only narrowly. And it applies to Airbnb bookings, not Vrbo or your direct site.

Your own policy is different. It's your contract with your insurer. It names you, lists limits and exclusions, may name the landlord, and can cover every channel if it says so. Verify Airbnb's current terms before you describe them.

Say it accurately: you rely on your own policy, not on the platform program

"I carry my own short-term-rental liability policy. Here's the declarations page with the limits. Airbnb also has a host program. Part of it is liability insurance with its own exclusions, and part of it, the damage protection, Airbnb says isn't insurance. The platform program only applies to Airbnb bookings, so I don't rely on it. My policy is what I rely on."

Confirm with

Your insurance broker: what the policy covers, whether it covers all booking channels, whether the landlord can be an additional insured, and how your policy and Airbnb's host liability insurance interact. Check Airbnb's current help pages before pitching.

5.7 · The 2026 Landlord Pitch§ 5.7.59 / 17
Slide 9 · Say it accurately: you rely on your own policy, not on the platform program

Here's an accurate way to say it to an owner. Read it a few times and put it in your own words, but keep the facts the same.

You lead with your own policy and the declarations page. That's the document the owner can hand to their attorney or insurer. Then you mention the platform program, and you describe both halves correctly. The liability part is insurance with its own exclusions. The damage protection part, Airbnb says, isn't insurance. Then you say why you don't rely on it: it only applies to Airbnb bookings. Your policy is what you rely on.

Why does this matter so much? If you call the platform program "insurance," the owner may repeat that to their insurer. Then you've damaged your credibility with the one person who can say yes.

Before you pitch, ask your broker four things. What your policy covers, and whether it covers every booking channel. Whether the landlord can be an additional insured. And how your policy and Airbnb's interact on an Airbnb booking. And check Airbnb's help pages again, because program terms change. Lesson 2.4 goes deeper on the policy itself.

5.7 · The 2026 Landlord Pitch§ 5.7.713 / 17
Slide 13 · A phone yes isn't approval until the same-day email lists what's signed and owed

A yes on the phone isn't approval. Close every conversation with an email the same day.

For approval, the email lists the signed documents and their dates. Then it lists what you still owe, like the declarations page, the permit number, and the local contact, with a date next to each one. And it states the condition plainly: no guest checks in before the permit is issued and the policy is bound. That email becomes the record of what both sides agreed.

For a pass, thank the owner and state the reason they gave, in neutral words. Ask whether you can check back if their situation changes. Then log it. The log has six columns: date, address, owner, outcome, reason given, and a follow-up date.

A pass log keeps you honest. It also shows you patterns you can't see one call at a time. If three owners in a row cite their building insurer, that's a market-level fact. You want to know it before your next pitch, not after your tenth.

Slide 17 · Every AirCover claim in this lesson comes from Airbnb's own help pages

The sources for this lesson are all Airbnb help center pages, checked on 25 September 2026.

Article 3733 is the AirCover for hosts overview. It's where Airbnb says Host damage protection isn't an insurance policy. Article 279 covers Host damage protection itself: the 14-day window, the wear-and-tear exclusion, and the reasonable-care condition. Article 3145 is the Host liability insurance program summary, with the third-party underwriting, the exclusions, and the narrow treatment of a host's landlord. Article 937 says the liability coverage applies to stays arranged through Airbnb.

Article 3345 is the Community Disturbance Policy on parties, and article 3290 is Neighborhood Support, where neighbors can report problems.

The Alder Street figures, the $46,800 of rent over the term and the 1.6 check-ins a week, come from the course's standard example. These platform terms change more often than city codes do, so open each page again before you pitch.
