Staff the jobs, not the titles, and test every fee against cash flow

Slide 1 · Staff the jobs, not the titles, and test every fee against cash flow

After this lesson you can list every job a stay requires. You'll name who does each one, and you'll name a backup.

You'll also decide what access each person gets. That covers the platform, money, guest data, and keys. Then you'll compare a set of hired vendors against a co-host or a manager, using your own numbers. And you'll find the places where you'd pay two people for the same task.

Cedar Ridge, the course's example house, makes this concrete. Its owners, Maya and Chris, live about two hours from the house, and both work full time. So the question of who does the work is real for them. We'll run their numbers four or five ways and watch what a percentage fee does to cash flow on the DSCR loan.

The retained lessons 6.1 Cleaners and 6.2 Handyman cover how to find and train those vendors. This lesson is about fitting the whole team together for a house you own.

List the jobs every stay needs before you choose a job title

  • Every stay: turnover cleaning, laundry, restocking, a condition check, guest messaging
  • On demand: repairs, lockouts, supply runs, access for vendors
  • Around the clock: emergencies and anything that can't wait until morning
  • On a schedule: inspections, bookkeeping, tax filings, permit renewals
  • Owner-only: pricing approval, compliance, bank and platform ownership, team review

You can delegate work. The owner still holds the house, the loan, and the platform account, and answers to the city, the lender, and the insurer.

Slide 2 · List the jobs every stay needs before you choose a job title

"Should I hire a co-host or a property manager?" is the wrong first question. Those titles cover very different scopes, and the scopes overlap. One co-host does messaging only. Another does everything but cleaning. You can't compare them until you know the jobs.

So start with the list of jobs every stay needs. A turnover is the clean and reset between one guest and the next. I group them by when they happen. Some happen every stay. Some happen on demand. Some happen at any hour. Some run on a schedule, like the filters, the alarms, the grill, the deck, and the water heater.

Then look at the last group. Pricing approval, compliance decisions, the bank and platform accounts, and reviewing how the team performs stay with the owner.

You can hand off a lot of work. You can't hand off responsibility for it. The owner keeps title to the house, the loan, and the platform account. When the city, the lender, or the insurer has a question, they ask the owner.

Slide 3 · Map each job to a role, then give every guest-facing role a trained backup

Once you have the job list, write one name beside each job. Then write a second name.

That's the backup rule. Every role that touches a guest stay needs a backup who has already been trained and given access. The access part matters. Say your cleaner gets sick at 7 a.m. You don't want to be adding the backup to the calendar and texting a door code while a guest drives in.

This table shows the usual roles for a small owned short-term rental. Look at the payment column. Some roles are per turnover, some are a retainer, some are a percentage or owner time. That mix is where costs hide later, so write it down now.

Notice that one person can hold several roles. At Cedar Ridge, the handyman is also the emergency contact and does the inspections. That's fine. What matters is that every role has a name and a backup. You'll see Cedar Ridge's backups on slide 14.

Decide five kinds of access separately, and give each person the least they need

  1. Platform access: see the calendar, message guests, or change prices and the listing
  2. Spending authority: how much, on what, without asking
  3. Guest data: names, phone numbers, messages, and whether they store any of it
  4. Keys and codes: keys, lock codes, the owner's closet code, the Wi-Fi admin login
  5. After-hours responsibility: who answers at 2 a.m., and how fast they must get there

Full platform access for someone who only needed a calendar. Access given for convenience is hard to take back later.

Slide 4 · Decide five kinds of access separately, and give each person the least they need

Every person on the team needs some access. The rule is to give each one the least access that still lets them do the job.

There are five kinds of access, and they aren't the same thing. Someone can have a lock code and no platform access. Someone can message guests and have no spending authority. Decide each one separately.

A common mistake is giving full platform access to someone who only needed the calendar. It feels easier at the start. Then it's hard to take back without friction, because it looks like you've stopped trusting them.

Guest names, phone numbers, and messages should stay in the platform or the PMS, your property management software. Sam's OP.4 lesson on tech stack architecture shows where messages and tasks live. From that lesson, note where each team member logs in. Then you can set access there instead of sharing passwords.

And after-hours responsibility is a real assignment. Somebody's phone is on at 2 a.m., and you need to know whose.

Slide 5 · Airbnb's co-host levels let a cleaner see the calendar without touching money

Airbnb lets a listing owner add co-hosts at different permission levels. Its co-host page describes three.

Full access lets someone manage the listing and the calendar, message guests, and view payouts and transaction history. They can also manage other co-hosts and their permissions, and submit reimbursement requests for you. That's a lot. It fits a full-service co-host or manager you've vetted and signed an agreement with.

Calendar and messaging access lets someone view the calendar, without editing it, and message guests. That fits a messaging co-host.

Calendar access lets someone see the calendar and the check-in and checkout details. These co-hosts don't show on the listing. That fits a cleaner or a handyman who only needs the turnover schedule.

One caution. These descriptions summarize Airbnb's help pages as of September 25, 2026. Airbnb changes these screens, so check the labels in your own account before you rely on them.

For Cedar Ridge, the cleaner gets calendar access. If Maya and Chris ever add a messaging co-host, that person gets calendar and messaging access. Nobody gets full access unless they're managing the listing under a written agreement.

The owner answers for every co-host, and Airbnb can pay co-hosts before you

  • The listing owner is responsible for co-hosts, including ones a full-access co-host adds
  • Only the listing owner can set up co-host payouts, at any permission level
  • When host earnings exceed co-host payouts, co-hosts are paid first
  • Co-hosts can't view or change the owner's payout method or taxpayer information
  • The host stays responsible for a co-host's actions from before the co-host was removed

A wrong payout percentage goes straight out the door. Check the co-host payout math every month.

Slide 6 · The owner answers for every co-host, and Airbnb can pay co-hosts before you

A few more points from Airbnb's help pages matter for owners.

First, you're responsible for your co-hosts. That includes co-hosts that a full-access co-host added on their own. Airbnb's co-host page tells owners to choose them with care.

Second, only the listing owner can set up co-host payouts. You can set them up for a co-host at any permission level.

Third, payment order. When your earnings on a booking are bigger than the co-host payout, the co-host gets paid first. That's convenient for them. It also means a wrong percentage gets paid out before your share, on every booking. So check the payout math monthly.

Fourth, co-hosts can't see or change your payout method or your taxpayer information. That stays with you.

And the Co-Host Additional Terms say you stay responsible for what a co-host did before you removed them.

Slide 7 · A short written agreement answers the questions you'd otherwise argue about later

A handshake works until the first damaged sofa. So every role that touches the house or guests gets a short written agreement. It doesn't need to be long. It needs to answer the questions you'd otherwise argue about.

Scope comes first. List the jobs by name from the job list. Anything not listed is extra or not included.

The fee base catches people. A percentage of what? Nightly revenue only? Nightly revenue plus cleaning fees? Payout after platform fees? Those give different numbers, and I'll show you how different in the Cedar Ridge math.

The quality standard is the OP.8 checklist, with photos. Insurance means general liability for anyone working in the house. Workers' compensation and auto coverage may apply, depending on the state and how the vendor is set up.

Data handling means guest details stay in the platform or your property management software. Keys and codes are individual. No shared master code. When someone leaves, their code gets changed.

And termination covers the handover: keys, platform removal, future bookings, and records.

  • Some states and cities require a real estate or property management license to manage a rental for someone else for pay

Slide 8 · Vet before you sign, and ask whether a paid co-host needs a license in your state

Vetting runs before the agreement, not after.

Ask for two references from other short-term rental owners. Friends don't count. Ask for proof of insurance and look at it. Ask the cleaner to do one paid trial turnover to the OP.8 standard before the first guest ever arrives.

For a co-host, ask how many properties they handle. Then ask who covers when two emergencies happen at the same time. The answer tells you whether there's a real backup or just one busy person.

Then the licensing question. Some states and cities require a real estate or property management license for anyone who manages a rental for someone else for pay. That can apply to a co-host or a manager. I can't tell you the rule for your state. An attorney or the state licensing board can, so ask before you sign.

A handyman retainer kept after the co-host's scope includes inspections. A messaging service while the co-host also answers guests. A manager's supply markup while the cleaner bills for supplies. Fix: a task ledger with exactly one payer per job.

Slide 9 · Vendor set, co-host, or manager: know what each covers so you don't pay twice

There are three broad setups for an owned house.

A vendor set means you hire each role yourself and coordinate them. You do the guest messaging and the approvals. It costs the least in fees and the most in your time.

A co-host usually takes on some or all of messaging, coordination, restocking, and local response. Cleaning and repairs are often still billed separately. Scope varies a lot, which is why the agreement matters.

A full manager usually takes the listing, pricing, messaging, coordination, and owner statements. Many use their own cleaners and vendors, and some add markups.

Paying twice happens quietly. You add a co-host whose scope includes monthly inspections, and you keep paying the handyman's inspection retainer. That's the example we'll price at Cedar Ridge. Or a manager charges a supply markup while the cleaner also bills for supplies.

The fix is a task ledger. Write every job in one column. Beside each job, name exactly one payer. If two names show up, cut one or rewrite a scope.

This is the number I'd work out before talking to any co-host. How big a percentage fee can the base case carry before cash flow hits zero?

Start with the room you have. NOI with a vendor set is $29,209. Take out loan payments of $23,637, and there's $5,572 left. Divide that by gross booking revenue of $74,275. You get 7.5%.

Slide 15 · Run your own cost check: find your break-even fee before you read any proposal

The exercise has three parts. Part A is the task ledger. List every job from the job list. Beside each, write who does it, the backup, who pays, and how. If a task has two payers, fix it before you sign anything.

Part B is the access matrix. One row per person, including backups.

Part C is on the slide. It's the cost check. Use your own underwriting from Sam's PA.4. If you haven't done that, use your best estimate of a year's revenue and costs, with a cleaner and a handyman retainer in costs.

Work down the lines in order. Get your cash flow with a vendor set. Divide it by gross to get your break-even fee. Only then look at the proposal. Put in the fee, the base, and the dollars. Slide 16 · Four decision rules settle the team before the calendar opens

These four rules turn the worksheet into a decision.

First, compare the proposed fee with your break-even fee. If it's higher, the base case goes negative. You can still go ahead. But only if you've decided to fund the gap from other income, and you've written that down. I want that decision on paper, not discovered in month four.

Second, check the weak case. If it goes negative by more than your reserves can cover for a year, reduce the scope or change the fee base. Before the permit renewal, the Cedar Ridge weak case is already -$5,475 with the vendor set, and -$11,208 with the 10% messaging co-host. Hold both against your reserve.

Third, every role needs a trained backup. If one doesn't have it, the calendar doesn't open.

Fourth, check access. If anyone has more platform access than their row requires, lower it now. It's easier on day one than after six months.

Pass all four, and the team is ready for its instructions.
