### 0.1.0 Learning outcome

By the end of this lesson you can explain, in plain terms, what changes when you own the house instead of leasing it. You can name who holds title, who carries the loan, who pays for a new roof, and who makes the final call on each decision. You can read the STR Concierge course as a sequence of decision gates. You know which gate can end a deal, and roughly how much money is at risk when you reach it. You also know which professional on your team answers which question.

### 0.1.1 Why this part of the course exists

Part One of this course taught arbitrage. You lease a unit, get written permission to host, furnish it, and run it. Your downside has edges. The lease has a term, the furniture has a price, and the rent is a known monthly number.

Owning removes those edges. You buy the building, the land under it, and every problem inside the walls. You sign a loan that runs for decades. Your cash goes in as a down payment and closing costs, and it does not come back out unless you refinance or sell.

That is the whole reason STR Concierge is a separate program. The skills from Part One still apply: pricing, cleaning, guest communication, local rules. What's new is the size of the commitment and the number of people whose sign-off you need before you commit.

This lesson is the map. It does not teach any single step in depth. Each later lesson does that. Here you'll see the order of the decisions, who helps with each one, and the points where walking away is the right answer.

### 0.1.2 Owner vs. arbitrage operator: what actually changes

Start with the legal and financial facts. The table below compares the two models on the items that change most.

| Item | Arbitrage operator (Part One) | Owner (Part Two) |
|---|---|---|
| Title | The landlord owns the property. You hold a lease. | You (or your entity) hold title. |
| Loan | None on the property. You may finance furniture. | A mortgage secured by the house. The lender may also want a personal guarantee, depending on the loan. |
| Up-front cash | Deposit, first month, furniture, setup, reserve | Down payment, closing costs, furniture, repairs, reserves |
| Monthly fixed cost | Rent set by the lease | Principal and interest, property tax, insurance, plus any HOA |
| Maintenance | Usually the landlord's for structure and systems, per the lease | Yours, all of it |
| Capital repairs (roof, HVAC, septic, water heater) | Usually the landlord's, per the lease | Yours, and they come in large amounts at irregular times |
| Local approvals | You need the city's permission and the landlord's written permission | You need the city's permission, and any HOA's. No landlord to ask. |
| Insurance | Your STR liability policy; the landlord insures the building | You insure the building and the liability, and the lender will require coverage |
| Who makes final decisions | The landlord can refuse, end, or not renew the lease | You decide, and you carry the result |
| Exit | Lease ends or you negotiate an early exit | Sell, refinance, change use, or hold. Each costs time and money. |
| What you build | Cash flow and an operating skill | Cash flow, loan paydown, possible change in value, and possible tax timing |

Read the "Maintenance" and "Capital repairs" rows twice. In arbitrage, a failed water heater is usually the landlord's problem. You lose some nights and call them. As an owner, it's your bill and your emergency. You pick the plumber, approve the cost, and pay it the same week.

The "Local approvals" row also changes shape. In arbitrage, a landlord can say no, and many do. As an owner, there's no landlord gate. But the city, the county, and any homeowners association still apply. Airbnb tells hosts to check HOA or co-op rules and local laws before hosting ([Airbnb Help Center, article 1397](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/1397)). Owning the deed does not give you the right to run a short-term rental at that address. Only the local rules do.

The last row is where 0.2 picks up. An owner can earn from four places at once. An operator earns mainly from one. Four possible streams sound better, and sometimes they are. But each one carries its own cost, and some of them never show up in your bank account until you sell. Lesson 0.2 tests that claim with numbers.

### 0.1.4 Your working team, and what each person answers

Owning brings more people into the deal. Each has a narrow job. Trouble starts when you ask one of them to answer a question that belongs to someone else. A real estate agent is not your tax advisor. A lender is not your insurer.

| Role | What they answer | What they do not answer |
|---|---|---|
| Buyer's agent | Comparable sales, offer terms, contingency timing, local market practice | Whether STR use is legal at the address; your tax result |
| Lender | Whether you qualify, the rate and points, required reserves, what income they will count | Whether the deal is a good investment for you |
| Home inspector | The physical condition of the house and its systems | Market value (that is the appraiser's job) |
| Appraiser | Market value for the lender | Condition problems an inspection would find |
| Insurance agent | Whether they will write an STR policy on this house, what it costs, what it excludes | Whether platform protection replaces a policy (it does not) |
| Title / escrow | Who owns the property now, liens, easements, how money moves at closing | Zoning or STR permission |
| CPA | How depreciation, losses, personal use, and sale tax apply to your household | Whether the lender will approve you |
| Real estate attorney (as needed) | Contract terms, HOA documents, entity and title questions, local STR law questions | Market rents |
| Local operators (cleaner, handyman, co-host if used) | Turnovers, access, repairs, guest issues on the ground | Pricing strategy, compliance decisions, your finances |
| STR Concierge team | The course process and worksheets | Legal, tax, lending, or insurance advice. Ask the team what support your membership includes. |

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau makes the inspector versus appraiser point directly. The appraiser estimates market value for the lender. The buyer is responsible for hiring the inspector ([CFPB on home inspections](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/close/schedule-a-home-inspection/)). Title coverage splits the same way. Lender's title insurance protects the lender. An owner's policy is what protects your equity ([CFPB, owner's title insurance](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-owners-title-insurance-en-164/); [CFPB, lender's title insurance](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-lenders-title-insurance-en-163/)).

One more role deserves a line. If you add a co-host on Airbnb later, you can set their permission level. You as the listing owner remain responsible for your co-hosts, including ones added by a full-access co-host ([Airbnb Help Center, article 1534](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/1534)). You can hand off tasks. You can't hand off accountability. OP.7 covers this in detail.

You make the final decision on every gate. Each professional gives you a fact or an opinion inside their lane. You combine them.

### 0.1.5 The decision gates that can end a deal

A deal moves forward only when it clears a gate. At each gate, one answer ends it. Ending a deal early is cheap. Ending it late costs real money.

Here is the flow. Read it top to bottom. Any "No" sends you back to the property list, not forward.

```
GATE 0  Readiness (0.3)
        Can we fund the full cash stack and keep our own emergency savings?
        No  -> wait, improve liquidity, or use the lease path (Part One)
        Yes |
            v
GATE 1  Buy box fit (PA.1)
        Does this property match the buy box on every hard requirement?
        No  -> pass
        Yes |
            v
GATE 2  Address-level STR permission (PA.2, SR.1)
        Is unhosted short-term rental use allowed at this exact address,
        including any HOA rules?
        No or unclear -> pass, or get a written answer before spending more
        Yes |
            v
GATE 3  Underwriting (PA.4)
        Does the weak case stay inside our downside limit at a real loan quote?
        No  -> pass, or lower the price (PA.8)
        Yes |
            v
GATE 4  Financing (PA.6, PA.7)
        Do we have a written quote we qualify for, with reserves we can meet?
        No  -> pass or restructure
        Yes |
            v
GATE 5  Offer ceiling (PA.8)
        Will the seller accept a price at or below our walk-away number?
        No  -> walk
        Yes |
            v
GATE 6  Diligence (PA.9)
        Inspection, insurance quote, title, appraisal, permit: any finding
        that breaks the numbers or the plan?
        Yes -> renegotiate or cancel inside the contingency window
        No  |
            v
GATE 7  Closing (PA.11)
        Do the Closing Disclosure and wire instructions match what we agreed?
        No  -> stop and verify before any money moves
        Yes |
            v
GATE 8  Launch readiness (PA.12, OP.6 to OP.8)
        Insured, safe, permitted, furnished, team in place?
        No  -> do not publish the listing yet
        Yes |
            v
GATE 9  30/90-day review (OP.10)
        Are actual results inside the range we underwrote?
        No  -> fix pricing, costs, or operations; revisit the plan
```

Three things about this flow.

Gates 2 and 3 are where most deals should die. They cost almost nothing to run. The city's rules are public. Sam's PA.4 underwriting uses your own spreadsheet. A buyer who treats these gates as formalities tends to find the problem at Gate 6. By then the inspections are paid for and earnest money is on the line.

Gate 6 has a clock. Inspection and financing contingencies run on deadlines in your purchase contract. If you miss a deadline, your options can shrink, depending on the contract. PA.8 and PA.9 cover how to set those windows.

Gate 9 is not a formality either. It is the first time the underwriting meets reality. OP.10 is Sam's walkthrough of that review. OP.11 uses it to decide whether a second property is on the table at all.

### 0.1.6 Worked example: Cedar Ridge against Alder Street

The two recurring examples in this course make the difference concrete. Alder Street is the Part One arbitrage unit. Cedar Ridge is the house Maya and Chris are considering. All figures are assumptions from the course's canonical example file.

**The properties.** Alder Street is a leased two-bedroom apartment. The rent is $1,950 a month on a 24-month lease. Cedar Ridge is a three-bedroom, two-bath house in a drive-to lake market about two hours from a metro area. Maya and Chris have a $385,000 contract price against a $399,000 asking price.

**Cash on day one.**

| | Alder Street (lease) | Cedar Ridge (own, DSCR quote) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Sunk setup / furnishing | $12,050 | $32,000 |
| Deposit, first month, operating reserve | $8,320 | (not in this line) |
| Down payment | none | $96,250 |
| Closing costs incl. 1 point | none | $14,438 |
| **Cash in before owner reserves** | **$20,370** | **$142,688** |

Step by step for Cedar Ridge: the down payment is 25% of $385,000, which is $96,250. Closing costs are 3% of price ($11,550) plus 1 point on the $288,750 loan ($2,888), for $14,438. Cash to close is $96,250 + $14,438 = $110,688. Add the $32,000 furnishing budget and the total is $142,688.

That is 7 times the Alder Street figure ($142,688 / $20,370 = 7.0). And the Cedar Ridge number still has no lender reserves or personal emergency savings in it. Lesson 0.3 adds those.

**Monthly fixed obligation.** Alder Street's rent is $1,950. Cedar Ridge's principal, interest, taxes, and insurance (PITIA) on the DSCR quote is $2,557. The owner's number also comes with repairs and capital replacements the tenant usually doesn't carry. The canonical model sets aside 5% of gross for repairs and another 5% for capital items. At base-case revenue, that is $3,714 each, $7,428 a year together.

**Bad-year exposure.** In Alder Street's stress case, the unit still nets $1,548 for the year. In Cedar Ridge's weak case, the owner loses $5,475 for the year after debt service on the DSCR quote. That money comes from reserves or from the household.

**Way out.** At Alder Street, the worst case is usually the remaining lease obligation plus the furniture. Twenty-four months of rent is $46,800, and the real exposure depends on the lease's early-termination terms. At Cedar Ridge, the way out is a sale. The canonical five-year sale model shows selling costs of $31,242 at 7% of an assumed $446,321 value. That is before any income tax on the sale.

**What Cedar Ridge offers that Alder Street doesn't.** In year one on the DSCR quote, the loan balance falls by $2,795. That is equity, though you can't spend it this year. The house may change in value. And depreciation may shift tax timing, depending on facts only a CPA can confirm. Lesson 0.2 separates those four streams and tests them.

The point of this comparison is not "ownership is worse" or "ownership is better". The commitment is seven times larger in cash, and the bad-year loss lands on you instead of being capped by a lease. Before you shop, you should know that.

### 0.1.7 Money at risk at each gate

One more view of Cedar Ridge helps you see why early gates matter. The table shows the money Maya and Chris would have committed or spent by the time each gate is decided. Figures marked (assumption) are new illustrative numbers for this lesson.

| Gate | Money spent or at risk if the deal dies here |
|---|---|
| 0 to 3: readiness, buy box, permission, underwriting | Time, and possibly a data subscription. No deposit yet. |
| 4: financing | Possibly an application or credit report fee, per the lender |
| 5: offer accepted | Earnest money of $3,850, 1% of price (assumption). Whether it is refundable depends on the contract and contingencies. |
| 6: diligence | Earnest money at risk, plus inspection fees of about $1,500 (assumption) for general, septic, and other specialist checks |
| 7: closing | $110,688 cash to close on the DSCR quote |
| 8: launch | Plus $32,000 furnishing, plus carrying costs until the first booking |

The first four gates cost almost nothing to fail. The last three cost thousands, then tens of thousands. So the work you do in PA.1 and in Sam's PA.2 to PA.4 is the cheapest protection you will buy in this program.

### 0.1.9 Who confirms what

**Confirm with:** the city or county licensing office that short-term rental use is allowed at the exact address and under any HOA rules, before an offer. Use an STR attorney if the answer is unclear.

**Confirm with:** your lender what reserves, credit, down payment, and guarantees their loan requires, in writing.

**Confirm with:** your insurance agent that they will write an STR policy for the specific house, and what it excludes, before your diligence window closes.

**Confirm with:** your real estate agent or attorney how the earnest money, inspection, and financing contingencies in your contract work, including every deadline.

**Confirm with:** your CPA how ownership, depreciation, and personal use affect your household's taxes before you count any tax benefit in a decision.
