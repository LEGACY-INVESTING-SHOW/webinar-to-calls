Arbitrage still works, one lease at a time

Slide 1 · Arbitrage still works, one lease at a time

People keep asking me whether arbitrage still works in 2026. My short answer is yes, at some addresses and not at others. By arbitrage I mean leasing an apartment, with the landlord's written permission, and renting it to short-stay guests. You keep what's left after rent and costs.

So we won't judge the whole market. We'll judge one apartment, at one rent, under one city's rules. Does it earn more than it costs in an ordinary bad month?

By the end you'll work out what each stay leaves behind, where you break even, a stress case, and how long it takes to earn back your setup cash. Then you'll run the unit through three gates and pick sign, investigate, or walk. Our example is Alder Street, a hypothetical two-bedroom from the course example.

Here are two sets of numbers that seem to disagree. In the second quarter of 2026, Airbnb's revenue rose 17% to $3.6 billion, and its bookings rose 10%. Travelers are still booking.

AirDNA sells short-term rental market data. For 2026 it expected supply to grow a little faster than demand, with occupancy down about 1% and nightly rates up about 1.5%. Its midyear update said a tougher economy favored existing hosts. Listings with reviews start ahead of you.

Both can be true. When more listings open, the new demand gets spread across more units. So I read these as weather reports. They don't tell you the rent your landlord wants or whether your city lets you host. Sam's 4.1 through 4.5 show you how to pull the data for your unit.

Slide 3 · A travel boom can't lower your rent

Every arbitrage deal has two kinds of cost. Some rise and fall with bookings: the platform fee, the cleaner, supplies. If nobody books, you don't pay them.

The others show up every month whether anyone books or not. Rent, utilities, insurance, software, a repair reserve, your permit fee. On most leased units, rent is the largest line on the page.

A strong travel season can nudge your nightly rate up a little. It can't touch your rent. So if the fixed costs are too high for what the unit can realistically earn, more visitors in the city won't close the gap. They book the listings with better locations, better reviews, or lower prices first.

Slide 4 · Each stay leaves a contribution toward fixed costs

Contribution is what one stay leaves behind after the costs that come with that stay. Here's the chain.

Booked nights times the nightly rate, plus the cleaning fee the guest pays, is the stay's gross revenue. Take off the platform fee. Our example uses Airbnb's 15.5% single fee on nightly plus cleaning. Then take off the cleaner and the supplies. What's left is the contribution.

Add up every stay's contribution for the year, subtract fixed costs, and you have net before income tax.

If a local manager takes a percentage, that fee moves with bookings too, so it's a variable cost. Lesson 6.5 covers that. And occupancy taxes pass straight through to the tax authority. They aren't your revenue.

Slide 5 · The gap to break-even is your cushion

Break-even occupancy is the share of nights you have to book for contributions to exactly cover fixed costs. Divide annual fixed costs by contribution per stay to get stays. Multiply by your average stay to get nights. Divide by 365.

The number I care about is the gap between break-even and what you expect to book. That gap is your cushion.

Break-even moves with price. Drop your rate in a slow month and each stay leaves less, so run it at your slow-month rate too.

New listings nearby eat into the same cushion. Before you sign, count the comparable listings near the address and look for new supply coming. Sam's 4.2 shows you how. Expect 70% and break even at 50%, and competition can take about 20 points before you lose money.

Slide 6 · A stress case shows what you can survive

I want two versions of every deal before you sign anything. The base case is what you expect. The stress case is what you can survive.

A useful stress case changes the two inputs the market decides for you: the nightly rate and the occupancy. Keep every other line the same. Then run it again with a higher rent, to test what happens if the landlord asks for more at renewal. I call that stress plus rent.

If the stress case is still positive, the deal has room. If it's barely positive, one more problem, like a rent increase, turns it negative. If it's negative, the lease has no margin for an ordinary slow year.

Occupancy moves in points: 70% down to 60% is 10 points.

Slide 7 · Three gates before you sign, permission first

I use three gates, and a deal has to pass all three before you sign a lease.

Gate one is address-level permission. The city, county, state, zoning, building, and any HOA rules have to allow unhosted, whole-unit short stays at this exact address. Module 3 teaches this gate.

Gate two is written landlord permission, in the lease or a signed addendum. A verbal okay isn't permission. Lessons 3.5, 5.3, and 5.7 cover it.

Gate three is the math. The stress case stays positive, even with a rent increase. Expected occupancy sits at least 10 points above break-even. Base payback is under half the lease. Those two thresholds are my suggested screens, not industry rules.

Check permission first. It's the cheapest to check and the most absolute.

Now let's put numbers on it. Alder Street is Unit 4B at 218 Alder Street, a hypothetical two-bedroom, two-bath apartment that sleeps five. It's near a hospital campus and downtown, in a city we don't name. The landlord has offered a 24-month lease. Every figure comes from the course example.

Slide 9 · At 70%, Alder Street nets $12,218 a year

Alder Street's fixed costs are $30,590 a year. Rent is $23,400 of that. The rest is utilities, insurance, software, a repair reserve, and the permit. That's about $2,549 a month before the first guest walks in.

At 70% occupancy you book 255 nights, which is 85 three-night stays. Eighty-five times $503.63 is about $42,808. Take off the $30,590 and you keep $12,218 a year before income tax. That's about $1,018 a month.

Break-even is $30,590 divided by $503.63, or 60.7 stays. That's 182.2 nights, or 49.9% occupancy. You expect 70%, so the cushion is about 20 points.

Gross booking revenue is $61,625, or 2.63 times the year's rent. Lesson 1.5 uses that ratio as a quick screen.

Slide 10 · The stress case survives until rent goes up

Here's the base case next to four what-ifs, as yearly net before income tax.

If the landlord asks for $2,150 at renewal, about 10% more, net drops to $9,818. If new listings pull you down to 64% at the same rate, net falls to $8,693. Still fine.

The stress case is the one to watch. The rate drops 12% to $180 and occupancy falls to 60%. Each stay now leaves $440.25, and the year nets $1,548, about $129 a month. Break-even at $180 is 57.1%, only about three points below you.

Stack the rent increase on top and net goes to minus $852. The deal survives each problem alone, but not two at once.

Slide 11 · A slow month at $180 loses about $788

An annual average hides the months when you lose money. In a slow season your fixed costs don't move, while bookings and rates both fall. And this is a cash problem. You pay rent on the first. The platform pays you after guests check in.

Say Alder Street's weakest month books 12 of 30 nights at the stress rate of $180. That's four stays, each leaving $440.25, so about $1,761. Fixed costs are still about $2,549. The month loses about $788.

That's why the operating reserve exists. Here it's $4,420, two months of rent plus utilities. It covers about 5.6 months like this one. For your own unit, take the weakest months from Sam's 4.3 AirDNA tutorial, not a guess.

Slide 12 · Payback takes 11.8 months, or 93 under stress

Setting up a unit costs money you don't get back: furniture, linens, safety gear, photos, the permit application. For Alder Street that sunk setup is $12,050. Another $8,320 is tied up in the deposit, the first month's rent, and the reserve, so you need $20,370 on day one.

Payback is sunk setup divided by monthly net. In the base case that's 11.8 months, just inside half the 24-month lease. Over the full lease you'd net about $24,432.

In the stress case you net about $129 a month, and payback stretches to about 93 months. That's almost four times the lease. If payback outlasts the lease, a landlord who declines to renew leaves you with used furniture and a loss.

Slide 14 · Run one real address through the sheet

Here's what to do this week. None of it needs a signed lease.

Pick one real address and fill in the viability decision sheet. Put a source and a date beside every input. The SR.2 underwriting spreadsheet does the formulas for you if you want it.

Pull 12 months of comparable rates and occupancy using Sam's 4.1 through 4.5, and note your three weakest months.

Then email the city or county licensing office and ask whether unhosted, whole-unit short stays are allowed at that exact address. Before you rely on any of it, get the city, the landlord, and an insurer to confirm in writing, and a CPA for anything after tax.
