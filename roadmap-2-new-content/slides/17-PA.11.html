Slide 2 · Money, documents, and control move in order

Closing looks like a pile of signatures. Underneath, three things move. Money goes from you and your lender to the seller and everyone else. Documents move ownership to you and give your lender a claim on the house. And control moves: keys, codes, accounts, records, and the right to occupy.

A title company or a closing attorney runs the closing. I'll call the person handling your file the settlement agent.

Most closing problems come from one of those three getting ahead of the others. You wire before you've checked the figures. You sign before the walkthrough. You get the deed but not the lock codes. So keep this order: check the figures, walk the house, review the documents, verify the wire by voice, sign what matches, then collect control. Ask your settlement agent early whether your funds must arrive before or after you sign.

Slide 3 · A DSCR loan may skip the Closing Disclosure, so ask early

For most consumer mortgages, the lender must give you a Closing Disclosure, its final form with your loan terms and costs. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, the CFPB, says you must get it at least three business days before closing, so you can compare it with your Loan Estimate.

A rental adds a wrinkle. The CFPB treats credit to buy a non-owner-occupied rental as business-purpose credit, and that falls outside many consumer disclosure rules. So a DSCR lender may give you a settlement statement instead. One exception: a vacation home the owner uses more than 14 days a year. Give your lender an honest number. Maya and Chris plan about 10.

Ask your lender in writing which document you'll get, and when. Then ask for final figures three business days ahead anyway.

Slide 4 · Check the final figures against what you agreed

Work through four groups, then the names.

Deal terms come from your contract: price, earnest money, any seller credit or repair escrow, and items the seller agreed to leave. For Cedar Ridge, the seller also pays a $3,100 contractor's lien from the title search. That payoff belongs on the seller's side, not yours.

Loan terms get checked against the written quote you accepted in PA.7. A rate or point change needs an explanation.

Closing costs go fee by fee, and your title charges should match the title company's quote.

Prepaids and escrow is where the insurance shows up. Make sure it's the STR policy from PA.9, not a generic homeowners estimate.

Then the names. They must match your PA.10 worksheet exactly. If cash to close doesn't match your estimate, ask the lender why.

Slide 5 · Cedar Ridge's cash to close came in $659 over the estimate

For this example, Maya and Chris closed on their backup DSCR loan. $385,000 price, a $288,750 loan at 7.25% with 1 point, $96,250 down. Total closing costs came to $14,438 against a $13,779 estimate. Cash to close is $110,688.

Now read the changes the way you would at your kitchen table. Insurance went up $600 and its escrow line $100, because the estimate used a generic homeowners premium and the final uses the real $3,200 STR quote from PA.9. That's the right policy. Just count that premium once in your year-one cash.

The appraisal, title, settlement, and survey lines added $245. Ask the lender and title company to explain each one. Prepaid interest dropped $286 because the closing moved later in the month.

The full line-by-line table is in the written lesson. Every dollar of change should have a reason.

Slide 6 · After earnest money, Cedar Ridge wires $102,988

Next, the wire amount. Suppose Maya and Chris paid $7,700 of earnest money at contract, 2% of price. That deposit is credited at closing. So they owe $110,688 minus $7,700, which is $102,988. That's the number they'll wire, and it's the number a scammer would love to redirect.

Your statement may label this figure differently, or its cash-to-close line may already subtract the deposit. So before wiring, confirm with the settlement agent which figure is the wire amount. By voice, at the saved number.

A few checks sit outside the fee table. The seller's septic and well invoices should be in the file. The lien payoff should be on the seller's side. And the monthly payment should read $1,970 principal and interest, about $2,557 with escrow.

Walk the house against your PA.9 tracker before you sign

  • Seller repairs done, invoices in hand
  • Included items still there
  • No new damage since inspection
  • Every tap, toilet, and system works
  • Photos of every room
  • Meter readings, with the time
Slide 7 · Walk the house against your PA.9 tracker before you sign

The CFPB says to use the final walkthrough to confirm agreed repairs were made and that items the seller agreed to leave are still there.

For an STR, it's also your first look at the house as an operator. So bring the PA.9 tracker and walk it row by row. See the septic work and the new well UV system, and get both invoices. Check the furniture and dock equipment. Look for water stains or storm damage. Run the taps, flush the toilets, test the HVAC in both modes. Then photograph every room and write down the meter readings with the time. You'll use those for final bills and the PA.12 handoff.

If you find a real problem, don't sign and hope. Call your agent, and your attorney if you have one. Common fixes are a repair escrow, a credit, or a short delay.

Settlement statementShows every dollar in and out
Slide 8 · Know what each document does before you sign

You'll sign a stack. Know what the main pieces do, and read the names on each one against your PA.10 worksheet.

The deed moves ownership to the title owner you chose, and it gets recorded with the county. Ask when you'll get the recorded copy.

The promissory note is the promise to repay. Read the rate, payment, maturity, and prepayment section.

The mortgage or deed of trust gives the lender a claim on the house. It holds the due-on-sale clause from PA.10.

If your loan is made to your LLC, the lender may want a personal guaranty. Read that with your attorney before closing day, not at the table.

Initial the settlement statement only after it matches your comparison. And check that your insurance binder starts on or before closing, with the right names.

Slide 9 · Verify every wire by voice at a number you saved

Closing-day wire fraud targets exactly your situation: a known date, a big transfer, and lots of people emailing each other. The CFPB warns that scammers pose as your agent or settlement agent with last-minute changes to wiring instructions.

Here's how I'd turn its guidance into a protocol. At contract, pick two trusted people, usually your agent and your settlement agent. Save their numbers from a card in your hand or a website you typed yourself. Agree on a code phrase and ask for it on every call about money.

Before you send anything, call the settlement agent at the saved number. Read back the bank name, account name, account number, and amount. After the wire, call again and confirm they got the exact amount.

Maya and Chris saved their settlement agent's number from the title company's website at contract.

Treat any change to wire instructions as fraud

  • Call the saved number, never the email's
  • Never email financial information
  • Don't trust caller ID
  • Wire gone wrong? Call your bank now
Slide 10 · Treat any change to wire instructions as fraud

A 'new account' email is the classic move. Don't reply to it. Stop and call the saved number.

Don't email financial information. The CFPB says email is never a secure way to send it.

Don't trust caller ID either. Scammers can spoof phone numbers. If a call feels wrong, hang up and call the saved number yourself.

And if something does go wrong, minutes matter. Call your bank or wire service right away to try to stop or reverse the transfer, and file a complaint with the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center.

For Cedar Ridge, that's $102,988 on the line. Ask your bank this week who to call and what its cutoff times are, so you aren't looking it up in a panic.

Slide 11 · Collect control before you leave the closing

The deed gets recorded after closing, but control transfers only when you have every way into the house and every record about it. Your contract says when possession passes.

Collect six things that day. Physical keys, remotes, and passes. Door and alarm codes and the admin logins for every smart device. Ask the seller to remove their accounts, then reset every code yourself.

Warranties and manuals, including the well UV system. Vendor records, like the septic invoices, water tests, and the seller's plow and trash services. Permit records. And proof of payment: final figures, wire confirmation, title documents, and insurance declarations.

Anything you don't get goes on the unresolved items log.

Slide 14 · Set up your wire protection before closing week

Three things this week.

First, email your lender and ask which closing document you'll get and when the final figures arrive.

Second, save the settlement agent's number from a source you trust, and agree on a code phrase. While you're at it, call your bank and write down its wire cutoff time and who to call if a wire needs stopping.

Third, copy the comparison table and fill in what you agreed now, from your offer, your PA.9 amendment, and your loan quote. When the final figures arrive, you only have one column left.

After closing you own the house, but it isn't a rental yet. PA.12 covers that. Bring your unresolved items log and the PA.9 tracker.
