Slide 2 · Write down who books the house before you open a single catalog

Most furnishing mistakes start in a store. Someone falls for a sofa, then makes the house fit the sofa. I want you to reverse that.

Write down who books this house and what they'll do there. Then buy for that person. The profile has four parts. The fourth one, complaints, comes from reading reviews of similar nearby listings, the comparable set. They're in the underwriting package Sam walks through in PA.4. If you skipped that, pick three similar listings nearby. Their reviews tell you what guests in this market already dislike.

Then write the other list: what this house is not for. If you won't allow parties, you don't need a sound system. If the lower level only has stairs, you can't imply step-free access.

Every accessibility claim has to be true on the day a guest arrives. Airbnb's ground rules for hosts say the listing should describe the home and the amenities available from check-in to checkout. So the guest profile also protects you from promising things the house can't deliver.

Set the sleeping layout before you buy, and count it by bed, not by room

  • The layout drives bookings, linen count, cleaning time, and insurance
  • If your guest count depends on a sofa bed, say so plainly in the listing
  • Airbnb counts a bunk as 2 beds, so list bed types next to the guest count
  • Check 1: occupancy limits and permit conditions on guest count (PA.9)
  • Check 2: egress, a way out in a fire from each bedroom, that officials accept
  • Check 3: insurance, because a mismatched guest count can create a claim problem
Slide 3 · Set the sleeping layout before you buy, and count it by bed, not by room

Guests check the sleeping layout twice. It's the one number they read closely, because it decides whether their group fits. It also sets your linen count and how long every turnover takes.

So I set it first, before any purchase. And I count sleeping spots by bed. Six beds can mean six people or twelve, depending on whether they're twins or queens. So list the bed types and the guest count together.

Airbnb's sleeping arrangements article says a bunk bed counts as 2 beds in the bed count on the listing. Keep that in mind when you read the Cedar Ridge numbers in a minute.

Before you lock the layout, run three checks. Occupancy limits and permit conditions should already be in your PA.9 due diligence notes. If not, ask the licensing office. Egress means a way out in a fire. Every sleeping room needs one that your building or fire official accepts, so ask them what counts. And insurance: if the policy says one guest count and the listing says another, that gap can become a claim problem. Settle all three before the bunk beds get ordered.

Work space and storage

  • A workspace is a desk, chair, lamp, and outlets within reach
  • If the internet can't hold a video call, don't advertise remote work
  • Test the speed at the desk; write it down for the listing and the handoff packet
  • Guests need space for a suitcase and clothes; the owner needs a locked closet

Outdoor use

  • Lake houses earn their rate outside, and many injuries happen there
  • A grill needs cleaning, fuel checks, and a placement your insurer accepts
  • A fire pit, dock, kayak, or hot tub each carries its own insurance question
  • If you can't maintain it every turnover, don't list it
Slide 4 · Work space, storage, and outdoor gear get skipped in budgets, then show up in reviews

Three areas get left out of most furnishing budgets. Then they appear in reviews.

The first is the work space. If you claim a dedicated workspace, a guest expects a desk, a chair, a lamp, and outlets they can reach. And the internet has to hold a video call. Test the speed at the desk itself, not in the kitchen. Write the result in your handoff packet, the property reference file from PA.12.

The second is storage, and it means two things. Guests need somewhere to put a suitcase and hang clothes for a four-night stay. The owner needs a locked place for personal items, spare inventory, and cleaning stock. Mix those two and you get the classic review: closets full of the owner's stuff.

The third is outdoor use. A lake house earns its rate on the deck and the water. That's also where many injuries happen. Every outdoor item is a maintenance job and an insurance question. If you can't keep it right every turnover, meaning the clean and reset between stays, leave it out of the listing.

Safety equipment is the first line in the budget, and CPSC guidance sets the floor

Airbnb: say whether smoke and CO (carbon monoxide) alarms are present, and keep them working. The CPSC (Consumer Product Safety Commission) list is a planning floor, not your code.

  • Smoke alarms: every level, outside each sleeping area, every bedroom
  • CO alarms: every level, outside sleeping areas
  • At least one fire extinguisher
  • TVs and furniture anchored; no dangling cords
  • Pools, spas: compliant drain covers; fence at least 4 feet, self-closing, self-latching gates
  • Cribs meet CPSC standards; bare sleep space
  • Cleaning supplies locked or out of reach
  • Recall check at SaferProducts.gov
Slide 5 · Safety equipment is the first line in the budget, and CPSC guidance sets the floor

Safety goes first in the budget, not last. Nothing gets cut from this layer later.

Airbnb's safety requirements say hosts must state whether smoke and carbon monoxide alarms are present. If they are, they must work and you must test them regularly. The same page says you must deal with fire and electrical hazards. That covers blocked exits, dead smoke detectors, exposed wiring, and poorly kept hot tubs, gas appliances, and furnaces. You're responsible for any area guests can use.

The checklist on this slide comes from the Consumer Product Safety Commission's vacation rental safety tips. It covers alarms, extinguishers, anchoring, pools, cribs, and cleaning supplies.

This is consumer guidance, and your local code may ask for more. Your city, county, or state may require more, such as alarms that are wired to each other. So I treat this list as a planning floor, and I ask the fire marshal what this address needs.

Slide 6 · Cameras stay outside and disclosed, and nothing that records goes indoors

Security devices come next, because they touch privacy and listing accuracy at the same time.

Airbnb's device rules ban security cameras and recording devices that monitor indoor spaces. That holds even if the device is turned off. Hidden cameras are prohibited everywhere.

Exterior cameras are allowed, with conditions. The listing has to disclose where they are. They can't point into the house. And they can't cover areas where people expect more privacy, like an enclosed outdoor shower or a sauna.

Noise monitors that only measure sound level are allowed in common spaces. They have to be disclosed, and they stay out of bedrooms, bathrooms, and sleeping areas.

So at Cedar Ridge the plan is simple. If Maya and Chris want a camera, they buy a doorbell camera. It goes outside, and its location goes into the listing. The noise monitor sits in a common area and gets disclosed. Check Airbnb's current page before you publish, since these rules change.

Slide 7 · Every item has three prices: purchase, turnover minutes, and replacement

Every item you buy has three prices. There's the purchase price. There's the minutes it adds to each turnover. And there's the cost of replacing it. The cheapest sofa is often the most expensive one by year three.

These buying rules hold up. Washable fabrics and removable covers let the cleaner handle a spill without a service call. Encasements go on every mattress on the first day. A stained mattress is a replacement. A stained encasement is a wash.

White or one-color bedding from one product line means any sheet fits any bed of that size. Dishware you can rebuy by the piece means one broken plate doesn't mean a new set.

Then think in cleaning minutes. Twelve decorative pillows get fluffed and placed after every stay. The cleaner is paid per turnover, not per hour. So extra minutes either get rushed or show up later as a higher rate. Buy for the turnover, not the photo.
