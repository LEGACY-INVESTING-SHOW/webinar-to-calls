Slide 1 · Owning swaps a lease's capped risk for an open-ended one, so check every deal at ten gates

Welcome to Part Two. This is STR Concierge, the ownership side of the course. STR means short-term rental.

In Part One you learned to lease a unit and run it. Here you buy the house. That sounds like the same business with a bigger check. It isn't quite. The check is bigger, the loan runs for decades, and you'll hear from more professionals before you commit.

So this first lesson is a map. I'm not teaching any single step in depth today. Each later lesson does that. By the end of this one, you should be able to say who holds title and who carries the loan. You'll also know who pays for a new roof, and who makes the final call. You'll see the course as ten go/no-go gates. And you'll know which gates can end a deal, and roughly how much money is on the line when you reach each one.

I'll use Cedar Ridge, the lake house Maya and Chris are looking at. I'll hold it next to Alder Street, the Part One apartment, so you can see the difference in real numbers.

Slide 2 · A lease has edges; owning removes them

Think about what capped your downside at Alder Street. The lease had a term. The furniture had a price. The rent was a known monthly number. If things went badly, you knew roughly where the bottom was.

Owning takes those edges away. You buy the building and the land under it. You sign a loan that runs for decades. Your cash goes in as a down payment and closing costs, and it doesn't come back out unless you refinance or sell.

That's the whole reason STR Concierge is its own program. Your Part One skills still work here. Pricing, cleaning, guest communication, and local rules all carry over. What changes is how much you commit, and how many professionals you need before you commit it.

Keep that frame for the rest of Module 0. Every table I show you is really asking two things. How big is the commitment? And who do you need to hear from before you make it? You still make the call.

Slide 3 · As an owner you hold title, carry the loan, and pay for the roof

Here's the side-by-side. I want you to read two rows twice: maintenance and capital repairs. Maintenance is routine upkeep. Capital repairs replace a big piece of the building.

At Alder Street, if the water heater fails, it's usually the landlord's problem. You lose a few nights, you call them, and they fix it. Check your lease, but that's the normal split.

At Cedar Ridge, it's your bill and your emergency. You pick the plumber. You approve the cost. You pay it that same week. Roofs, HVAC, septic, and water heaters don't break on a schedule. They come in big amounts at odd times.

The loan row matters too. A mortgage is secured by the house. Depending on the loan, the lender may also want a personal guarantee. That means your household can be on the hook, and not just the property.

And look at up-front cash. The operator's list is deposit, first month, and furniture. The owner's list starts with a down payment and closing costs, and it still needs furniture and reserves on top.

Slide 4 · Owning the deed does not give you the right to host; only local rules do

The approvals row changes shape when you own. In arbitrage, a landlord can say no, and many do. As an owner, there's no landlord gate. That feels like freedom. But the city, the county, and any homeowners association still apply.

Airbnb's own help center tells hosts to check HOA or co-op rules and local laws before hosting. Owning the deed doesn't give you the right to run a short-term rental at that address. Only the local rules do. We'll turn that into a hard gate in a minute.

Now the last row. An owner can earn from four places at once: cash flow, loan paydown, a change in value, and tax timing. Tax timing means depreciation can lower your tax now, and some of it can come back when you sell. An operator earns mainly from one. Four streams sound better, and sometimes they are. But each one carries its own cost. Some of them never reach your bank account until you sell.

That's exactly what lesson 0.2 tests with numbers. For now, just notice that "what you build" is a longer list with longer strings attached.

Slide 6 · Each stage hands the next one a document, so a skipped stage leaves a gap

Here's why the order matters. Each stage produces a document, and the next stage uses it.

PA.1 produces your buy box. Sam's PA.2 and PA.3 use that buy box to pick a market and build a list of properties. PA.4 produces an underwriting package for one property. PA.8 uses that package to set your offer ceiling. Then PA.9 checks the package against what the inspector and the city actually say. PA.5 to PA.7 sit in between. So picture someone who skips the buy box and goes straight to touring houses. What does Sam's pipeline filter on? Nothing. What does the underwriting test against? Their mood that day.

That's what I mean by a gap. Each document is the input to the next decision. When you're tempted to jump ahead, ask which document you're missing.

Slide 7 · Each professional answers inside one lane, and you combine the answers

Owning brings more people into the deal. Each one has a narrow job. Trouble starts when you ask someone a question that belongs to someone else.

Your agent knows comparable sales and offer terms. Your agent does not know whether short-term rental use is legal at that address, and shouldn't be guessing about your taxes.

Your lender tells you whether you qualify, the rate and points, and what income they'll count. Points are upfront loan fees. The lender does not tell you whether this is a good investment. A lender approval means they expect to be repaid. It says nothing about whether you'll be happy.

The inspector and the appraiser get mixed up all the time. The inspector looks at condition. The appraiser estimates value for the lender. Neither does the other's job.

And the insurance agent tells you whether they'll write a short-term rental policy on this specific house. Don't assume platform protection replaces that policy. We'll come back to that in PA.12.

You make the final call on every gate. These people each hand you one fact.

Slide 8 · Title, tax, and local help each answer a narrow question, never your whole deal

Here's the rest of the team.

Title and escrow tell you who owns the property now, what liens and easements sit on it, and how money moves at closing. They don't tell you whether the city allows short-term rentals there. A clean title says nothing about zoning.

Your CPA handles depreciation, losses, personal-use days, and tax on a future sale, for your household. Your CPA can't tell you whether a lender will approve you.

Bring in a real estate attorney as needed. Contract terms, HOA documents, entity and title questions, and local short-term rental law are their lane.

Your local cleaner, handyman, and maybe a co-host run the ground game. Turnovers, access, repairs, guest problems. They don't set pricing strategy or make compliance calls.

And the STR Concierge team helps with the course process and the worksheets. We don't give legal, tax, lending, or insurance advice. Ask the team what support your membership includes.

Where buyers get caught

Other people do parts of the work, but you carry the result

  • The appraiser works for the lender. The inspector works for you, and hiring one is your job (CFPB).
  • A lender's title policy protects the lender. An owner's policy protects your equity (CFPB).
  • On Airbnb, you set each co-host's permissions. You stay responsible for co-hosts, even ones a full-access co-host adds (article 1534).
  • Each professional gives you a fact or an opinion inside their lane. You make the final decision at every gate.
0.1 · Welcome to STR Concierge§ 0.1.49 / 18
Slide 9 · Other people do parts of the work, but you carry the result

Three places where responsibility splits in a way new owners miss.

First, inspection. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau is direct about this. The appraiser works for the lender's question, which is value. You, the buyer, are responsible for hiring the inspector. If you skip it, nobody else is checking the roof on your behalf.

Second, title insurance. The lender will require a lender's policy. That one protects the lender. If you want your own equity protected, that's an owner's policy. Ask about it at closing. PA.11 covers this.

Third, co-hosts. If you add a co-host on Airbnb later, you choose their permission level. But Airbnb says the listing owner remains responsible for co-hosts. That includes ones added by a full-access co-host. You can hand off tasks. You can't hand off accountability. OP.7 goes deeper on this.

The pattern across all three is the same. Other people do parts of the work. You carry the result.

Slide 10 · Gates 0 to 3 cost only time, so most bad deals should die here

A deal moves forward only when it clears a gate. At each gate, one answer ends it. There are ten gates, numbered 0 to 9, and this slide shows the first five.

Gate 0 is readiness, from lesson 0.3. Gate 1 is your buy box. Gate 2 is permission at the exact address. Unhosted means you don't live there during the stay. SR.1 is the regulatory compliance checklist. Gate 3 is underwriting. The weak case is a bad-year version of your numbers. Your downside rule, from PA.1, is the most you'll accept losing in that year. Gates 0 to 3 cost only time. Gate 2 and Gate 3 are where most bad deals should die. The city's rules are public. Sam's PA.4 underwriting runs in your own spreadsheet.

Here's the trap. A buyer who treats these gates as formalities tends to find the problem later, at Gate 6. By then the inspections are paid for and earnest money is on the line.

So when a house fails Gate 2, don't argue with it. Pass, or get a written answer before you spend another dollar.

Slide 11 · From Gate 5 on, real money is at stake, and Gate 6 runs on a contract clock

Here are the second five gates. Notice the money changes here.

Gate 5 is the offer. If the seller won't come to your walk-away number, you walk. PA.8 shows you how to set that number.

Gate 6 is diligence: inspection, insurance quote, title, appraisal, and permit. A No means a finding broke the numbers or the plan. Then you renegotiate or cancel. And this gate has a clock. Contingencies are conditions in your purchase contract that let you cancel, and they run on deadlines. Miss one, and your options can shrink, depending on the contract. PA.8 and PA.9 cover how to set those windows.

Gate 7 is closing. The Closing Disclosure is the lender's final statement of costs. Check it and the wire instructions against what you agreed. If anything is off, stop before money moves.

Gate 8 is launch. Don't publish the listing until it's insured, safe, permitted, furnished, and staffed.

Gate 9 happens after you own. It's the first time your underwriting meets reality. Sam walks through that review in OP.10.

Slide 16 · Five confirmations, each due before a set point in the deal

Here's who confirms what, and when.

The city or county licensing office confirms that short-term rental use is allowed at the exact address, and under any HOA rules. Do that before an offer. If the answer is unclear, get an STR attorney.

Your lender confirms reserves, credit, down payment, and any guarantee, in writing. A verbal "you should be fine" doesn't count.

Your insurance agent confirms they'll write a short-term rental policy on this specific house, and what it excludes. Get that before your diligence window closes. If you can't get it insured at a price the numbers can carry, treat that as a failed gate.

Your agent or attorney explains how earnest money and the inspection and financing contingencies work in your contract. That includes every deadline. Put those dates on a calendar the day you sign.

And your CPA confirms how ownership, depreciation, and personal use affect your household's taxes. Do that before you count any tax benefit in a decision. Until then, the tax benefit is zero in your math.
