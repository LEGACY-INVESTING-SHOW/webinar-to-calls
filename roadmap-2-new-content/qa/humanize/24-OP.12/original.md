### OP.12.0 Learning outcome

After this lesson you can put four options side by side for a property you own. They are hold, refinance, sell, or sell through a 1031 exchange. You'll compare them on after-tax cash, time to complete, future debt, ongoing work, and opportunity cost. You'll know how the gain on a sale is built, which parts depreciation touches, and which answers only your CPA can give. You'll leave with a decision worksheet you can hand to that CPA and to a qualified intermediary. That's the independent company that holds your sale money during a 1031 exchange.

### OP.12.1 One question behind all four options

Every exit decision comes down to one question. Is the equity in this property doing more for you here than it would do somewhere else?

Equity is the value you could turn into cash: market value minus the loan balance, minus the costs of getting it out. Holding keeps that equity in the house. Refinancing pulls some of it out as a new loan. Selling turns it into cash, minus taxes. A 1031 exchange moves it into another investment property and may defer the tax.

Each option mixes five things differently. They are after-tax cash, time to complete, future debt, the work you keep doing, and what you give up (opportunity cost). No option wins on all five. You're choosing a trade-off you can live with.

You'll make this call more than once. Use it whenever your 90-day or annual review shows a real change. That review comes from the 30-Day and 90-Day Performance Review lesson (OP.10, taught by Sam). Examples: a big capital repair, a new local rule, a life change, or equity that has outgrown the cash flow.

### OP.12.2 Start with an honest equity number

Four inputs start every exit analysis.

The updated value. Get it from a broker's opinion of value, recent comparable sales, or an appraisal. Don't use the price you'd like. Short-term rental (STR) houses sometimes sell with furniture and bookings. Ask the broker whether buyers in your market pay for that.

The loan balance. Take it from the servicer's latest statement, not your own amortization schedule. The servicer is the company that collects your loan payments. If you plan to sell, request a payoff quote; it can include items the statement doesn't show.

Capital repairs coming in the next few years. A roof, HVAC system, or deck replacement changes the hold case. It can also change the sale price if a buyer's inspector finds it.

The cost of selling: agent commissions, transfer taxes, title and closing fees, and any seller concessions (costs you agree to cover for the buyer). The Cedar Ridge example later in this lesson uses 7% of value (assumption). Your agent can give you a line-by-line net sheet.

Value − loan balance − selling costs = net proceeds before income tax. That is not your spendable number yet.

### OP.12.3 Hold, refinance, or sell: the before-tax comparison

Holding is the default. It needs no transaction, but it has a cost you don't see on a statement. Cedar Ridge, the example later in this lesson, earns about $5,172 a year in cash flow after the mortgage (assumption). That's on about $142,560 of equity, so roughly 3.6%. If you could sell and put the after-tax cash somewhere safer at a similar return, holding carries more risk for the same money. That comparison is your opportunity cost.

Holding makes sense when the property still covers its costs in a weak season, the capital repairs are funded, and the work is manageable. It also makes sense when a sale would trigger a large tax bill you'd rather defer. The property should still meet the goals you set in PA.1 (your buy box).

Refinancing lets you keep the property and pull out cash. The previous lesson, OP.11, showed the math: new loan, costs, new payment, and the weak-season test. That test asks whether the property still pays all its bills, including the mortgage, in a bad year. A refinance doesn't trigger a sale, so it doesn't trigger tax on a gain. It does add debt and payments. Refinance when you have a specific, better use for the cash and the property can carry the bigger loan in a weak year.

Selling ends the risk and the work. It turns equity into cash you can redeploy or keep. It also brings the tax consequences in the next section. Sell when the property no longer fits your goals or the local rules have tightened. Also sell when a large repair doesn't pay back, or the equity has a clearly better use.

### OP.12.4 How the gain on a sale is built

You don't need to calculate your own tax. You do need to know how the calculation is built, so you can collect the right records and read your CPA's answer. Every rule below depends on your facts and the tax year. Treat it as the structure of the conversation, not a result.

**Gain = amount realized − adjusted basis.** Amount realized is roughly the sale price minus selling costs. Adjusted basis starts with what you paid plus certain acquisition costs, goes up by improvements, and goes down by depreciation. [IRS Publication 544](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p544) describes adjusted basis this way, including decreases for depreciation.

**Depreciation reduces basis whether or not you claimed it.** Suppose you didn't claim all the depreciation you were entitled to. [IRS Publication 527](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p527) says you still reduce basis by the full amount you could have deducted. The IRS phrase is depreciation "allowed or allowable." Skipping depreciation doesn't protect you at sale. It only means you lost the deduction. Publication 527 also says basis goes down by any special depreciation allowance (bonus depreciation) you claimed. Bonus depreciation means deducting a large share, or all, of an asset's cost in its first year, if it's eligible. Basis also goes down by any section 179 deduction, another way to expense an asset up front.

One more term before the split. The cost-segregation lesson (PA.5, taught by Sam) showed how a study can move part of the building into 5-, 7-, and 15-year property. If it's eligible, bonus depreciation can deduct it in year one.

**The gain then gets split into pieces that can be taxed differently.** Your CPA does this split. Here is the structure:

1. Personal property is generally section 1245 property. That includes furniture and many building parts a cost-segregation study moves to 5- or 7-year lives. Land improvements on 15-year lives can be treated differently, so your CPA classifies each asset. [Publication 544](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p544) says gain on section 1245 property is treated as ordinary income to the extent of depreciation allowed or allowable. The ordinary part is the lesser of that depreciation or the gain on those assets. Those assets can show a gain because part of the sale price gets assigned to them, while depreciation has shrunk their basis. Example: say furniture fully depreciated to $0 is assigned $6,000 of the sale price (assumption). That produces $6,000 of ordinary income. So the recapture depends on how much of the sale price is allocated to those items. That allocation is a CPA and appraisal question.
2. The building itself is section 1250 property. Gain tied to straight-line depreciation on it is called unrecaptured section 1250 gain. [IRS Topic 409](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc409) says that portion is taxed at a maximum 25% rate.
3. Any remaining gain is section 1231 gain under [Publication 544](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p544). Your CPA nets it with your other gains and losses for the year. The rate depends on that netting and your income, so the example below uses a placeholder rate.

Two more items belong on the CPA list. First, if depreciation losses from the property were suspended under the passive activity rules, ask how a sale affects them ([IRS Publication 925](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p925)). Suspended means you couldn't use the losses yet, so they carried forward. Second, ask which state income taxes and any other federal taxes apply to your gain. I have not modeled either here.

Cost segregation matters at this step. Its faster deductions reduce basis faster. At sale, more of the gain can land in the ordinary-income bucket. The study mostly changes timing. It can still be worth it, as the Cedar Ridge example in OP.12.6 shows. Resource SR.3 is the tool for that conversation.

### OP.12.5 The 1031 exchange: what qualifies and the clock

A like-kind exchange under section 1031 lets you sell one investment property and buy another. You defer the gain instead of paying tax on it now. Deferral is not forgiveness. Ask your CPA how the deferred gain affects the replacement property's basis and future depreciation.

**What qualifies.** Since 2018, section 1031 applies only to real property held for use in a trade or business or for investment. Real property held primarily for sale doesn't qualify ([IRS like-kind exchange tips](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/like-kind-exchanges-real-estate-tax-tips)). Both the property you sell and the one you buy must be held that way. The IRS says property used primarily for personal use, like a primary residence, second home, or vacation home, does not qualify. Exchanges of machinery, equipment, vehicles, and other personal property generally no longer qualify after 2017. That means the furniture in an STR generally can't be part of the like-kind exchange.

**The two deadlines.** You have 45 days from the sale of your property to identify possible replacement properties. The identification must be in writing, signed by you, and delivered to a person involved in the exchange, such as the qualified intermediary. You must receive the replacement property by the earlier of two dates. One is the 180th day after the sale. The other is the due date, including extensions, of your tax return for the year of the sale ([IRS like-kind exchange tips](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/like-kind-exchanges-real-estate-tax-tips); [Form 8824 instructions](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8824)). The IRS treats these deadlines as strict.

**The qualified intermediary (QI).** In a deferred exchange you use a QI to hold the sale proceeds. Your own agent can't serve as the QI. The IRS list includes your real estate agent or broker, accountant, attorney, and employee. It also covers anyone who acted in those roles for you in the previous two years ([IRS like-kind exchange tips](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/like-kind-exchanges-real-estate-tax-tips)). The IRS also warns that some intermediaries have gone bankrupt or failed to perform, and that cost taxpayers their deadlines. Vet the QI: ask how funds are held, what bonding or insurance exists, and who signs withdrawals.

**Reporting.** You report the exchange on [Form 8824](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8824). Ask your CPA how any cash you receive, or a smaller replacement loan, would be treated on that form.

**The vacation-home caveat.** An STR owner who also stays at the property has a personal-use question. The IRS issued Revenue Procedure [Rev. Proc. 2008-16](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/rp-08-16.pdf) as a safe harbor. Under it, the IRS will not challenge whether a dwelling unit with occasional personal use qualifies as property held for investment. The safe harbor has qualifying-use standards in its section 4.02. Summaries of that section describe a holding period around the exchange, a minimum number of fair-rental days, and a cap on personal use. I couldn't confirm the exact counts from the IRS text, so I don't state them here. Have your CPA check them against section 4.02 before you rely on them. Outside the safe harbor, qualification depends on your facts. Don't assume a property you use personally qualifies automatically.

### OP.12.6 Worked example: Cedar Ridge at the end of year 5

Cedar Ridge is the course's example house. Maya and Chris bought it for $385,000 with a $288,750 DSCR loan. DSCR stands for debt service coverage ratio: the lender qualifies the loan mainly on the property's income compared with its payment. They put about $142,688 of cash in at closing, including furniture. Now it's the end of year 5, and they never refinanced. Every figure below is an assumption. Tax rates are placeholders for the CPA to replace.

All cash-flow figures below include the $400-a-year permit renewal found during due diligence (PA.9).

**Step 1. Equity and before-tax proceeds.**

- Value: $446,321 (3% a year growth, assumption).
- Loan balance: $272,519. They paid down $16,231 of principal in five years.
- Selling costs at 7%: $31,242.
- Net proceeds before income tax: $446,321 − $272,519 − $31,242 = $142,560.

For comparison, they put about $142,688 of cash in at closing. After five years of paydown and 3% growth, their equity net of selling costs is almost exactly what they started with.

**Step 2. Hold.** The example assumes net operating income stays flat. Net operating income (NOI) is booking revenue minus operating costs, before the mortgage. On the original financing, that gives base-case cash flow of $5,172 and weak-case cash flow of -$5,875 a year. On $142,560 of net equity, the base case earns 3.6% ($5,172 / $142,560). That's before any appreciation, which is not spendable income.

**Step 3. Refinance at year 5 instead.** They use the same refinance terms as earlier in the course: 75% loan-to-value, 2.5% costs, and a 7.0% rate (all assumptions). Loan-to-value (LTV) is the new loan as a share of the property's value.

- New loan: $446,321 × 0.75 = $334,741.
- Costs: $8,369.
- Cash out: $334,741 − $272,519 − $8,369 = $53,853.
- New principal and interest: $26,724 a year ($2,227 a month, 30-year amortization, assumption).
- Cash flow after: base $2,085 ($28,809 NOI − $26,724); weak -$8,962 ($17,762 NOI − $26,724).

They'd get $53,853 now and keep the property. Their base-case margin would shrink to $2,085 a year, and a weak year would cost $8,962.

**Step 4. Sell: build the gain two ways.** The amount realized is $446,321 − $31,242 = $415,079. The adjusted basis depends on how they depreciated. I simplified: full-year straight-line for five years, with the building on a 39-year life. This example assumes short average stays make it transient (nonresidential) use. A 27.5-year residential life would give larger yearly figures. Your CPA decides which applies. The real first year also depends on the month each asset was placed in service and the timing convention your CPA uses.

| | Scenario A: no cost-seg study (furniture still expensed with bonus) | Scenario B: cost-seg study + 100% bonus |
|---|---:|---:|
| Starting basis (price + $6,000 capitalized costs) | $391,000 | $391,000 |
| Of which land, not depreciable (20%, assumption) | $78,200 | $78,200 |
| Of which building | $312,800 | $312,800 |
| Furnishings basis | $32,000 | $32,000 |
| Depreciation on 5/7/15-year building parts (bonus) | $0 | -$68,816 |
| Depreciation on furnishings (bonus, if eligible) | -$32,000 | -$32,000 |
| Building straight-line, 5 years | -$40,105 ($8,021 × 5) | -$31,280 ($6,256 × 5) |
| **Total depreciation** | **-$72,105** | **-$132,096** |
| **Adjusted basis** | **$350,895** | **$290,904** |
| Amount realized | $415,079 | $415,079 |
| **Gain** | **$64,184** | **$124,175** |

The land and building rows show what's inside the $391,000; they aren't subtracted again. The building's yearly figure is $312,800 ÷ 39 = $8,021 in Scenario A. In Scenario B, the study moved $68,816 out, so $243,984 ÷ 39 = $6,256.

Now the split. The recapture on section 1245 items depends on how much of the price is allocated to them. I assumed $6,000 for the used furniture in both cases, plus $30,000 for the reclassified building parts in Scenario B. These allocations are assumptions only. I also treated all $30,000 as section 1245 property for simplicity; the CPA may classify the 15-year parts differently.

| Piece of the gain | Scenario A | Scenario B | Placeholder rate |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Section 1245 recapture (ordinary) | $6,000 | $36,000 | 32% (assumption) |
| Unrecaptured section 1250 gain | $40,105 | $31,280 | 25% (maximum per Topic 409) |
| Remaining section 1231 gain | $18,079 | $56,895 | 15% (assumption) |
| **Illustrative federal tax** | **$14,658** | **$27,874** | |
| **Net proceeds after illustrative tax** | **$127,902** | **$114,686** | |

Scenario B's section 1250 figure is smaller because the study moved $68,816 out of the building and into faster property. That depreciation shows up in the section 1245 row and the remaining gain instead.

Check the logic in Scenario B. They took $59,991 more depreciation over five years than in Scenario A. At 32%, that's up to $19,197 of earlier tax savings, but only if the losses were usable when taken. At sale, the illustration shows $13,216 more tax. The study moved deductions forward. It didn't erase the tax. Net, Scenario B comes out up to about $5,981 ahead ($19,197 − $13,216), plus the use of that money for five years. That's why a study can still be worth it. But if the early losses weren't usable, B is simply $13,216 worse off at sale.

On the after-tax figures, holding earns 4.0% on Scenario A's $127,902 and 4.5% on Scenario B's $114,686. B's return looks higher only because B's after-tax cash is smaller. The property earns the same either way. The equity you'd actually walk away with is smaller than the equity on paper.

**Step 5. The 1031 path.** Suppose they sell on October 20, 2031, and want to exchange (dates are assumptions).

- Day 45, the written identification deadline: December 4, 2031.
- Day 180: April 17, 2032. Check whether their 2031 return is due before April 17, 2032. If it is, that earlier date is the real deadline unless they file an extension. Their CPA confirms which date applies.

If the exchange qualifies, the gain on the real property is deferred. The furniture sits outside the like-kind exchange, so the CPA handles its $6,000 allocation separately. Their 10 personal-use days a year go to the CPA to check against the section 4.02 personal-use cap. They need their calendar records for the two years before the sale, and a plan for the two years after, to show the CPA.

The exchange also carries next-property assumptions. The replacement property needs its own address-level permission check (resource SR.1) and underwriting (Sam's PA.4). It also needs financing (the DSCR lessons, PA.6 and PA.7) and a team (OP.7). All of that must be done within 180 days. That clock argues for having the replacement shortlist ready before the sale closes.

**Step 6. The decision.** Here is how the options line up for Maya and Chris.

| Option | Cash now (illustrative) | Future debt | Annual cash flow, base / weak | Work | Main risk |
|---|---:|---:|---|---|---|
| Hold | $0 | $272,519 | $5,172 / -$5,875 | Same | Weak seasons; capital repairs |
| Refinance | $53,853 | $334,741 | $2,085 / -$8,962 | Same | Bigger loan in a weak year |
| Sell | $127,902 (Scenario A) or $114,686 (Scenario B) | $0 | n/a | Ends | Tax due; redeploying cash |
| 1031 exchange | About $142,560 rolled into the next property (the $6,000 furniture allocation is handled outside the exchange; CPA) | New loan | Depends on replacement | Restarts | Deadlines; QI; qualification |

Notice that both Hold and Refinance lose money in a weak year. By the test in OP.12.3, holding only makes sense if they keep reserves for about $5,875 of weak-year losses. A refinance would need reserves for about $8,962.

Which is right depends on their buy-box goals from PA.1 and their answers in the worksheet below. The numbers make the trade-offs visible. They don't pick for you.

### OP.12.8 Closing the loop on the track

This is the last lesson in STR Concierge. Look at where the worksheet's inputs came from.

Every input comes from work you've already done. That includes your investment goals (your buy box) and your weak-season test. It also includes your depreciation choices with your CPA, your inspection and maintenance log, and your monthly and quarterly reviews.

An exit is only as good as the records behind it. Keep four things from day one. They are the closing documents, every depreciation schedule your CPA files, receipts for improvements and repairs, and a calendar of rental and personal-use days. With those, any of the four options is a short conversation. Without them, even a good sale becomes a reconstruction project.

If you sell or exchange, the course path starts over. A new property goes back to the buy box in PA.1, then market and deal review, financing, diligence, closing, launch, and the 30/90-day review. If you hold, the next decision point is your next annual review.

### OP.12.9 Who confirms what

**Confirm with:** a broker or appraiser the current market value, and whether furniture or future bookings add anything a buyer will pay for.

**Confirm with:** the loan servicer a written payoff quote and any prepayment penalty.

**Confirm with:** your CPA the adjusted basis, depreciation allowed or allowable by asset, and the allocation of sale price to section 1245 items. Also confirm the split of gain, the rates, state tax, any suspended passive losses, and any other federal taxes on the gain.

**Confirm with:** your CPA, QI, and attorney everything in Part C, the 1031 checklist.

**Confirm with:** the refinance lender, in writing, value, LTV, rate, costs, and reserves if you choose to refinance.

**Confirm with:** the licensing office or an STR attorney that any replacement property's exact address permits your planned use.
