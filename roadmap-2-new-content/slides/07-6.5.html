6.5 · When to Bring In a Local Manager§ 6.5.01 / 20
Slide 1 · Pick the lightest local help that covers emergencies and still pays in a bad year

This lesson answers one question: how much local help does a unit need, and what does that help cost you?

There are four setups. Cleaner only. Cleaner plus an on-call vendor. A co-host. Or a full manager. By the end you'll be able to pick one for a specific unit. You'll compare each one's cost against the net cash flow it leaves you, in both the base case and the stress case. The base case is a normal year. The stress case is a bad one: the nightly rate down 12% to $180 and only 60% of nights booked.

You'll also leave with a contract and vetting checklist. And you'll know which Airbnb permissions to give a co-host, and which controls you keep no matter who you hire.

We'll run Alder Street through all four setups with real math. Laid side by side, one of the four takes the whole margin. I'll show you which one, and why.

Slide 2 · Your cleaner covers the scheduled work; the gap is unscheduled work at odd hours

Remote operation works because most of the business is digital. Pricing, messaging, bookkeeping, and listing changes happen from anywhere. But some jobs need someone who can physically reach the unit.

Start with turnovers. A turnover is the clean between one guest's checkout and the next check-in. Alder Street books 255 nights a year at a 3-night average stay. That's 85 stays a year, or about 7.1 cleans a month. Your cleaner handles those, plus restocks if you pay them to.

Now look at the response column. The cleaner covers the scheduled work. What remote operators struggle with is the unscheduled work. The 11 p.m. lockout. The leak on a Sunday. The guest who can't get the heat on. Those need someone within an hour or two, at any time.

That's the gap local help fills. It's also exactly what you promised the landlord in the lease addendum in lesson 5.7: a named local contact who answers day and night. So this isn't optional polish. It's a commitment you've already signed.

6.5 · When to Bring In a Local Manager§ 6.5.23 / 20
Slide 3 · Each step up in local help buys back time and costs more of a thin margin

Here are the four levels, lightest to heaviest.

Cleaner only. You run everything remotely. Your cleaner turns the unit and reports problems, and when something breaks, you find and dispatch a vendor yourself. That's the Alder Street base case. It works if you live within reach, or if you're very good at remote dispatch and the unit is simple.

Cleaner plus an on-call vendor. You add a local handyman or small service company on a monthly retainer, a flat fee that keeps them on call. They commit to a response time, do a monthly inspection, and handle repair access. You still run guests, pricing, and the platform.

A co-host takes a defined share of the hosting: guest messages, in-person issues, cleaner coordination, sometimes pricing. On Airbnb they get permissions on your listing. You pay a percentage or a fixed amount per booking.

A full manager runs the whole operation. You approve the budget and review performance. Fees are usually a percentage of revenue.

Each step up saves you more time. Each step up also costs more. In arbitrage you lease the unit, so rent is already your biggest fixed cost. Every dollar of fee comes straight out of what's left.

Slide 4 · If test 4 is true, or any two of five are, you need at least an on-call vendor

Use five tests. If test 4 is true, or any two tests are true, you need at least an on-call vendor. Test 4 counts on its own, because it's a promise you've already made in writing.

Distance: can you reach the unit within two hours at night? That two-hour line is an assumption. Set your own.

Missed responses: in the last 90 days, did a guest wait longer than your promised response time for an in-person fix more than once?

Your hours: are you spending more time on the unit than you planned, to the point that it crowds out finding your next deal?

Written promises: does your addendum, the city permit, or your own house rules require a local contact you don't have right now? Some cities make that a permit condition. The SR.1 compliance checklist covers your city's rules.

Volume: are you adding units nearby? Help that's too expensive for one unit may pay off across three. That's a cost test, not a risk test.

After 90 days with a vendor, re-check tests 2 and 3. If either is still true, move up a level. Hiring won't change your distance.

Five controls stay with you no matter who you hire, because you carry the consequences

  • Bank and payouts. Revenue flows to your account. The helper gets paid from it.
  • Platform ownership. The listing stays in your account, with your reviews, calendar, and history.

Slide 5 · Five controls stay with you no matter who you hire, because you carry the consequences

Local help can take tasks off your plate. Certain controls stay with you, because you carry the consequences.

You keep bank and payout ownership. Revenue lands in your account, and the helper gets paid from it, not the other way around.

You keep the platform. The listing sits in your account. If the relationship ends, you remove the helper and keep your reviews, calendar, and history.

You keep compliance decisions. A manager can remind you a permit renewal is coming. You decide and you sign.

You keep pricing approval. A helper can recommend rates or run a pricing tool, but you set the floor, the ceiling, and minimum stays. Module 9 covers pricing tools, in lessons 9.10 and 9.13. There you'll see how to set that floor and ceiling inside a pricing tool, which is where this control lives.

And you keep performance review. Every month, look at occupancy, nightly rate, response times, cleaning scores, and complaints. The helper reports. You judge.

6.5 · When to Bring In a Local Manager§ 6.5.56 / 20
Slide 6 · Give a co-host the least Airbnb access the job needs, and set their payouts yourself

If you choose a co-host, set up Airbnb access deliberately.

Airbnb offers different permission levels. A co-host with full access can see messages, the calendar, and transaction history. They can manage the listing. And they can change other co-hosts' permissions. Lower levels limit access to the calendar and messaging. You can change a co-host's permissions at any time.

Give the least access the job needs. A co-host who only handles guest messages and in-person issues may not need full access. Full access includes the power to change other co-hosts' permissions, so treat it like handing over your keys.

Payouts are the second control. Airbnb says only the listing owner can set up co-host payouts. The owner picks a percentage per booking, with or without the cleaning fee, or a fixed amount. Percentage payouts are calculated from the host's earnings, which is your payout after Airbnb's fee. That basis matters, and you'll see why in the math. Regional limits may apply, so check the page for your country.

And responsibility doesn't transfer. Airbnb's terms say the host stays responsible for a co-host's actions taken before removal. If a co-host breaks a rule, the account at risk is yours.

Slide 7 · Pin down coverage, response times, and money terms before you sign anything

Whoever you hire, the agreement should answer twelve questions in writing. Here are the first six, and they're about coverage and money.

Service area: the exact addresses they cover. "We cover the metro area" with no named zone is a red flag.

Coverage hours: days and hours, including nights and holidays. Business hours with emergencies handled on a best-effort basis doesn't cover the gap you're hiring for.

Response targets: time to acknowledge and time on site, by type of issue. If there are no numbers, there's no promise.

Fees: the rate and the base it's calculated on. I'll show you in a minute how the same 10% can cost two different amounts depending on the base.

Markup: do they add a percentage to vendor bills or supplies? If they won't say, assume yes.

Authorization limits: the dollar amount they can spend without asking you. "As needed" means your money, their judgment.

Slide 8 · Backup, insurance, reporting, data, exit, and licensing complete the contract

The other six items protect you when things go wrong.

Backup: who covers when they're sick or away? One person with no backup is a single point of failure at 2 a.m.

Insurance: their general liability and workers' compensation, with certificates. If they can't produce a certificate, stop.

Reporting: what the monthly report contains, when it's due, and photo proof of turnovers.

Data and access: who holds the codes, the guest data, and the logins. Anyone who wants your platform password instead of a co-host invitation is a red flag.

Termination: the notice period and the handback of keys, codes, and records. Watch for long lock-ins or fees to leave.

Licensing: does local law require a license to manage short-term rentals for others? If they don't know, that tells you something.

Then vet. Ask for two references with similar units, their certificate of insurance, a sample monthly report, and the fee schedule in writing. And run one test: ask how they'd handle a lockout at 11 p.m. on a Saturday. A specific answer, with names and times, is a good sign.

Here's who confirms what.

The state real estate commission or licensing board confirms whether your helper needs a license to manage short-term rentals for others. Ask whether it matters that you're a tenant, because that's the arbitrage wrinkle.

Your insurance broker confirms whether your STR policy covers the acts of a co-host or vendor. Ask which certificates you should require from them, so your vetting asks for the right paper.

An attorney reviews the management or co-host agreement. I'd point them at three terms: termination, authorization limits, and data access. Those are the terms that hurt most when a relationship ends badly.

The local licensing office confirms whether your permit requires a named local contact and a response time. If it does, that's one of your threshold tests already answered.

And Airbnb's current help pages confirm the co-host permission levels and payout options in your region. Those can differ by region and can change, so check before you set anyone up.

Here are the sources for this lesson. I checked summaries of these official pages on 25 September 2026. Help pages change, so open the live page before you act.

Three Airbnb help pages cover co-hosting. Article 1534 explains what co-hosts can do: the permission levels, what full access includes, and that you can change permissions any time. Article 3264 is the Co-Host Additional Terms of Service. That's where Airbnb says the host alone is responsible for choosing co-hosts and managing access, and stays responsible for a co-host's actions before removal. Article 3389 explains co-host payouts: only the listing owner sets them up, the percentage or fixed options, the host-earnings basis, and regional limits.

The Tennessee Real Estate Commission page supports the licensing example: firm and designated agent licenses for Vacation Lodging Services.
