Pick the lightest help that survives a bad year

Slide 1 · Pick the lightest help that survives a bad year

How much local help does a unit need, and what does it cost you?

There are four setups: cleaner only, cleaner plus an on-call vendor, a co-host, or a full manager. You'll compare each one against the net cash flow it leaves you, in the base case and the stress case. The base case is a normal year. The stress case is a bad one. For Alder Street, the nightly rate drops 12% to $180 and only 60% of nights get booked.

We'll run all four setups through the numbers. Laid side by side, the full manager takes the whole margin.

Slide 2 · Your cleaner covers the schedule, not the emergencies

Most of this business is digital. Pricing, messaging, bookkeeping, and listing changes happen from anywhere. But some jobs need someone who can reach the unit.

Alder Street books 255 nights at a 3-night average stay. That's 85 turnovers a year, about 7 a month, and your cleaner handles them.

The cleaner covers the scheduled work. The trouble is the unscheduled work: the 11 p.m. lockout, the leak on a Sunday, the guest who can't get the heat on. Those need someone there within an hour or two, at any time.

That's the gap local help fills. It's also what you promised the landlord in the lease addendum in lesson 5.7: a named local contact who answers day and night.

Slide 3 · Each step up saves time and costs more margin

Here are the four levels, lightest to heaviest.

Cleaner only. You run everything remotely and dispatch a vendor yourself when something breaks. That's the Alder Street base case.

Cleaner plus an on-call vendor. A local handyman or small company on a monthly retainer. They commit to a response time, do a monthly inspection, and handle repair access.

A co-host takes a defined share of the hosting, like guest messages and in-person issues, and gets permissions on your listing. You pay a percentage or a fixed amount per booking.

A full manager runs the whole operation, usually for a percentage of revenue.

Each step up saves you time and costs more. In arbitrage, rent is already your biggest fixed cost, so every dollar of fee comes out of what's left.

Add at least a vendor if any two are true

  • Can't reach the unit in two hours at night
  • More than one missed in-person fix in 90 days
  • Hours on the unit over your plan
  • A lease or permit needs a local contact (alone is enough)
  • Adding units nearby
Slide 4 · Add at least a vendor if any two are true

Use five tests. If the written promise test is true, or any two tests are true, you need at least an on-call vendor. The promise counts on its own because you've already made it in writing.

Distance: can you reach the unit within two hours at night? That two-hour line is my assumption, so set your own. Missed responses: in the last 90 days, did a guest wait past your promised time for an in-person fix more than once? Your hours: is the unit crowding out finding your next deal? Written promises: does your addendum, the city permit, or your house rules require a local contact you don't have? Volume: help that's too expensive for one unit may pay off across three nearby.

After 90 days with a vendor, re-check missed responses and your hours. If either is still off, move up a level.

  • The listing stays in your account
  • Compliance decisions and signatures
  • Pricing floor, ceiling, and minimum stays

Slide 5 · Keep these five controls, whoever you hire

Local help can take tasks off your plate. These five controls stay with you, because you carry the consequences.

Revenue lands in your account, and the helper gets paid from it, not the other way around. The listing stays in your account, so if the relationship ends, you remove the helper and keep your reviews, calendar, and history.

Permits, tax registrations, occupancy limits, and lease duties are yours. A manager can remind you a renewal is due. You decide and you sign.

A helper can recommend rates, but you set the floor, the ceiling, and minimum stays. Lessons 9.10 and 9.13 show where that lives in a pricing tool.

And every month you review occupancy, nightly rate, response times, cleaning scores, and complaints. The helper reports. You judge.

Give a co-host the least access the job needs

  • Full access can change other co-hosts' permissions
  • You can change their access any time
  • Only you can set up their payouts
  • You answer for what they do before removal
Slide 6 · Give a co-host the least access the job needs

If you choose a co-host, set up Airbnb access on purpose.

Airbnb has different permission levels. Full access covers messages, the calendar, transaction history, and managing the listing. It also lets them change other co-hosts' permissions, so treat it like handing over your keys. A co-host who only handles guest messages and in-person issues may not need it. You can change permissions at any time.

Payouts are the second control. Only the listing owner can set up co-host payouts, as a percentage or a fixed amount per booking. Percentage payouts are figured on your host earnings, which is your payout after Airbnb's fee. That matters in the math.

And you stay responsible for what a co-host does before you remove them. Check Airbnb's current help pages for the options in your region.

Slide 7 · Get these terms in writing before you sign

Whoever you hire, the agreement should answer twelve questions in writing. The full checklist is in the written lesson. Here are five of them.

Coverage hours: if emergencies are handled on a best-effort basis, you haven't covered the gap you're hiring for. Response targets: time to acknowledge and time on site. No numbers means no promise. Fees: the rate and the base it's calculated on. You'll see why the base matters in a minute. Spending limit: the dollar amount they can spend without asking you. Platform access: anyone who wants your password instead of a co-host invitation is a red flag.

Before you sign, ask for two references, a certificate of insurance, and a sample monthly report. Then ask how they'd handle a lockout at 11 p.m. on a Saturday. A specific answer with names and times is a good sign.

  • Others use real estate or management licensing
  • Some states have no specific rule
  • Your tenancy may change the answer
Slide 8 · Some states license people who manage rentals for others

One contract item needs care: licensing. Some states regulate people who manage rentals for someone else.

Tennessee is one clear example, and I'm not assuming you're there. It licenses Vacation Lodging Services, businesses that manage, market, book, and rent units owned by others for stays of up to 14 days. Each needs a firm license and a designated agent license. Other states handle this under general real estate or property management licensing. Some have no specific rule.

Arbitrage adds a wrinkle. You lease the unit, so your helper works for a tenant, not the owner. Whether that changes the answer depends on the state. Ask your state real estate commission or licensing board, and an attorney if the answer is unclear.

Slide 9 · In a bad year, every setup but cleaner only loses money

Now the math. The Alder Street base case books 255 nights at a $205 average nightly rate (ADR). Gross booking revenue is $61,625. After Airbnb's fee, the cleaner, $23,400 of rent, and every other cost, net before income tax is $12,218 a year, or $1,018 a month. Lesson 1.3 shows every line.

The vendor, co-host, and manager rates are my assumptions, so swap in real quotes. The vendor is a $150 monthly retainer plus a $600 call-out budget, $2,400 a year. That leaves $818 a month.

The stress case is $180 and 60% of nights booked, a net of $1,548 a year. Cleaner only still makes $129 a month. Every other setup ends the year with a loss. The full manager loses $662 a month.

Slide 10 · A full manager needs more occupancy than Alder Street books

Break-even occupancy is the share of nights you must book to cover every cost, rent included.

At $205, cleaner only breaks even at 49.9%. The vendor pushes it to 53.8%. A co-host at 10% of gross needs 58.3%, or 56.8% on host earnings. A full manager at 20% needs 70.1%.

Alder Street books 255 of 365 nights, which is 69.9%. So with a full manager there's no cushion at all. That's why the net is slightly negative.

At the $180 stress rate it's worse. The full manager needs 81.0%.

Write the fee base into the contract.

Slide 11 · The same 10% fee can differ by $955 a year

A co-host at 10% of gross booking revenue costs $6,162 a year. Airbnb figures percentage co-host payouts on host earnings instead. That's gross minus Airbnb's host fee, which is $9,552 in this example, so host earnings are $52,073. Ten percent of that is $5,207.

Same 10%, and a $955 difference in yearly cost. A co-host quoting 10% of gross and one quoting 10% through Airbnb's payout tool are quoting different prices. So write the base into the contract, in words.

Above about 10% of gross, you keep under $500 a month.

Slide 12 · Above about 10% of gross, you keep under $500 monthly

Two numbers help you negotiate. A fee of 19.8% of gross wipes out Alder Street's base-case net entirely. A fee of about 10.1% leaves you $500 a month. So any percentage deal above roughly 10% of gross leaves you under $500 a month, and that's in a good year. The $500 is just an example floor. Use your own.

You can also compare cost with the time you get back. On the hours I assumed, the co-host is the cheapest per hour saved, about $37. But it pushes the stress case negative.

Far from the unit? Add an on-call vendor to your cleaner.

Slide 13 · A far-away operator should add an on-call vendor

For Alder Street, if you live within two hours and can answer at night yourself, setup 1 can work. If you live far away, I'd pick setup 2, the cleaner plus an on-call vendor. It covers the emergency gap you promised the landlord, and it keeps $818 a month in the base case.

In a bad year it turns a $129 monthly gain into a $71 monthly loss. That's $852 over the year. The $4,420 operating reserve from lesson 1.3 can carry that, and it uses about a fifth of it.

A co-host becomes reasonable only after lower rent at renewal, or comparable listings (comps) that support a higher rate. The full manager doesn't fit this unit at 20%.

Three things this week.

Score the five threshold tests for one unit and count the trues. That tells you whether you need help at all.

Ask two local handymen or small service companies for a written retainer quote with response times. Use the contract checklist to see what's missing.
