Slide 2 · Subletting and short stays are two separate questions

Students often treat "Can I sublet?" and "Can I run an Airbnb?" as the same question. They aren't, and mixing them up is a common way to end up in a dispute with your landlord.

An ordinary sublet usually means you hand the unit to one person for a stretch of time. They live there, they pay you, you pay the landlord, and it has a start and an end date.

Arbitrage is different. You keep control, and a new group arrives every few nights. Alder Street expects about 85 stays a year at 3 nights each. Over a 24-month lease, that's roughly 170 separate guest groups.

So every time you read a lease clause, a statute, or a landlord email, ask which question it answers. If it only answers the sublet one, you still need an explicit answer on short stays.

Slide 3 · In New York, a sublet right doesn't allow short stays

New York is just the illustration here. Alder Street's city is unnamed on purpose, because rules attach to the exact address.

Real Property Law section 226-b gives some tenants in buildings with four or more units a right to sublease. The tenant needs the landlord's advance written consent, and the landlord can't unreasonably withhold it. It says nothing about repeated stays under 30 days.

New York City answers that separately. For whole units, the answer is no.

So a tenant could have a real, statutory sublet right and still have no lawful path to short stays. One rule answers one question. The city answers the other.

Your plan might also be treated as a sublet, a license to occupy, or a hotel-type use, and each pulls in different rules. An attorney licensed in the property's state confirms which one applies.

Written authority needs all four of these at once

  • Signed by the owner or an authorized agent
  • Names short stays and the booking channels
  • Names the unit, the tenant, and your LLC
  • Attached to the lease as a signed addendum
Slide 4 · Written authority needs all four of these at once

Written authority has four parts. They aren't steps in order. You need all four at once, and if any one is missing, you don't have it yet.

First, the right person signs. That's the owner of record, the owner named in the county's property records, or an agent with written authority to bind the owner. A leasing agent who says "should be fine" may have no power to change the lease. If an agent signs, ask for the page that shows their authority.

Second, it names the use: short stays through listed booking channels. "Residential purposes" or "guests permitted" don't name the use.

Third, it names the exact unit, the exact tenant, and your LLC if you sign through one.

Fourth, it's a signed addendum, an add-on page that becomes part of the lease. That's far stronger than an email thread. Lesson 5.3 walks through a full addendum.

Slide 5 · Landlord consent can't override city or building rules

Landlord consent is necessary, but it has limits. It can't override a city ban, a primary-residence rule, or a homeowners' association restriction, what people call an HOA. The owner can say yes and the building or the city can still say no.

Airbnb's own guidance points the same way. It tells hosts to read the lease, talk to the landlord, and check HOA or co-op rules before hosting.

Los Angeles shows both sides. A renter needs written landlord approval, on a city form, before registering for home-sharing. But home-sharing is limited to a primary residence. So even a signed landlord form doesn't make an arbitrage plan legal there, because you don't live in the unit. In lesson 3.4 that's hard stop H2, which ends the deal on its own.

Consent is one layer. It doesn't replace the others.

5. Insurance10. Change in law and exit
Slide 6 · Written consent should cover ten topics

Think of the consent as the operating agreement between you and the owner. If a topic will matter the first time something goes wrong, it belongs in writing now.

Permitted use removes the "residential only" argument. Guest access and occupancy head off building disputes. Insurance lines the lease up with your actual policy.

Two items surprise people. Item 8 is permit and tax authority. Some cities want the owner's signature on the permit application. You don't want to find that out after you've signed a two-year lease and the owner has gone cold.

Item 10 is change in law and exit, and it protects you as much as the owner. If a new ordinance bans your use, you want a clean way to convert to a standard tenancy or end the lease. It also sets cure periods: the time you get to fix a problem after written notice.

Slide 7 · Tell the owner exactly what business you'll run

The owner should understand exactly what business will run in their property. That's fair, and it protects you. A consent given on a vague description can be attacked later as consent to something else.

Disclose five things. The operating model, with volume: for Alder Street, about 85 stays a year, roughly 1.6 check-ins a week. The local team: who cleans, who fixes, who shows up. Your guest screening. Airbnb bans open-invite parties, but your own rules still need writing down.

Then damage handling. Airbnb says Host damage protection, part of AirCover, isn't an insurance policy, so don't describe it to an owner as insurance. And neighbors: how they reach you and what quiet hours apply.

Put all five on one page and have the owner sign it with the addendum. If they later say "I didn't know it would be this many guests," you point to that page.

Slide 8 · Only a signed addendum gives you real permission

Now go back to the lease. This is where many arbitrage leases go wrong. The words sound permissive, but they don't say what you need.

The standard sublet clause says you need the landlord's prior written consent. People read that as "ask and you'll get it." It gives you nothing until the landlord signs something that names short stays.

"Tenant may have guests" usually means social guests, not paying ones. "Residential purposes only" can be read to exclude a lodging business. A text saying "Airbnb is fine with me" is unsigned, has no terms, and may come from someone without authority.

The medium row matters. A one-line consent is a big step up from silence. But the first time a neighbor complains about six guests, you'll be arguing about terms nobody wrote. A signed addendum covering all ten topics is lower risk, and even that stays subject to local law.

  • Stays under 30 nights, full term
  • No more than 5 overnight guests
  • Landlord signs owner forms within 10 days

Slide 9 · A sample addendum names the use and plans for change

The lesson includes a full ten-clause sample addendum, written for the Alder Street unit. Here are the parts I want you to understand first.

It names the use: furnished stays of fewer than 30 nights by paying guests, for the whole term and any renewal. It caps occupancy at 5 overnight guests.

It puts compliance on you, which is what an owner wants to see. You get and keep every permit and tax account. The landlord signs any owner form the city requires within 10 business days.

And it plans for a change in law. If short stays become prohibited, either side can give 30 days' notice to convert to a standard tenancy or end without penalty. A breach-and-cure clause gives you a set number of days to fix a problem.

This language is illustrative. An attorney licensed in the property's state reviews it before anyone signs.

Slide 10 · Alder Street has a restriction, a risk, and a friendly text

Back to Unit 4B, 218 Alder Street. The landlord owns the four-unit building and offered a 24-month lease at $1,950 a month. Two lease rows held it at investigate in 3.4: L1, no-cause termination, and L2, guest rights. Assume you have three documents.

Item A is lease clause 9. It restricts subletting and asks for written consent. It doesn't mention short stays at all. You need a signed addendum.

Item B is lease clause 14, the L1 flag. Either party can end the lease on 60 days' written notice. We'll put a number on that next.

Item C is a text: "I'm ok with the Airbnb thing as long as there are no problems." It's friendly and it's weak. It's unsigned, has no terms, and who decides what a problem is? Save it as evidence of good faith and use it to open the addendum talk. Don't rely on it.

Slide 11 · A notice at month 4 leaves $5,942 unrecovered

Your setup cost already spent is $12,050: furniture, linens and kitchen, safety gear, photos, and the permit application. You don't get it back if the lease ends early. At the base case, Alder Street nets $1,018 a month, so payback takes 11.8 months.

Now suppose the landlord gives 60 days' notice at the end of month 4. The lease ends after month 6. You'd have earned back about $6,108, which leaves about $5,942 unrecovered. Any notice before about month 10 leaves part of your setup on the table.

In the stress case, net falls to $129 a month. You'd recover only $774 by month 6, leaving $11,276 unrecovered.

That's why no-cause termination scores a 2 on the 3.4 scorecard. It turns your furniture budget into the landlord's option. So ask for breach and cure, and a plain statement that clause 14 doesn't apply while the addendum is in force.

Slide 12 · A signed addendum clears both lease flags

Build the addendum around the ten topics using Alder Street's facts: stays under 30 nights for the full term, Airbnb, Vrbo, and a direct site, a cap of 5 guests, the STR liability policy quoted at $1,300 a year, and the $250 yearly permit. Attach the one-page operating summary, and have the landlord sign both.

Now go back to the 3.4 scorecard, where each row scores 0 for fine, 1 for unclear, or 2 for serious. With a signed addendum covering these rows, guest rights drops from 1 to 0. If the landlord accepts breach and cure in place of no-cause termination, L1 drops from 2 to 0.

The landlord's text goes in the folder as good faith. It isn't what you rely on.

If the landlord won't sign, hard stop H5 applies: no explicit written permission. That's a reject, however friendly the texts are.

Run a four-part consent review before you sign

No written authority in hand means no guests.

  • A. Who owns it and who signs
  • B. What each clause answers, and its risk
  • C. Which of the ten topics are covered
  • D. What you disclosed, and when
Slide 13 · Run a four-part consent review before you sign

Your exercise is a consent review on the lease for one address you're considering.

Part A is authority, and it comes first on purpose. Look up the owner of record, write down who will sign and their role, and save the evidence with a file name and date.

Part B is the clause review. Quote each clause or message, mark whether it answers the sublet question, the short-stay question, or neither, and rate the risk. Part C checks the ten topics against the draft, then the signed copy. Part D records what you disclosed and when.

Then decide. If you don't have explicit written authority, you don't accept guests, and you go back to the 3.4 scorecard. Before you sign, an attorney licensed in the state confirms the addendum holds up, and the local permit office confirms a renter can hold the permit.
