### PA.6.0 Learning outcome

After this lesson you can read a DSCR loan quote the way the lender reads it. You'll know which income number the lender puts on top of the ratio, which payment it puts underneath, and why a ratio above 1.0 can still leave you losing money. You'll leave with a written question list for two lenders and a table for comparing their written offers on the same house.

### PA.6.1 What the title means, and what it leaves out

The tracker calls this lesson "How They Qualify the Property, Not You." That's shorthand. It points at the main difference from a regular mortgage: a DSCR lender sizes the loan mostly on the property's rental income, not on your pay stubs and tax returns.

But the lender still looks at you. Kiavi's own [complete guide to DSCR rental loans](https://www.kiavi.com/the-complete-guide-to-dscr-rental-property-loans) says the lender considers the borrower's credit score, the available down payment, and the property's coverage ratio. Expect a DSCR lender to check some mix of these:

- your credit score and credit history
- how much cash you're putting in (the loan-to-value, or LTV)
- your reserves after closing, if that lender requires them
- the property type, condition, and appraisal
- whether short-term rental use is legal at that address
- the entity you'll hold title in, and who signs a personal guarantee

So "qualifies the property" means the property's income replaces your W-2 income in the math. It doesn't mean nobody asks about you. Kiavi's terms are one lender's terms. Other lenders publish different minimums, and the same lender can change its terms. Treat every lender page in this lesson as an example, then get your own written quote.

### PA.6.2 The ratio itself: income on top, payment underneath

DSCR stands for debt service coverage ratio. In plain words: how many dollars of property income the lender counts for every dollar of loan payment.

The top number (the numerator) is some version of the property's income. The bottom number (the denominator) is some version of the payment. Every lender writes its own definition. Kiavi's material describes a common version: gross rent divided by PITIA, which is principal, interest, property taxes, insurance, and any HOA dues ([Kiavi DSCR guide](https://www.kiavi.com/the-complete-guide-to-dscr-rental-property-loans)). Kiavi's material also says lenders calculate it with different inputs. Some use gross rent, some use a vacancy-adjusted figure, and some include items like flood insurance in the payment while others leave them out.

That flexibility is the whole problem. The same house can produce very different ratios depending on three choices the lender makes:

1. Which income: gross booking revenue, a discounted version of it, market rent from the appraiser, or net operating income after expenses.
2. Whose income estimate: your pro forma, the appraiser's opinion, a third-party data report, or actual booking history.
3. Which payment: principal and interest only, or full PITIA.

Kiavi's guide says a ratio of at least 1.1 is needed to prequalify and describes 1.25 or higher as strong. Your lender's minimum may be different, and pricing often changes with the ratio. Ask for the definition in writing before you compare any two quotes.

### PA.6.3 Cedar Ridge: one house, three ratios

Maya and Chris are buying Cedar Ridge for $385,000 with 25% down. All figures below are assumptions from the canonical Cedar Ridge example (Example B). The hypothetical DSCR quote is 7.25%, 1 point, 30-year fixed.

Start with the payment underneath. The loan is $288,750. Monthly principal and interest is $1,970. Property tax ($3,850 a year) and STR insurance ($3,200 a year) add $587.50 a month. That makes monthly PITIA $2,557, or $30,684 a year.

Now put three different incomes on top.

**Ratio 1: gross booking revenue / PITIA.** Base-case gross revenue is $74,275 (212 booked nights at a $300 ADR, plus cleaning fees).

$74,275 / $30,684 = **2.42**

**Ratio 2: gross revenue discounted 20% / PITIA.** Some lenders haircut projected STR income. The 20% haircut here is an assumption, not any lender's published rule.

$74,275 x 0.80 = $59,420. Then $59,420 / $30,684 = **1.94**

**Ratio 3: NOI before property tax and insurance / PITIA.** Net operating income is revenue after operating costs. Base-case NOI is $29,209, and that figure already has tax and insurance taken out. Add them back so they aren't counted twice (once in costs, once in PITIA): $29,209 + $3,850 + $3,200 = $36,259.

$36,259 / $30,684 = **1.18**

Same house, same loan, same year. The ratio runs from 1.18 to 2.42. The lender's written definition decides which one counts.

Now run the weak case. The canonical weak case drops ADR to $265 and occupancy to 50%. Gross revenue falls to $57,330 and NOI to $18,162. I computed the three ratios in Python from those canonical inputs:

| Definition | Base case | Weak case |
|---|---:|---:|
| Gross revenue / PITIA | 2.42 | 1.87 |
| Gross revenue x 80% / PITIA | 1.94 | 1.49 |
| NOI before tax and insurance / PITIA | 1.18 | 0.82 |

All figures are assumptions from the Cedar Ridge example.

### PA.6.4 Why a lender ratio above 1.0 doesn't mean you make money

Look at the weak case on the gross definition: 1.87. That sounds comfortable. It says the house brings in almost twice its loan payment.

Here's what Maya and Chris actually keep in the weak case. Weak-case NOI is $18,162. Annual principal and interest on the DSCR loan is $23,637. NOI already covers tax and insurance, so cash flow is NOI minus principal and interest:

$18,162 - $23,637 = **-$5,475 a year**, about -$456 a month.

A lender ratio of 1.87 and an owner losing $456 a month. Both are true at once.

The reason is simple. A gross-revenue ratio ignores the platform fee, the cleaner, utilities, supplies, software, repairs, and the capital reserve. An STR carries far more operating cost than a long-term rental where the tenant pays utilities. On Cedar Ridge, base-case operating costs are $45,066 on $74,275 of revenue. The gross ratio never sees that $45,066.

Even the base case is thin. Base-case cash flow is $29,209 - $23,637 = $5,572 a year, about $464 a month, before any reserves the lender requires you to hold.

So use the lender's ratio for one question only: will this lender make this loan at this size? Use your own NOI and cash flow for the other question: should you buy this house? Sam's PA.4 lesson builds that NOI line by line. Bring his operating-cost total to every lender conversation.

### PA.6.5 How the definition changes the loan size

The definition does more than pass or fail you. It can shrink the loan.

Say a lender uses the NOI-based definition (Ratio 3) and requires a minimum of 1.20 (assumption). The most PITIA it will accept is $36,259 / 1.20 = $30,216 a year. Take out $7,050 of tax and insurance and the most principal and interest it allows is $23,166 a year. At 7.25% over 30 years, that supports a loan of about **$282,989**.

Maya and Chris asked for $288,750. This lender would cut the loan by about $5,761, and they'd bring that much more cash to closing.

Under the 80% gross definition with a 1.25 minimum (assumption), the same house supports a loan well above what they asked for. The binding limit there becomes the lender's maximum LTV, not the ratio.

I computed both loan sizes in Python from the canonical inputs. The lesson for you: ask each lender for the maximum loan its definition supports on your numbers, not just the rate.

### PA.6.6 How a lender verifies STR income with no history

Cedar Ridge has no booking history under Maya and Chris. Neither will most houses you buy to convert. So where does the lender's income number come from?

Lenders use a few sources, and each lender picks its own:

- The appraiser's market rent. Kiavi says it requires an appraisal on its rental loans, and its rental loan page says it uses the lower of 110% of appraised market rent or a valid lease rent as the qualifying rent ([Kiavi rental loans](https://www.kiavi.com/loans/rental)). That structure fits a long-term lease. Ask how the lender applies it to a house with nightly bookings and no lease.
- Short-term rental revenue. Kiavi publishes that it finances short-term rentals in some markets and that borrowers there can qualify on nightly rental revenue ([Kiavi Arizona DSCR programs](https://www.kiavi.com/arizona-dscr-loan-programs)). The page I could confirm through search doesn't say which data source or haircut it applies. Ask.
- Third-party revenue estimates. Some lenders accept a report from an STR data provider. Ask which provider, which comparable set, and whether they discount it.
- Actual history. On an existing STR, a lender may ask for 12 or 24 months of platform statements or tax returns. The seller's history may not transfer to you, and it may not reflect your pricing or costs.

The lender's number will rarely match your pro forma. It shouldn't. The lender is protecting a loan, so it will usually lean lower on income and ignore upside. When the lender's income is lower than yours, don't argue the loan up. Ask whether your pro forma is the one that's too high.

**Confirm with:** each lender, in writing, which income source it uses for a no-history STR purchase, any haircut, and whether the appraiser must be STR-experienced.

### PA.6.7 The other terms that decide the real cost

The rate gets all the attention. Six other terms decide what the loan actually costs and how much room you have later.

**Down payment and LTV.** Kiavi's guide says DSCR loans typically need a larger down payment than a primary-residence mortgage, often 20% to 25%, depending on property, lender, and borrower ([Kiavi DSCR guide](https://www.kiavi.com/the-complete-guide-to-dscr-rental-property-loans)). Maximum LTV can drop for lower credit scores, lower ratios, or STR use. Cedar Ridge assumes 25%, which is $96,250.

**Rate and points.** A point is an upfront fee equal to 1% of the loan, usually paid to lower the rate. The [CFPB explains points and lender credits](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/how-should-i-use-lender-credits-and-points-also-called-discount-points-en-136/): points cost more upfront and less over time; credits do the reverse. On Cedar Ridge, 1 point is $288,750 x 1% = $2,888. Closing costs rise from $11,550 (3% of price) to $14,438.

**Prepayment penalty.** Many DSCR loans charge a fee if you pay the loan off early, including through a refinance or sale. A common shape is a step-down. In a 3-2-1 structure (example, not a standard), the penalty is 3% of the balance in year one, 2% in year two, 1% in year three, and nothing after. Kiavi publishes 3-, 5-, and 7-year step-down options for its rental loans ([Kiavi rental loans for investors](https://www.kiavi.com/rental-loans-for-investors)). Other lenders use flat percentages, months of interest, or no penalty at a higher rate.

This matters for Cedar Ridge. The canonical example refinances at the end of year 2 (Example B7). The DSCR balance then is $282,951. Under a 3-2-1 schedule, a payoff in month 24 is still in year two:

$282,951 x 2% = **$5,659 penalty** (assumption: 3-2-1 schedule)

The canonical refinance shows $15,725 of cash out. Subtract the penalty and it drops to $10,066. Wait one more month, into year three, and the penalty falls to 1%. State law and loan type can limit prepayment penalties. Ask the lender how its penalty applies in your state.

**Reserves.** Some lenders require cash left in the bank after closing, usually measured in months of PITIA. Search results from Kiavi's site describe its DSCR process as having no cash reserve requirement ([Kiavi DSCR loans](https://www.kiavi.com/loans/dscr)). Other lenders do require reserves, so ask. Six months of PITIA on Cedar Ridge (assumption) is 6 x $2,557 = $15,342. Lesson 0.3 covered why that cash is on top of your cash to close.

**Entity and guarantor.** Many DSCR lenders let you close in an LLC. Kiavi's site says LLC borrowing is available for its DSCR rental loans, with requirements that vary ([Kiavi DSCR loans](https://www.kiavi.com/loans/dscr)). A lender may also ask the owners to sign a personal guarantee, which makes you personally liable if the LLC defaults. Ask who must guarantee, and whether title must be in the LLC before closing. PA.10 covers the entity decision itself.

**Permit and property eligibility.** A lender may refuse STR income on a property where short-term rental use isn't allowed, or may require proof of the permit. Some lenders exclude condos with rental restrictions, rural acreage, or unusual property types. Bring your SR.1 compliance checklist to the first call.

### PA.6.9 The questions you send to two lenders

Send the same written questions to at least two lenders, on the same house, on the same day if you can. Rates change daily, so quotes from different days can differ for reasons that have nothing to do with the lender.

1. What is your exact DSCR formula? Which income goes on top, and which payment goes underneath?
2. For a short-term rental with no history, which income source do you use? Do you discount it? By how much?
3. What minimum ratio do you require for this loan, and does the rate or maximum LTV change with the ratio?
4. What is the maximum loan you'll offer on this address with my numbers?
5. What credit score tier am I in, and how does it change the rate or LTV?
6. What are the rate, points, and all lender fees? Please send a written quote or Loan Estimate.
7. What prepayment penalty applies? Show the schedule by year.
8. How many months of reserves do you require after closing, and which accounts count?
9. Can I close in an LLC? Who must sign a personal guarantee?
10. What proof of legal STR use or a permit do you need, and when?
11. Does your appraiser need STR experience? Who orders the market-rent or revenue report?
12. How long is the rate lock, and what does an extension cost?

### PA.6.10 Comparing two written offers

Put the two written answers side by side. The illustration below shows the format. Lender A is the canonical DSCR quote. Lender B is invented for this exercise.

| Term | Lender A (canonical) | Lender B (assumption) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Rate | 7.25% | 7.50% |
| Points | 1.0 | 0.0 |
| DSCR definition | Gross / PITIA | NOI-based / PITIA |
| Minimum ratio | ask | 1.20 |
| Max loan on Cedar Ridge | $288,750 | about $276,096 |
| Prepayment | ask | 3-2-1 |
| Reserves | ask | 6 months PITIA |
| Guarantee required | ask | yes |

All Lender B figures are assumptions. Lender B's maximum loan uses the NOI method from PA.6.5: at most $23,166 a year of principal and interest, which at 7.50% over 30 years supports about $276,096 (computed in Python).

Read this table in order. First, does the lender make the loan you need at the size you need? Lender B's loan is about $12,654 short, so Maya and Chris would need that much more cash. Second, what's the full upfront cost, including points and fees? Third, what does it cost you to leave early? Rate comes after those. A lower rate with a 5-year prepayment penalty may cost more than a higher rate if you plan the year-2 refinance in OP.11.

The [CFPB's guide to comparing loan offers](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/compare/compare-loan-estimates/) tells borrowers to request several Loan Estimates and focus on the fees that vary by lender. One catch for DSCR loans: federal rules treat credit to buy a rental property you won't live in as business-purpose credit ([Regulation Z commentary to § 1026.3](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/interp-3/)). So a DSCR lender may give you a term sheet instead of a Loan Estimate. Ask for every fee in writing either way.

### PA.6.12 Who confirms what

**Confirm with:** each lender, the DSCR formula, income source for a no-history STR, minimum ratio, maximum loan, points, fees, reserves, and prepayment schedule, all in writing and dated.

**Confirm with:** the lender and your attorney, whether a prepayment penalty is enforceable in your state and how it applies to a refinance or sale.

**Confirm with:** your attorney, what a personal guarantee commits you to and whether title must sit in the LLC at closing.

**Confirm with:** the local licensing office, that STR use and any permit the lender asks for are available at this exact address (use your SR.1 checklist).

**Confirm with:** your CPA, how points and loan costs are treated for tax purposes on a rental.
