### PA.1.0 Learning outcome

By the end of this lesson you have a written, one-page buy box. It ranks your goals, states your constraints, and names your hard requirements. It also describes the property you want in terms someone else can search with. It ends with a pass rule: a sentence that tells you when to walk away from a listing without debate. ("Pass" here means decline, as in "we'll pass on that one.") Sam's PA.2 and PA.3 lessons use this page to choose a market and build your property pipeline.

**Terms this lesson uses.** The Cedar Ridge figures come from the course's running example, and all of them are assumptions.

| Term | What it means here |
|---|---|
| STR | Short-term rental. |
| Base case and weak case | The course's normal year and bad year for a house. For Cedar Ridge, the weak case is $265 a night at 50% occupancy, instead of $300 at 58%. |
| ADR | Average nightly rate: what guests pay per booked night, on average. |
| NOI (net operating income) | Booking revenue minus operating costs, before the loan payment. Cedar Ridge's costs include a 5% capital reserve, so NOI already sets that aside. |
| Debt service (P&I) | The yearly principal and interest on the loan. |
| PITIA | The full monthly payment: principal, interest, property taxes, insurance, and association (HOA) dues. |
| Point | An upfront lender fee equal to 1% of the loan. |
| DSCR loan | A loan the lender qualifies mainly on the property's income compared with its payment. PA.6 covers it. A conventional investment loan is qualified mainly on your household income and debts. |
| Cash to close; total cash in | Cash to close is the down payment, closing costs, and points. Total cash in adds furnishing. |
| Underwriting | Your spreadsheet estimate of what a property will earn and cost. Sam's PA.4 shows it. |

### PA.1.1 What a buy box is for

A buy box is a search specification. It says what you'll buy, where, at what price, and under what conditions. Anything outside the box, you don't look at twice.

It exists to protect you from two things.

The first is drift. You start looking for a three-bedroom house at $380,000. A month later you're touring a $450,000 house with a pool, because the photos were good and the agent said it "books great". Nobody decided to raise the budget by $70,000. It just happened, one listing at a time.

The second is wasted work. Every property you seriously consider costs hours: reading rules, pulling comparable listings (similar rentals nearby), underwriting, calling lenders. Sam's PA.3 lesson builds a pipeline of 5 to 10 qualified properties. Without a buy box, "qualified" means nothing, and the pipeline fills with houses you should never have looked at.

A good buy box is short and specific. It's written before you see listings. And it's hard to change: if you want to change it, you rewrite it on purpose, with numbers.

### PA.1.2 Start with a goal hierarchy

Before property details, rank your goals. People usually want several things from an STR purchase, and they conflict. A ranking tells you which one wins when they do.

The common goals:

- **Cash flow.** Money left after all costs and the loan payment. This lesson counts it by the year.
- **Long-term ownership.** Holding for many years and building equity through loan paydown and possible value change.
- **Personal use.** Using the house yourself some days each year.
- **Tax planning.** Depreciation and possible use of losses, which depend on facts your CPA confirms (lesson 0.2).
- **Portfolio growth.** Using this property as a step toward a second one.
- **Desired involvement.** How many hours you want to put in, and which jobs you want to hand off.
- **Safety.** Protecting your household's savings and income if the property underperforms.

Here's how they conflict in practice:

- Personal use pulls against cash flow. Your own peak-season week is a week you didn't rent.
- Tax planning can pull against everything else. A deal bought "for the depreciation" can lose money every month. The deduction may also be unusable, depending on your household facts.
- Low involvement pulls against cash flow. In 0.2, a full-service co-host taking 18% of booking revenue turned Cedar Ridge's $5,572 a year of cash flow into -$7,798.
- Portfolio growth pulls against safety. Stretching to buy now so you can buy again sooner leaves less reserve for this house.

Rank your goals from 1 to 7. Only the top two or three should shape the search. The rest are tie-breakers. If two goals always move together for you, you can give them one rank; say so in writing. If both owners rank differently, settle it now. A disagreement you skip now tends to come back at the offer.

### PA.1.3 Write down your constraints

Constraints are limits you can't wish away. They come mostly from lesson 0.3.

**Money.** Two caps. First, the maximum cash to close: the down payment, closing costs, and points you pay on closing day. Second, the maximum total cash in: cash to close plus furnishing. Both must leave your property reserve and personal emergency savings untouched.

To find the total cap, start from your liquid savings. Subtract your personal emergency savings and your property reserve. Then subtract the other startup costs in your 0.3 cash stack, the full list of cash a purchase needs. Those are repairs, inspections, permits, and launch months. What's left is the most you can put into the house.

Why two caps? Closing money is due on one day, and furnishing comes in the weeks after. The cash-to-close cap also stops a smaller furnishing budget from turning into a bigger house. If your 0.3 scorecard carried a funding condition, write it here too.

**Financing path.** Which loan types you'll pursue (DSCR, conventional investment, or both) and the down payment you're planning around. The lender makes the eligibility call later, in PA.6 and PA.7. For now, write the path and the assumed terms.

**Distance.** How far the house can be from you. This isn't only about convenience. Distance decides whether you can handle a problem yourself or must pay someone. Write it in driving hours.

**Time.** The hours per week you'll actually give it, from 0.3.

**Minimum reserves.** The property reserve and personal emergency savings you won't spend below. These are floors, not targets.

### PA.1.4 Describe the property

Now the property itself. Keep each line concrete enough that Sam's PA.2 and PA.3 filters can apply it.

**Market type.** Drive-to leisure, urban, event-driven, business travel. Name the type and the demand driver. Sam's PA.2 picks the actual market.

**Property type.** Detached single-family, townhouse, condo, small multi-unit. This matters for legality (condo and homeowners association, or HOA, rules). It matters for lending, because some lenders restrict condos or certain property types. And it matters for insurance.

**Location inside the market.** Rules can change street by street. Zoning districts, HOA boundaries, and permit caps can all differ within one town. Write where you'll look ("within 10 minutes of the lake") and where you won't.

**Price range.** A floor and a ceiling. The floor screens out houses too small or too worn to fit the rest of the box. The ceiling comes from your money constraints and your downside rule, not from what the market charges. Section PA.1.7 shows how to calculate it.

**Guest capacity.** Bedrooms, bathrooms, and how many guests it sleeps. More capacity can mean a higher nightly rate (ADR), and also more wear, more cleaning time, and more noise risk. Local rules may cap occupancy too.

**Amenities.** Separate what guests in this market expect (parking, reliable internet, a working kitchen) from what adds complexity (pools, hot tubs, docks, boats). Every amenity with water or height carries insurance and safety questions. Your insurer's quote will tell you which ones cost extra or aren't covered.

**Operating complexity.** Septic and wells, long private roads, snow access, older systems, big lots. None of these is a deal-breaker by itself. Each adds cost, inspection work, or response time. Decide how much of it you'll accept.

### PA.1.5 Hard requirements

Hard requirements are the lines a property must clear, every one, before you spend real time on it. They aren't preferences. If a property fails one, you pass on it: you walk away.

Five belong in almost every STR buy box:

1. **Legal STR use at the exact address.** Unhosted short-term rental use must be allowed at that address. Unhosted means you don't live there during guest stays. Any HOA or deed restrictions must allow it too. Airbnb itself tells hosts to check local laws and HOA rules before hosting ([Airbnb Help Center, article 1397](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/1397)). Use the SR.1 checklist and record the official city or county source.
2. **Acceptable insurance.** A written quote from an insurer willing to cover STR use of this house, at a premium your underwriting can carry. Platform protection is not a substitute; PA.12 covers that.
3. **Lender fit.** The property type, location, and projected income fit a lender's written guidelines for the loan you plan to use.
4. **Maximum cash to close and total cash in.** From PA.1.3. No exceptions for "a great house".
5. **Minimum downside performance.** A number for how badly the weak case may perform at your actual loan quote. Set two numbers. The first is a floor on weak-case annual cash flow after debt service (P&I). The second is a minimum base-case ratio of NOI to debt service.

Add a sixth if 0.3 surfaced it. For Maya and Chris, it's "the deal must work with self-management".

### PA.1.6 The pass rule

The pass rule is one or two sentences you agree on in advance. It tells you what to do when a listing doesn't fit.

A good pass rule has four parts:

- **The trigger.** Any failed hard requirement. Or a price above your ceiling that the seller won't bring down.
- **The action.** Pass. A listing priced above the ceiling can still get an offer at or under the ceiling. If the seller won't accept it, pass. Don't "keep an eye on it" unless the price drops to the ceiling.
- **The record.** Log every pass with the reason: which requirement failed.
- **The change process.** The buy box changes only on purpose: in writing, with the new numbers, signed by every owner. Never during a showing.

Why log the pass? Because after 20 passes you'll see a pattern. Maybe every house in your price range fails the insurance quote. That's information for Sam's PA.2 market choice, not a reason to lower your standards.

### PA.1.7 Worked example: Maya and Chris's buy box, tested on Cedar Ridge

Maya and Chris are the couple this course follows. They're two earners with $210,000 in liquid savings, buying a short-term rental. Cedar Ridge is a hypothetical three-bedroom lake house listed at $399,000. In PA.8 they agree to buy it at $385,000.

In real life, they write the box first and meet Cedar Ridge later, in Sam's PA.3. I'm showing both steps here so you can see the box at work. We'll write their box, then check whether Cedar Ridge belongs in it at both prices.

They finished 0.3 with "proceed to PA.1, with conditions". Figures without a tag come from the course's running example. New figures are marked (assumption).

**Their goal hierarchy**

| Rank | Goal | How it shapes the search |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Safety: protect the household | Emergency fund stays at $51,000; property reserve stays at 6 months of PITIA ($15,342) |
| 2 | Long-term ownership, 10+ year hold (assumption) | Favor durable houses and stable rules over a short-term price opportunity |
| 3 | Cash flow, with desired involvement: self-managing about 2.7 hours a week (assumption, from 0.3) | Base-case NOI minus debt service stays positive; NOI already includes the capital reserve |
| 4 | Personal use, about 10 days, off-peak | Drive time matters; personal days go in slow weeks |
| 5 | Tax planning | Tie-breaker only; no deal is bought for depreciation |
| 6 | Portfolio growth | Not before Sam's OP.10 performance review and the OP.11 decision on a second property |

Cash flow and desired involvement share rank 3. For them, self-managing with a local cleaner and an on-call handyman is how the cash flow stays positive. So they ranked the two together.

**Their constraints**

- Maximum total cash in, including $32,000 of furnishing: $145,000 (assumption). The math is below.
- Maximum cash to close: $115,000 (assumption).
- Funding condition from 0.3: no offer until liquid savings cover the full stack. Their plan is to save $3,500 a month (assumption, from 0.3) and be funded four months after starting PA.1.
- Financing: DSCR at the example's hypothetical quote (7.25%, 30-year fixed, 1 point, 25% down). On $385,000, that's a $288,750 loan. Debt service (P&I) is $1,970 a month, or $23,637 a year. They've asked for a conventional quote too, for comparison.
- Distance: 2.5 hours' drive or less from home (assumption: they live in the metro that Cedar Ridge's market serves).
- Reserves: 6 months of PITIA as property reserve (assumption), which is $15,342 on Cedar Ridge. Plus $51,000 personal emergency savings (assumption).

**Where the $145,000 cap comes from.** Start with savings and take out everything that must stay untouched. Today they have $210,000. Take away the $51,000 emergency fund and the $15,342 property reserve: $210,000 - $51,000 - $15,342 = $143,658. That's already under $145,000, before the other startup costs.

Their 0.3 stack has $12,614 of those: a $4,000 punch list, $1,500 of inspections, $600 of permits, and $6,514 for two launch months. Take those out too, and $131,044 is left. So today they can't fund the cap. That's the job of the funding condition.

Four months of saving at $3,500 brings them to $224,000. Then $224,000 - $51,000 - $15,342 - $12,614 = $145,044. They round down to $145,000.

**Why a separate cash-to-close cap?** It doesn't bind for them. $145,000 minus $32,000 of furnishing leaves $113,000 for closing, already under $115,000. It would only take over if the furnishing budget dropped below $30,000. That's its job: a cheaper furnishing plan shouldn't turn into a pricier house.

**Turning the money constraints into a price ceiling.** On the DSCR terms, cash to close is 25% down plus 3% closing costs (the course example's assumption). Then add 1 point. A point is 1% of the loan, and the loan is 75% of the price. So the point costs 1% x 75% = 0.75% of the price. Cash to close is 25% + 3% + 0.75% = 28.75% of the price.

- Cash-to-close cap: $115,000 / 0.2875 = $400,000.
- Total-cash-in cap: ($145,000 - $32,000) / 0.2875 = $113,000 / 0.2875 = $393,043.

The tighter cap wins. Their box ceiling is **$393,000 contract price** on these terms. That covers section C, lines 1 to 3. Line 4, the downside rule, comes later, house by house.

**The downside rule.** Maya and Chris set two numbers (assumption):

- Weak-case annual cash flow after debt service (P&I): no worse than -$6,000. That's about $500 a month they could cover from salary without touching reserves (assumption).
- Base-case NOI divided by annual debt service (P&I): at least 1.20. So NOI must be at least 20% above the loan payment, a cushion before cash flow turns negative.

These are their own rules, not a lender's ratio. PA.6 covers how lenders define coverage.

**The buy box itself**

| Field | Maya and Chris |
|---|---|
| Market type | Drive-to lake leisure market; Sam's PA.2 selects the market |
| Location | Within 2.5 hours of home; within a 10-minute drive of lake access (assumption) |
| Property type | Detached single-family; no HOA, or HOA with written STR permission; no condos |
| Size | 3 to 4 bedrooms, 2+ baths, sleeps 6 to 8 |
| Price range | $325,000 (assumption) to $393,000 contract price |
| Financing | DSCR 25% down; conventional quote for comparison |
| Amenities wanted | Parking for 3 cars, reliable internet, outdoor space, year-round road access |
| Complexity limits | No pool; hot tub only if the insurer quotes it; septic and well accepted with inspection and water test; punch list (small repairs before guests arrive) under $4,000 |
| Hard requirements | (1) Legal unhosted STR use at the address, and HOA if any; (2) written STR insurance quote at or under $3,500 a year (assumption); (3) lender fit in writing; (4) cash to close at or under $115,000 and total cash in at or under $145,000; (5) weak case no worse than -$6,000 a year after debt service, base NOI / debt service at least 1.20; (6) works with self-management and a named local cleaner plus backup |
| Pass rule | Pass on any listing that fails a hard requirement. A listing priced above $393,000 can get an offer at or under $393,000; if the seller won't accept it, pass. Log every pass with the reason. The box changes only in writing, signed by both. |

Their $325,000 floor is a judgment call. It screens out listings too small or too worn to meet their size rule and punch-list limit.

**Test 1: Cedar Ridge at the contract price, $385,000.**

| Check | Result | Clears it? |
|---|---|---|
| Cash to close | $110,688, under $115,000 | Yes |
| Total cash in | $142,688, under $145,000 | Yes |
| Weak-case cash flow | Weak NOI $18,162 - $23,637 debt service = -$5,475, better than -$6,000 | Yes |
| Base NOI / debt service | $29,209 / $23,637 = 1.24, above 1.20 | Yes |
| Property fit | 3 bed, 2 bath, sleeps 8, no HOA, drive-to lake market | Yes |
| Insurance | $3,200 a year in the course example, under the $3,500 cap; written quote needed | Pending |
| Legal STR use | Address-level check needed (PA.2, SR.1) | Pending |
| Lender fit | Written lender answer needed (PA.6) | Pending |
| Self-management | Named local cleaner plus backup still to line up | Pending |

At $385,000, Cedar Ridge clears every number in the box. Four evidence checks are still pending: insurance, legality, lender fit, and a named cleaner with a backup. "Pending" doesn't mean it clears. Those rows stay open until the evidence arrives.

**Test 2: Cedar Ridge at the asking price, $399,000.** Same revenue and operating costs as the course example. One change: property tax scales with price at 1% (assumption). That matches the example's $3,850 on $385,000. All figures below are assumptions.

| Check | Result | Clears it? |
|---|---|---|
| Cash to close | $114,713, under $115,000 | Yes, barely |
| Total cash in | $146,713, over $145,000 | **No** |
| Weak-case cash flow | -$6,475, worse than -$6,000 | **No** |
| Base NOI / debt service | 1.19, under 1.20 | **No** |

Base-case cash flow is still positive, $4,572. The base case still looks fine. That's why people talk themselves into houses like this.

At asking price, the same house fails three hard requirements. So the $14,000 negotiation from $399,000 to $385,000 is what makes Cedar Ridge a deal for Maya and Chris. If the seller had refused to go below $399,000, their pass rule says walk. PA.8 covers setting that walk-away price.

The downside rule also gives a price ceiling, but only for a specific house. It depends on that house's revenue, so it's section C, line 4. For Cedar Ridge, the weak case reaches -$6,000 at about $392,000 (assumption, same property-tax scaling). That's about $1,000 under the $393,000 box ceiling, and the lowest ceiling wins. So for this house, the limit is about $392,000. At $392,500, Cedar Ridge is under the box ceiling but fails hard requirement 5. Its weak case is about -$6,011.

**Test 3: a house to walk away from.** A four-bedroom house with a pool, listed at $435,000 in the same market (hypothetical). On their DSCR terms, cash to close would be $435,000 x 28.75% = $125,063. Total cash in would be $125,063 + $32,000 = $157,063. It fails the cash caps before anyone looks at revenue. It also has a pool, which is outside their complexity limit. An offer at $393,000 wouldn't fix the pool. Pass, logged as "over cash cap; pool". No underwriting needed.

That's the value of the box. The $435,000 house never takes an evening of their time.

### PA.1.9 Who confirms what

**Confirm with:** the city or county licensing office that unhosted STR use is allowed at each address that passes your other filters. Use the SR.1 checklist. If the answer is unclear, an STR attorney.

**Confirm with:** your insurance agent the premium and exclusions for STR use on each finalist. Do it before the diligence window, the period after your offer is accepted when you inspect and verify.

**Confirm with:** your lender that the property type, location, and planned use fit their loan, and the terms you've used in section C. Do it before you rely on your price ceiling.

**Confirm with:** your CPA before you put tax planning in your top three goals, so the ranking reflects your household's actual situation.
