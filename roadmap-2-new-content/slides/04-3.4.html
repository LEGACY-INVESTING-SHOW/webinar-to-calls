Find the fastest reason to say no

Slide 1 · Find the fastest reason to say no

The title says cities to avoid, but you won't get a list. A list of cities goes stale fast. You get a test you can run on any address instead.

Arbitrage, in this course, means renting an apartment from a landlord and re-renting it to short-stay guests.

The screen gives every address one of three labels: reject, investigate, or advance. Stage one is six hard stops. Any one of them ends the deal. Stage two is thirteen commercial and lease red flags, scored with fixed point rules so two people get the same answer. The first pass takes about an hour of desk work. Written confirmations and the lease review take longer.

We'll run Alder Street through it. Then we'll drop the same apartment into New York City and Los Angeles.

Slide 2 · Screen every address before you run revenue numbers

Most students do this in the wrong order. They find a nice apartment, run revenue numbers, pitch the landlord, and only then check whether the city allows the plan. By then they're attached, and the rules feel like an obstacle.

Flip it. Look for the fastest reason to say no. If you find one, move to the next address. If you can't find one, you've earned the right to spend real time on the deal.

Rules attach to the exact address, not the city. Lesson 3.1 walked through the layers: address, building, zoning district, permit category, and the signed lease. This screen turns those layers into two stages and one label.

Any one of six hard stops ends the deal

  • H1 Planned use is prohibited
  • H2 Primary-residence rule you can't meet
  • H3 Permit capped, unavailable, or not yours
  • H4 HOA, condo, or co-op ban
  • H5 No written permission in the lease
  • H6 No insurer will cover the use
Slide 3 · Any one of six hard stops ends the deal

A hard stop ends the deal by itself. No revenue number fixes it. Answer yes to any of these and the label is reject. If an answer is unknown, the best you can get is investigate.

H1 to H3 come from the city. Is unhosted use banned? Is there a primary-residence rule? Can you actually get the permit? A previous tenant's permit is not your permit.

H4 to H6 come from the building, the lease, and the insurer. A lease that says nothing about short stays is not permission, and neither is a friendly text from the landlord. If nobody will write a short-term rental (STR) liability policy, you carry guest-injury risk yourself.

Get the city answers in writing from the licensing or planning office, and have an attorney review the lease.

Eight commercial flags ask if the deal survives slow months

  • Thin break-even cushion or weak stress case
  • Weak comps or one demand source
  • High rent or slow setup payback
  • Thin cash reserve or new supply
Slide 4 · Eight commercial flags ask if the deal survives slow months

Once an address clears the hard stops, the question changes. Does the deal survive an ordinary slow stretch? Lesson 1.3 built the math. Here it becomes eight flags, C1 to C8.

C1 and C2 are cushion. How far is your base case above break-even, and does the stress case lose money? This course's stress case cuts the average nightly rate (ADR) 12% and occupancy by 10 percentage points.

C3 and C4 are where revenue comes from. Use comps, meaning similar active listings nearby, with a full year of data. And watch for one stadium or one employer driving your bookings.

C5 to C8 are rent you owe regardless, setup cost you've sunk, your cash reserve, and new listings arriving. Sam's 4.2 and 4.3 show how to check comps and supply.

Five lease flags decide whether you keep what you build

  • L1 Landlord can end it without cause
  • L2 Guest rights not spelled out
  • L3 Insurance terms don't match your policy
  • L4 A term you need is only verbal
  • L5 Lease ends before stress-case payback
Slide 5 · Five lease flags decide whether you keep what you build

The lease decides whether you keep what you build. These are the five terms I'd check first.

L1 is termination. If the landlord can end the lease without cause on short notice, your setup money is at risk every month. You want termination only for a defined breach, with written notice and a cure period, which is a chance to fix it.

L2 is guest rights: how many guests, how they get in, and which areas they use. L3 asks whether the lease's insurance terms match the policy you can actually buy. L4 is anything verbal. If you're counting on it, it goes on paper.

L5 ties back to the math. If stress-case payback runs past the lease term, you could hand back the keys before you earn your setup cost back. Lesson 3.5 covers what written permission must say.

Slide 6 · The total score sets the label, with two overrides

Every flag scores 0, 1, or 2. The full point bands are in the lesson text. They're fixed so you and a partner scoring the same address get the same answer. Nobody rounds up because they like the apartment.

Apply the labels in order. Any hard stop answered yes is reject. Ten points or more is reject. Any hard stop still unknown, any lease row at 2, or five to nine points is investigate. Otherwise it's advance.

One rule I want you to keep. If you haven't done the work, the row is a 1. Comps not pulled scores 1. Supply not checked scores 1. You score the numbers you have, not the ones you hope for.

The bands and cut-offs are my teaching defaults, not industry rules.

Slide 7 · Alder Street's stress case nets only $129 a month

Here's Alder Street, the course example. Unit 4B, two bedrooms, sleeps 5, a 24-month lease at $1,950. I'm assuming the city allows unhosted stays with a $250 permit, no HOA, a willing landlord, and a $1,300 insurance quote. So the hard stops pass, pending the licensing office's written answer. These figures come from lesson 1.3.

C1 looks great. Base occupancy is 70% and break-even at $205 is 49.9%. That's a 20.1-point cushion, which scores 0.

C2 is thin. The stress case drops the rate to $180 and occupancy to 60%. Net is $1,548 a year, or $129 a month. That scores 1.

And break-even at $180 is 57.1%. The stress case runs at 60%, so there's only 2.9 points of room.

Five commercial points, and most come from unfinished work. C3 is a 1 because nobody has pulled comps. C8 is a 1 because supply isn't checked. C4 is a 1 because hospital stays drive most bookings. C5 is a 1 because rent is 38% of gross.

Now the lease. I'm assuming the draft lets either side end it on 60 days' notice without cause. That puts L1 at 2. The addendum allows short stays but says nothing about occupancy or access, so L2 is a 1. L5 is a 1 because stress-case payback is 93.4 months against a 24-month lease.

Nine points with a 2 in L1. The label is investigate, which means a to-do list of what has to change.

The fixes drop Alder Street to 4, a thin advance

  • Termination only for breach, with a cure period
  • Addendum sets 5 guests, access, and common areas
  • Comps and supply screen come back clean
  • Licensing office confirms H1 to H3 in writing
Slide 9 · The fixes drop Alder Street to 4, a thin advance

Investigate means you write the to-do list. Here it mostly writes itself.

Ask the landlord to replace no-cause termination with termination for a defined breach, written notice, and a cure period. L1 goes to zero. Ask for addendum language naming a maximum of 5 guests, key-code access, and which common areas guests use. L2 goes to zero. Pull comps with Sam's 4.3 method and run the 4.2 supply screen. If both come back clean, C3 and C8 go to zero. And get the licensing office's written answer on H1 to H3.

If all five clear, the score is 4. That's advance, but only just. It earns lease negotiation and full underwriting. Lower rent or some free rent would help C2, C5, and L5 at once. Lesson 5.4 covers negotiating free rent.

Slide 10 · At $2,150 rent, the stress case loses $71 a month

Now test the other direction, because landlords push back. Suppose the landlord asks $2,150 instead of $1,950. That's the rent what-if from lesson 1.3.

The extra $200 a month is $2,400 a year. Take that off the stress net and you're at negative $852, or losing $71 a month. C2 goes from 1 to 2.

The base case moves too. Base net drops to $818 a month, so setup payback stretches to 14.7 months and C6 goes to 1. Break-even at $180 rises to 61.6%, above the 60% stress case.

On the draft lease, Alder Street goes from 9 to 11. That's reject. Even with all five fixes, it goes from 4 to 6, back to investigate. Know which rows a rent change touches before you agree to it.

Slide 11 · Same apartment, three cities, three answers

Now put the same unit in two real cities.

In New York City, stage one ends it. Whole-unit stays of about three nights with no host present fail H1. The city allows short stays only with the host present and up to two guests. You don't even score stage two.

In Los Angeles, you won't live in Unit 4B, so H2 fails. Home-sharing is limited to the host's primary residence. And if the building predates October 1, 1978, it's likely under the Rent Stabilization Ordinance, where home-sharing isn't allowed at all. That's H1 too.

Same apartment, same rent, three answers. The address decided each one. That's why there's no city blacklist. These rules are dated 25 September 2026, so recheck the official page. Sam shows that search live in 3.2.

Slide 12 · Score one real address in two passes

Your exercise is to score one address you're actually considering, in two passes.

Pass 1 happens before you contact the landlord. Answer H1 to H4 and H6, and score C1 to C8. H5 stays unknown because you don't have a lease yet. If nothing is a yes and the commercial rows total under 10, the address has earned a landlord conversation. That's the pitch in lesson 5.7.

Pass 2 starts when the landlord sends a draft lease and addendum. Answer H5, score L1 to L5, and give the address its final label.

For every stage-one answer, note the source, the date, and whether you saved a written answer. Resource SR.1 keeps those sources in one place. When a row scores 2, write the specific fix and who can make it.

Slide 13 · Run one address through both stages this week

Here's what I'd do this week. Each takes under an hour of your time, though replies take longer.

Pick one real address and answer H1 to H4 and H6. Every answer gets a source and a date. If you don't know, write unknown. Don't guess.

Send one email to the licensing office about unhosted stays at that address, and save the reply. A dated written answer beats an hour of forum posts.

When the landlord sends a draft lease, score L1 to L5. For any row at 2, write the fix and who can make it.

An address that lands on investigate usually has a lease problem. Lesson 3.5, Sublease Legality and Landlord Disclosure Best Practices, shows what written permission has to say. Bring your scorecard and the draft lease.
