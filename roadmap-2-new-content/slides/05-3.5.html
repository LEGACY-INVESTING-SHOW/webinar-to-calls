3.5 · Sublease Legality & Disclosure§ 3.5.12 / 18
Slide 2 · Subletting and short stays are separate questions that need separate answers

Students often treat "Can I sublet?" and "Can I run an Airbnb?" as the same question. They aren't, and mixing them up is how people end up in court.

An ordinary sublet usually means you hand the unit to one person for a stretch of time. They live there, they pay you, you pay the landlord, and it has a start and an end date.

Arbitrage is different in kind. You keep control. A new group arrives every few nights. Alder Street expects about 85 stays a year at an average of 3 nights. Over a 24-month lease, that's roughly 170 separate guest groups. A landlord who agreed to a sublet was probably picturing one subtenant, not 170 check-ins.

So here's the habit I want you to build. Every time you read a lease clause, a statute, or an email from the landlord, ask which of the two questions it answers. If it only answers the sublet question, you still need a separate, explicit answer on short stays.

3.5 · Sublease Legality & Disclosure§ 3.5.13 / 18
Slide 3 · A New York tenant can hold a sublet right and still have no lawful path to short stays

New York shows why the two questions split.

Real Property Law section 226-b gives some tenants in buildings with four or more units a right to sublease. The tenant needs the landlord's advance written consent, and the landlord can't unreasonably withhold it. The statute's notice process is built around a named subtenant and a sublease term. What it doesn't do is tell you whether repeated stays under 30 days are legal.

New York City answers that separately, through the Office of Special Enforcement. For whole units, the answer is no.

So a tenant could have a real, statutory sublet right and still have no lawful path to running short stays. The sublet right answers one question. The city rule answers the other.

This is also why the label matters. Depending on the lease, state law, and local code, your plan might be treated as a sublet, a license to occupy, a hotel-type use, or something else. Each label can pull in different rules. That classification is a question for an attorney licensed in the property's state, not for you or me.

Slide 4 · Written authority has four parts, and missing any one means you don't have it yet

Written authority has four parts. If any one is missing, you don't have it yet.

First, the right person signs. That's the owner of record or an agent with written authority to bind the owner. A leasing agent who says "should be fine" may have no power to change the lease at all. Check the owner's name against the county property record. If an agent signs, ask for the management agreement page or a letter that shows their authority.

Second, it names the use. The document should say short stays through listed booking channels are allowed. General words like "residential purposes" or "guests permitted" don't name the use.

Third, it names the parties and the unit. The exact unit, the exact tenant, and your LLC if you're signing through one.

Fourth, it's attached to the lease. A signed addendum that the lease references is far stronger than a separate email thread. Lesson 5.3 walks through a full addendum line by line.

Slide 5 · Landlord consent can't override a city ban, a residence rule, or an HOA restriction

Landlord consent is necessary, but it has limits. It can't override a city ban, a primary-residence rule, or an HOA restriction. The owner can say yes to you and the building or the city can still say no.

Airbnb's own guidance points the same way. It tells hosts to read the lease, talk to the landlord, and check HOA or co-op rules before hosting.

Some cities go further and build the landlord's written approval into the permit itself. Los Angeles requires written landlord approval before a renter can register for home-sharing, and the city publishes a landlord authorization form for exactly that purpose.

But here's the catch in LA. Home-sharing is limited to a primary residence. So even a signed landlord form doesn't make an arbitrage plan legal there, because you don't live in the unit. That's the H2 hard stop from lesson 3.4. Consent is one layer. It doesn't replace the others.

Slide 6 · Ten topics belong in writing before the first thing goes wrong

Think of the consent as the operating agreement between you and the owner. If a topic will matter the first time something goes wrong, it belongs in writing now.

Most of these are obvious once you see them. Permitted use removes the "residential only" argument. Booking channels tell the owner where the unit is advertised. Guest access and occupancy head off building disputes and noise worries. Insurance lines the lease up with your actual policy.

Two items surprise people. Item 8 is permit authority. Some cities want the owner's signature or consent on the permit application. You don't want to discover that after you've signed a two-year lease and the owner has gone cold.

Item 10 is change in law and exit. It protects you as much as the owner. If a new ordinance bans your use, you want a clean way to convert to a standard tenancy or end the lease. The alternative is a default.

We'll fill all ten for Alder Street in a few slides.

Slide 7 · Disclose five things in plain words so the consent covers what you'll actually run

The owner should understand exactly what business will run in their property. That's fair to them, and it protects you. A consent obtained with a vague description can be attacked later as consent to something else.

Disclose five things. The operating model, with the expected volume. For Alder Street that's about 85 stays a year, roughly 1.6 check-ins a week. The local team: who cleans, who fixes, who shows up. Your guest screening. Airbnb says it doesn't allow open-invite parties and may block higher-risk reservations, but that's a platform policy. Your own rules still need to be written down.

Then damage handling. Be accurate here. Airbnb states that Host damage protection isn't an insurance policy, so don't describe it to an owner as insurance. And neighbors: how they reach you and what quiet hours apply.

Put all five on one page and attach it to the addendum. If the owner later says "I didn't know it would be this many guests," you point to a page they signed. Then keep everything in one dated folder. That folder is what an attorney, insurer, or permit office will ask for.

Slide 8 · Most arbitrage clauses sound permissive but give you nothing until someone signs

This is where most arbitrage leases go wrong. The words sound permissive, but they don't say what you need.

The standard sublet clause says you need the landlord's prior written consent. People read that as "ask and you'll get it." It actually gives you nothing until the landlord signs something that names short stays.

"Tenant may have guests" usually means social guests, not paying ones. "Residential purposes only" can be read to exclude a lodging business. And a text saying "Airbnb is fine with me" is unsigned, unattached, has no terms, and may come from someone with no authority.

The row I want you to notice is the medium-risk one. A one-line consent to short-term rentals is a big step up from silence. But the first time a neighbor complains about six guests, you and the owner will be arguing about terms that were never written. Occupancy, channels, insurance, permits, exit: none of it is there.

The lower-risk row is a signed addendum covering all ten topics. Even that stays subject to local law.

Slide 9 · An explicit addendum puts compliance on you and plans for the day the rules change

The lesson includes a full ten-clause sample addendum. Here are the five clauses I want you to understand first.

Clause 1 names the use: furnished stays of fewer than 30 nights by paying guests, for the whole term and any renewal. Clause 3 sets occupancy and access for this unit: no more than 5 guests, keypad entry, the shared laundry, and one rear parking space.

Clause 4 matters most. It puts compliance on you. You get and keep every permit and tax account. The landlord agrees to sign any owner form the city requires within 10 business days.

Clause 8 plans for a change in law. If short stays become prohibited, either side can give 30 days' notice to convert to a standard tenancy or end the lease without penalty. Clause 9 is breach and cure.

Notice what this language doesn't do. It doesn't promise the use is legal. And whether clauses 8 and 9 are enforceable as written depends on state law. That's the attorney's job, and you should ask specifically how they interact with local tenant protections.

The New York sublet right comes from the state's Real Property Law, section 226-b, on the state senate's legislation site. The 30-day whole-unit rule and the lease certification come from New York City's Office of Special Enforcement.

The Los Angeles points come from City Planning: the FAQ for written landlord approval and the primary-residence rule, and the landlord authorization form itself. I'll be straight about that last one. It was confirmed from the official search listing. I haven't walked through the form's contents here, so open it yourself if you're working in LA.

The three Airbnb help articles support the lease and HOA check, the neighbor conversation, the party policy, and the point that Host damage protection isn't insurance.

The Alder Street figures come from the canonical example set. Rules change, so recheck each page before you rely on it for a real lease.
