Slide 3 · Order the diligence work by what can kill the deal fastest

Your accepted offer from PA.8 bought you time. That time is the real asset in this phase.

A contingency is a condition in the contract that lets you cancel if it isn't met. Most purchase contracts give you a window to inspect and a window to secure financing. Some add a separate appraisal contingency. The names and lengths come from your contract and your state's forms.

The CFPB puts it plainly. With a satisfactory-inspection contingency, you should be able to cancel without penalty if serious flaws turn up. That protection works only while the window is open, and only in the form your contract states.

So order the work by what can kill the deal fastest. Legal use and the insurance quote go first. Inspections come next, because specialists book up. Title and survey run alongside the inspections. The lender orders the appraisal, so put its due date on your calendar.

Open one folder for the property on day one. Every report, quote, email, and photo goes in with a date in the file name. You'll use that folder again at closing in PA.11 and in the handoff in PA.12.

Water and septic

Test the well and septic during the contingency, because after closing they're yours

  • Well: test during the contingency, at a certified lab (EPA, CDC). After closing, the test is on you.
  • Septic: pump the tank, inspect the baffles and the drain field (EPA).
  • Ask the county for the septic's permitted bedroom count. It can cap your guests (next slide).
PA.9 · Due Diligence§ PA.9.36 / 23
Slide 6 · Test the well and septic during the contingency, because after closing they're yours

Lake houses often run on a private well and a septic tank. Both are easy to skip and expensive to find out about later.

On the well, the EPA is direct. Its public drinking water rules don't apply to private wells. The owner does the testing. EPA recommends yearly tests for coliform bacteria, nitrates, dissolved solids, and pH. The CDC recommends certified labs. Do the test during the contingency, not after closing.

On septic, EPA notes many states require an inspection at transfer. For a typical household it suggests inspecting every 1 to 3 years and pumping every 3 to 5. A rental with eight guests is not a typical household.

So ask the septic inspector for three things. Pump the tank. Inspect the baffles, the walls that hold solids in the tank, and the drain field, the buried pipes that spread treated water into the soil. And tell you what bedroom count the system was permitted for. That last number comes back on the capacity slide.

Inspection versus appraisal

A clean appraisal says nothing about the septic, and a clean inspection says nothing about value

Home inspector

Works for you.
  • Looks for flaws
  • Check references and licensing yourself
  • Findings drive repairs and renegotiation

Appraiser

Independent. Estimates value for the lender.
  • Gives the lender a market value estimate
  • On a first-lien dwelling loan, you're entitled to a copy before closing
  • On a DSCR loan (sized on the property's rental income), ask if it includes a rent or income analysis
PA.9 · Due Diligence§ PA.9.58 / 23
Slide 8 · A clean appraisal says nothing about the septic, and a clean inspection says nothing about value

The CFPB draws this line clearly. A home inspector works for you and looks for flaws. An appraiser is an independent professional whose job is to give the lender an estimate of market value.

So you need both, and neither one covers the other. A clean appraisal doesn't mean the septic works. A clean inspection doesn't mean the house is worth your price.

On a first-lien dwelling loan, you're entitled to a copy of the appraisal before closing. That's in the CFPB's version of Regulation B, section 1002.14. When it arrives, read the comparable sales, the recent sales of similar houses nearby. Don't just look at the final number.

If you're on a DSCR loan, ask one more question. Does the appraisal include a rent or income analysis, and which figure will the lender actually use? You want that answer before the report lands.

PA.9 · Due Diligence§ PA.9.610 / 23
Slide 10 · Anything in the title commitment's exceptions stays with the land after you buy

The title company searches public records and issues a title commitment. Read it yourself, with your attorney or closing agent. You're the one who knows how you plan to use the land.

Two parts matter. The requirements list what has to happen before the title company will insure, like paying off the seller's mortgage. The exceptions list what the policy won't cover. Anything in the exceptions stays with the land after you buy it.

The CFPB gives unpaid contractors and unpaid taxes as examples of claims owner's title insurance can protect against. It also lists survey fees among the closing services you can often shop for. A lake lot with a dock and shared access is reason enough to order a survey.

On the policies, the CFPB says most lenders require the lender's policy. The owner's policy is optional, and it protects your equity, your ownership stake. With a $96,250 down payment at stake, I'd want it priced and decided on purpose. PA.11 shows where it lands on the closing figures.

Slide 11 · Every finding gets one of four decisions, written down with a reason that day

The decision column in your tracker has only four possible answers. They're alternatives, not steps. You pick one.

Accept means you'll live with it and put it in the budget. Seller fixes means the seller repairs it before closing and shows you proof, like an invoice. Credit or price means you negotiate money. Walk means you terminate inside the contingency window.

Write the decision the day you make it, and write why. One sentence is enough. Three weeks later, when the lender asks why the septic line changed, you'll have the answer in your own words.

Maya and Chris decided to proceed. They wrote down why: the PA.4 budget's reserve lines fund the roof and the routine septic and well costs. The permit is the only new cost. They also wrote a new rule. If the first 12 months run closer to the weak case, they revisit the co-host and pricing plan at the 12-month review in OP.11.

A buyer with less cash could read the same file and walk inside the 10-day window. So could a buyer who wants the whole capital reserve kept free for surprises. That would also be a sound decision.

What makes either answer defensible is timing. Both are written down, with a reason, before the deadline. The bad version is the one where nobody decides and the window just closes.

Slide 19 · Run five gates on your deal before your contingency windows close

These are the decision rules from the exercise. Run them in order, before your contingency windows close.

Gate one is legal use. If STR use at this address is prohibited or still unresolved, walk, or get the window extended in writing. Don't close on 'probably.'

Gate two is insurance. If no insurer will write a policy at a price your numbers can carry, walk.

Gate three is capacity. If the real number is lower than the underwriting assumed, stop and rerun revenue before you touch anything else.

Gate four is the revised base case. If it's negative, or it breaks a written rule from PA.1 or PA.8, renegotiate or walk. A budget change or a rule change only counts in writing, with the reason.

Gate five is your own cash test from PA.8. If the revised numbers break it, renegotiate or walk.

If everything passes, write one sentence with the decision, the date, and the reason. Cedar Ridge passed gate four on its final budget, and gate five with $7,265 unallocated.

Here's who confirms what. I'm not the last word on any of these, and neither are you.

Your attorney or agent owns the calendar. Contingency deadlines, extensions in writing, and how to terminate without losing your earnest money, the deposit you made with your offer.

The licensing or zoning office owns legal use. Permit transfer, occupancy caps, and how long a new application takes.

The county health department or the septic inspector owns the bedroom count the septic was designed for.

Your lender owns loan sizing after the appraisal, and whether the appraisal includes an income analysis.

The title company and your attorney own the liens, the exceptions, and whether you need a survey.
