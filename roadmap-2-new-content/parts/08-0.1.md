### 0.1.0 Learning outcome

By the end of this lesson you can explain, in plain terms, what changes when you own a short-term rental (STR) instead of leasing one. You can name who holds title, who carries the loan, who pays for a new roof, and who makes the final call on each decision. You can read STR Concierge, the ownership part of this course, as ten go/no-go decision gates. You know which gate can end a deal, and roughly how much money is at risk when you reach it. You also know which professional on your team answers which question.

### 0.1.1 Why this part of the course exists

Part One of this course taught arbitrage. You lease a unit, get written permission to host, furnish it, and run it. Your downside has edges. The lease has a term, the furniture has a price, and the rent is a known monthly number.

Owning removes those edges. You buy the building, the land under it, and every problem inside the walls. You sign a loan that runs for decades. Your cash goes in as a down payment and closing costs, and it does not come back out unless you refinance or sell.

That is the whole reason STR Concierge is a separate program. The skills from Part One still apply: pricing, cleaning, guest communication, local rules. What's new is the size of the commitment. So is the number of professionals you need to hear from before you commit.

This lesson is the map. It does not teach any single step in depth. Each later lesson does that. Here you'll see the order of the decisions, who helps with each one, and the points where walking away is the right answer.

### 0.1.2 Owner vs. arbitrage operator: what actually changes

Start with the legal and financial facts. The table below compares the two models on the items that change most.

| Item | Arbitrage operator (Part One) | Owner (Part Two) |
|---|---|---|
| Title | The landlord owns the property. You hold a lease. | You (or your entity) hold title. |
| Loan | None on the property. You may finance furniture. | A mortgage secured by the house. The lender may also want a personal guarantee, depending on the loan. |
| Up-front cash | Deposit, first month, furniture, setup, reserve | Down payment, closing costs, furniture, repairs, reserves |
| Monthly fixed cost | Rent set by the lease | Principal and interest, property tax, insurance, plus any HOA |
| Maintenance | Usually the landlord's for structure and systems, per the lease | Yours, all of it |
| Capital repairs (roof, HVAC, septic, water heater) | Usually the landlord's, per the lease | Yours, and they come in large amounts at irregular times |
| Local approvals | You need the city's permission and the landlord's written permission | You need the city's permission, and any HOA's. No landlord to ask. |
| Insurance | Your STR liability policy; the landlord insures the building | You insure the building and the liability, and the lender will require coverage |
| Who makes final decisions | The landlord can refuse, end, or not renew the lease | You decide, and you carry the result |
| Exit | Lease ends or you negotiate an early exit | Sell, refinance, change use, or hold. Each costs time and money. |
| What you build | Cash flow and an operating skill | Cash flow, loan paydown, possible change in value, and possible tax timing |

A personal guarantee means that if the property can't repay the loan, the lender can come after your household, not just the house. Your entity (the company that may hold title) is a PA.10 topic.

Read the "Maintenance" and "Capital repairs" rows twice. Maintenance is the routine upkeep. Capital repairs replace a big piece of the building, like the roof, the HVAC system, or the septic system. In arbitrage, a failed water heater is usually the landlord's problem. You lose some nights and call them. As an owner, it's your bill and your emergency. You pick the plumber, approve the cost, and pay it the same week.

The "Local approvals" row also changes shape. In arbitrage, a landlord can say no, and many do. As an owner, there's no landlord gate. But the city, the county, and any homeowners association still apply. Airbnb tells hosts to check HOA or co-op rules and local laws before hosting ([Airbnb Help Center, article 1397](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/1397)). Owning the deed does not give you the right to run a short-term rental at that address. Only the local rules do.

The last row is where 0.2 picks up. An owner can earn from four places at once: cash flow, loan paydown, a change in the home's value, and tax timing. Tax timing means depreciation can lower your tax now, and some of it can come back as tax when you sell. An operator earns mainly from one place, cash flow. Four possible streams sound better, and sometimes they are. But each one carries its own cost, and some of them never show up in your bank account until you sell. Lesson 0.2 tests that claim with numbers.

### 0.1.3 The course path, stage by stage

The STR Concierge course runs in one direction: from "what should I buy" to "is it performing the way I underwrote it". Underwriting is your spreadsheet estimate of what the property will earn and cost. Here is the path, with the lessons that carry each stage.

Lesson codes work like this. The 0.x lessons are this readiness module. PA lessons cover buying the property, and OP lessons cover operating it. I'm Preston, and my lessons teach the decisions. Sam runs the live demonstrations.

| Stage | What you decide | Lessons (in course order) |
|---|---|---|
| Readiness | Should I own at all, and can I afford the full cash stack (every dollar I need on day one, plus reserves)? | 0.1, 0.2, 0.3 (Preston) |
| Buy box | What exactly am I shopping for, and when do I pass? | PA.1 (Preston) |
| Market and deal review | Which market, which properties, and do the numbers work for this one? | PA.2, PA.3, PA.4, PA.5 (Sam) |
| Financing and offer | Which loan, and what is my walk-away price? | PA.6, PA.7, PA.8 (Preston) |
| Diligence | Does the house and the STR business survive inspection? | PA.9 (Preston) |
| Structure | Who holds title, who operates, how is it taxed? | PA.10 (Preston) |
| Closing | Verify documents, move money safely, take possession | PA.11 (Preston) |
| Handoff to operations | Insurance, utilities, safety, launch packet | PA.12 (Preston) |

The order matters because each stage feeds the next one a document. PA.1 produces a buy box. Sam's PA.2 and PA.3 use that buy box to pick a market and build a list of properties. PA.4 produces an underwriting package. PA.8 uses that package to set an offer ceiling. PA.9 checks the package against what the inspector and the city actually say. If you skip a stage, the next one has nothing reliable to work from.

### 0.1.4 Your working team, and what each person answers

Owning brings more people into the deal. Each has a narrow job. Trouble starts when you ask one of them to answer a question that belongs to someone else. A real estate agent is not your tax advisor. A lender is not your insurer.

| Role | What they answer | What they do not answer |
|---|---|---|
| Buyer's agent | Comparable sales (recent sales of similar homes), offer terms, contingency timing, local market practice | Whether STR use is legal at the address; your tax result |
| Lender | Whether you qualify, the rate and points (upfront loan fees), required reserves, what income they will count | Whether the deal is a good investment for you |
| Home inspector | The physical condition of the house and its systems | Market value (that is the appraiser's job) |
| Appraiser | Market value for the lender | Condition problems an inspection would find |
| Insurance agent | Whether they will write an STR policy on this house, what it costs, what it excludes | Whether platform protection replaces a policy (don't assume it does; PA.12 covers this) |
| Title / escrow | Who owns the property now, liens, easements, how money moves at closing | Zoning or STR permission |
| CPA | How depreciation (a paper write-off for wear on the building), losses, personal use, and sale tax apply to your household | Whether the lender will approve you |
| Real estate attorney (as needed) | Contract terms, HOA documents, entity and title questions, local STR law questions | Market rents |
| Local operators (cleaner, handyman, co-host if used) | Turnovers, access, repairs, guest issues on the ground | Pricing strategy, compliance decisions, your finances |
| STR Concierge team | The course process and worksheets | Legal, tax, lending, or insurance advice. Ask the team what support your membership includes. |

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau makes the inspector versus appraiser point directly. The appraiser estimates market value for the lender. The buyer is responsible for hiring the inspector ([CFPB on home inspections](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/close/schedule-a-home-inspection/)). Title coverage splits the same way. Lender's title insurance protects the lender. An owner's policy is what protects your equity ([CFPB, owner's title insurance](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-owners-title-insurance-en-164/); [CFPB, lender's title insurance](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-lenders-title-insurance-en-163/)).

One more role deserves a line. If you add a co-host on Airbnb later, you can set their permission level. You as the listing owner remain responsible for your co-hosts, including ones added by a full-access co-host ([Airbnb Help Center, article 1534](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/1534)). You can hand off tasks. You can't hand off accountability. OP.7 covers this in detail.

You make the final decision on every gate. Each professional gives you a fact or an opinion inside their lane. You combine them.

### 0.1.5 The decision gates that can end a deal

A deal moves forward only when it clears a gate. At each gate, one answer ends it. Ending a deal early is cheap. Ending it late costs real money.

Here are the ten gates, in order. Before closing, a "No" at Gates 1 to 6 sends you back to the property list, not forward. Gates 0, 7, 8, and 9 have their own fixes, shown in the last column.

| Gate | Question | Lessons | If No |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 Readiness | Can we fund the full cash stack and keep our own emergency savings? | 0.3 | Wait, build up cash you can reach, or use the lease path (Part One) |
| 1 Buy box fit | Does this property meet every hard requirement (a must-have) in the buy box? | PA.1 | Pass |
| 2 Address-level STR permission | Is unhosted short-term rental use (you don't live there during the stay) allowed at this exact address, including any HOA rules? | PA.2; SR.1, the regulatory compliance checklist | Pass, or get a written answer before spending more |
| 3 Underwriting | Does the weak case stay inside the downside rule you set in PA.1? Use a current rate and terms from a lender (a written quote comes at Gate 4). | PA.4 | Pass, or plan a lower offer (PA.8) |
| 4 Financing | Do we have a written quote we qualify for, with reserves we can meet? | PA.6, PA.7 | Pass or restructure |
| 5 Offer ceiling | Will the seller accept a price at or below our walk-away number? | PA.8 | Walk |
| 6 Diligence | Do the inspection, insurance quote, title, appraisal, and permit all hold up against the numbers and the plan? | PA.9 | Renegotiate or cancel inside the contingency window |
| 7 Closing | Do the Closing Disclosure (the lender's final statement of costs) and the wire instructions match what we agreed? | PA.11 | Stop and verify before any money moves |
| 8 Launch readiness | Insured, safe, permitted, furnished, team in place? | PA.12, OP.6 to OP.8 | Do not publish the listing yet |
| 9 30/90-day review (after you own) | Are actual results inside the range we underwrote? | OP.10 (Sam) | Fix pricing, costs, or operations; revisit the plan |

The weak case is a bad-year version of your numbers: lower nightly rates and fewer booked nights. Your downside rule is the most you'll accept losing in that bad year.

Gates 0 to 3 cost only time. Gate 2 (permission) and Gate 3 (the numbers) are where most bad deals should die. The city's rules are public. Sam's PA.4 underwriting uses your own spreadsheet. A buyer who treats these gates as formalities tends to find the problem at Gate 6. By then the inspections are paid for and earnest money (the deposit that shows the seller you're serious) is on the line.

Gate 6 has a clock. Inspection and financing contingencies are conditions in your purchase contract that let you cancel. They run on deadlines. If you miss a deadline, your options can shrink, depending on the contract. PA.8 and PA.9 cover how to set those windows.

Gate 9 is not a formality either. It is the first time you check the underwriting against actual results. OP.10 is Sam's walkthrough of that review. OP.11 uses it to decide whether a second property is on the table at all.

### 0.1.6 Worked example: Cedar Ridge against Alder Street

The two recurring examples in this course make the difference concrete. Alder Street is the Part One arbitrage unit. Cedar Ridge is the house Maya and Chris are considering. All figures are assumptions from the course's worked examples.

**The properties.** Alder Street is a leased two-bedroom apartment. The rent is $1,950 a month on a 24-month lease. Cedar Ridge is a three-bedroom, two-bath house in a drive-to lake market about two hours from a metro area. Maya and Chris are modeling a $385,000 price against a $399,000 asking price. That's the price the seller later accepts in PA.8.

Their loan quote in this example is a DSCR loan. DSCR stands for debt service coverage ratio, and debt service means the loan payments. PA.6 explains how this loan type works.

**Cash on day one.**

| | Alder Street (lease) | Cedar Ridge (own, DSCR quote) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Sunk setup / furnishing | $12,050 | $32,000 |
| Deposit, first month, operating reserve | $8,320 | (not in this line) |
| Down payment | none | $96,250 |
| Closing costs incl. 1 point | none | $14,438 |
| **Cash in before owner reserves** | **$20,370** | **$142,688** |

Step by step for Cedar Ridge. The down payment is 25% of $385,000, which is $96,250. The loan is the rest: $385,000 - $96,250 = $288,750. Closing costs are 3% of price ($11,550) plus 1 point. A point is a fee equal to 1% of the loan, so 1 point is $2,888. Closing costs total $11,550 + $2,888 = $14,438. Cash to close is $96,250 + $14,438 = $110,688. Add the $32,000 furnishing budget and the total is $142,688.

That is 7 times the Alder Street figure ($142,688 / $20,370 = 7.0). And the Cedar Ridge number still has no lender reserves or personal emergency savings in it. Lesson 0.3 adds those.

**Monthly fixed obligation.** Alder Street's rent is $1,950. Cedar Ridge's principal, interest, taxes, insurance, and association dues (PITIA) on the DSCR quote is $2,557. Cedar Ridge has no HOA, so the dues are zero. The owner's number also comes with repairs and capital replacements the tenant usually doesn't carry. The course example sets aside 5% of gross booking revenue for repairs and another 5% for capital items. In the base case, gross booking revenue is $74,275 a year. So each set-aside is $3,714, and together they're $7,428 a year.

**Bad-year exposure.** Each property has its own bad-year scenario in the course example. Alder Street's "stress case" has a nightly rate of $180 (down 12%) and 60% occupancy. Cedar Ridge's "weak case" has $265 a night and 50% occupancy. In its stress case, Alder Street still nets $1,548 for the year. In its weak case, Cedar Ridge loses $5,475 for the year after debt service on the DSCR quote. That loss already includes the repair and capital set-asides, at the lower weak-case revenue. It is also the pre-diligence figure, and PA.9 revisits it. The money comes from reserves or from the household. Lesson 1.3 builds the Alder Street stress case, and 0.2 comes back to the Cedar Ridge weak case.

**Way out.** At Alder Street, the worst case is usually the remaining lease obligation plus the furniture. Twenty-four months of rent is $46,800, and the real exposure depends on the lease's early-termination terms. At Cedar Ridge, the way out is a sale. The course's five-year sale example assumes the value grows 3% a year, to $446,321. Selling costs at 7% of that value are $31,242. That is before any income tax on the sale.

These two numbers aren't directly comparable. The lease figure is a ceiling paid over time, while the unit can still earn. The sale figure assumes the house has gone up in value. If it has gone down, the loss is larger.

**What Cedar Ridge offers that Alder Street doesn't.** In year one on the DSCR quote, the loan balance falls by $2,795. That is equity, though you can't spend it this year. The house may change in value. And depreciation may change when you owe tax (tax timing), depending on facts only a CPA can confirm. Lesson 0.2 separates those four streams and tests them.

Neither model wins on this comparison alone. The commitment is seven times larger in cash, and the bad-year loss lands on you instead of being capped by a lease. Know that before you shop.

### 0.1.7 Money at risk at each gate

One more view of Cedar Ridge helps you see why early gates matter. The table shows what Maya and Chris would have spent, put at risk, or committed by the time each gate is decided. Figures marked (assumption) are new illustrative numbers for this lesson.

| Gate | Money spent, at risk, or committed at this gate |
|---|---|
| 0 to 3: readiness, buy box, permission, underwriting | Time, and possibly a data subscription. No deposit yet. |
| 4: financing | Possibly an application or credit report fee, per the lender |
| 5: offer accepted | Earnest money of $7,700, 2% of price (assumption, the figure Maya and Chris agree in PA.8). Whether it is refundable depends on the contract and contingencies. |
| 6: diligence | The $7,700 earnest money at risk, plus inspection fees of about $1,500 (assumption) for general, septic, and other specialist checks |
| 7: closing | If you stop here to verify, the inspection fees are spent and the earnest money is still governed by the contract. If you close, $110,688 of cash to close is committed on the DSCR quote. The earnest money counts toward it (PA.8). It's also the amount a wire-fraud scam would target (PA.11). |
| 8: launch | Plus $32,000 furnishing ($142,688 in total), plus carrying costs until the first booking |
| 9: 30/90-day review | You own the house by now. A "No" here means fixing pricing, costs, or operations, not losing a deal. |

Gates 0 to 3 cost only time. Gate 4 may cost a lender fee. From Gate 5 on, the cost is thousands, then tens of thousands. So the work you do in PA.1 and in Sam's PA.2 to PA.4 is the cheapest protection you will buy in this program.

### 0.1.9 Who confirms what

**Confirm with:** the city or county licensing office, before an offer, that STR use is allowed at the exact address and under any HOA rules. Use an STR attorney if the answer is unclear.

**Confirm with:** your lender, in writing and before you rely on their quote, what reserves, credit, down payment, and guarantees their loan requires.

**Confirm with:** your insurance agent that they will write an STR policy for the specific house, and what it excludes, before your diligence window closes.

**Confirm with:** your real estate agent or attorney, before you sign, how the earnest money, inspection, and financing contingencies in your contract work. Include every deadline.

**Confirm with:** your CPA how ownership, depreciation, and personal use affect your household's taxes before you count any tax benefit in a decision.
