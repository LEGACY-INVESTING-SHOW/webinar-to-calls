Property two starts with proof from property one

Slide 1 · Property two starts with proof from property one

This lesson is about buying a second property. We start with the books on the first one, before you open a single listing.

If property one earned about what you forecast, a second similar house repeats a plan you've already tested. If it didn't, a second house doubles a plan that hasn't worked yet, and it adds a second loan.

So there are two jobs. Proof: what property one actually earned. And liquidity: where the cash for property two comes from, whether that's savings, a refinance, or a line of credit on your home. Borrowed money needs a wider margin than savings.

We'll use Cedar Ridge at the end of year 2, and you'll leave with a dated yes, not yet, or no.

Check four gaps between your forecast and the actual year

  • Revenue miss: rate or occupancy?
  • Which costs grew?
  • Was the capital reserve funded?
  • Debt service: principal and interest only
Slide 2 · Check four gaps between your forecast and the actual year

Start with Sam's OP.10 review and stretch it to a full year. That's your trailing 12 months. Build it from records, not memory: payouts and fees from each platform's earnings reports, expenses from the property's own bank account and card, nights and rate from your property management software.

Set it up in three columns: the underwriting you bought on, the actual year, and the variance. Then look for four things. Split any revenue miss into rate and occupancy, because they point to different fixes. Find the costs that grew. Check that the capital reserve actually left the operating account. And keep debt service as principal and interest only, with tax and insurance in operating costs.

The number that matters most is net operating income, or NOI. If it's well under your forecast, property two waits until you know why.

Slide 4 · A cash-out refinance nets them $15,725

A cash-out refinance replaces your loan with a bigger one and hands you the difference, minus costs. It can feel like free money, but it's a new loan with a new payment and a new 30-year clock.

For Cedar Ridge, I assumed the value grew 3% a year to $408,446. At 75% loan-to-value, the new loan is $306,334. Paying off the $282,951 balance removes $23,383 of equity. Costs of $7,658 come out, and they keep $15,725.

Those costs eat 32.8% of the equity they pulled out. That can happen when the equity is small. Every input here comes from a written lender quote in real life: the appraisal, the maximum loan-to-value for a short-term rental, and the rate.

The new loan is 7.0% for 30 years, a bit under the original 7.25%. So the payment only rises $820 a year, to $24,457. That sounds mild, but the loan is bigger and the payoff schedule starts over.

A HELOC puts your own home on the line

  • Your home is the collateral
  • The rate is usually variable
  • A value drop can freeze the line
  • Undrawn credit is not a reserve
Slide 6 · A HELOC puts your own home on the line

A home equity line of credit, or HELOC, is a revolving line secured by a home you already own. For most people, that's the house they live in.

The CFPB describes a draw period, when you can borrow up to your limit, and then repayment. Depending on the plan, you may repay over time or owe the whole balance at once. The rate is usually variable, so price it at today's rate and at a higher stress rate.

Two risks. If you can't repay, you could lose your home. And the lender can generally freeze or cut the line if your home's value drops significantly or a big change in your finances makes the lender doubt you can pay. So don't count an undrawn line as your emergency reserve. Your CPA also needs to tell you how the interest is treated.

Slide 7 · Borrowing the down payment turns property two negative

Maya and Chris's home is worth $520,000 with a $260,000 mortgage. If a lender allows 80% combined loan-to-value, the line could go up to $156,000. Say they draw $100,000 toward a twin of Cedar Ridge, using Cedar Ridge's original, pre-diligence base and weak cases.

I priced the interest at 8% and 10%, both assumptions. That's $8,000 or $10,000 a year. I charge it to property two, because property two is why they borrowed.

A thin base case goes negative. The weak case becomes a five-figure gap every year, with their home securing part of the money. And if the home's value drops 10%, the room left on the line falls from $56,000 to $14,400, and a significant drop is itself a reason the lender can freeze the line.

Next is the cash reserve test.

After buying and launching Cedar Ridge, Maya and Chris had $80,471.50 left. I hold that flat to keep it simple. In your worksheet, use today's balance.

A twin costs $142,688 all in. With $100,000 from the HELOC, savings cover $42,688. Then they need six months of PITIA on both houses. Adding the refinance cash still leaves them short, and it raises Cedar Ridge's payment.

Slide 9 · Name a primary and a backup for every role

Once the numbers work, the operating question starts. Can your team run two houses as well as it runs one?

A second house in the same market can share your cleaner, your handyman, and what you know about permits and seasons. But one bad season, ordinance, or storm hits both. A new market spreads that risk, and the learning curve starts over with a new market check and a new local team.

Either way, go through the OP.7 roles and write who covers each one at property two and who backs them up. If one cleaner has two Saturday turnovers, you likely need a second cleaner before you buy.

Keep one bookkeeping file tagged by property, one calendar, and one reserve policy.

Slide 12 · Write your go tests before you look at listings

Now it's your turn. The worksheet has three parts. Part A is your trailing 12 against your underwriting, with a source, a date, and a one-line cause on every row. Part B puts savings, a refinance, and a HELOC side by side, with the net cash, the payment at today's rate and a stress rate, and what's at risk as collateral.

Part C is your go tests. Write the thresholds before you look at listings. If you write them after you've fallen for a house, it's easy to let them bend.

Then fill in your results and circle one answer: proceed to a property-two buy box in PA.1, not yet with a recheck date, or no and hold one property. Date it and have each owner sign it.
