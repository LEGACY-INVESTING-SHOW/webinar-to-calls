Slide 1 · Owning trades a capped lease for a bigger, open-ended bet, so you move through gates

Welcome to Part Two. This is STR Concierge, the ownership side of the course.

In Part One you learned to lease a unit and run it. Here you buy the house. That sounds like the same business with a bigger check. It isn't quite. The check is bigger, the loan runs for decades, and a lot more people have to sign off before you commit.

So this first lesson is a map. I'm not teaching any single step in depth today. Each later lesson does that. By the end of this one, you should be able to say who holds title, who carries the loan, who pays for a new roof, and who makes the final call. You'll see the course as a series of gates. And you'll know which gates can end a deal, and roughly how much money is on the line when you reach each one.

I'll use Cedar Ridge, the lake house Maya and Chris are looking at. I'll hold it next to Alder Street, the Part One apartment, so you can see the difference in real numbers.

Why Part Two exists

A lease has edges. Owning removes them: you buy the building, the land under it, and every problem inside the walls.

Pricing, cleaning, guest messages, and local rules still apply. What's new is the size of the commitment and the number of people who must sign off.
0.1 · Welcome to STR Concierge§ 0.1.12 / 18
Slide 2 · A lease has edges; owning removes them

Think about what capped your downside at Alder Street. The lease had a term. The furniture had a price. The rent was a known monthly number. If things went badly, you knew roughly where the bottom was.

Owning takes those edges away. You buy the building and the land under it. You sign a loan that runs for decades. Your cash goes in as a down payment and closing costs, and it doesn't come back out unless you refinance or sell.

That's the whole reason STR Concierge is its own program. Your Part One skills still work here. Pricing, cleaning, guest communication, and local rules all carry over. What changes is how much you commit, and how many professionals you need before you commit it.

Keep that frame for the rest of Module 0. Every table I show you is really asking one question: how big is the commitment, and who has to say yes first.

Slide 3 · As an owner you hold title, carry the loan, and pay for the roof

Here's the side-by-side. I want you to read two rows twice: maintenance and capital repairs.

At Alder Street, if the water heater fails, it's usually the landlord's problem. You lose a few nights, you call them, and they fix it. Check your lease, but that's the normal split.

At Cedar Ridge, it's your bill and your emergency. You pick the plumber. You approve the cost. You pay it that same week. Roofs, HVAC, septic, and water heaters don't break on a schedule. They come in big amounts at odd times.

The loan row matters too. A mortgage is secured by the house. Depending on the loan, the lender may also want a personal guarantee. That means your household can be on the hook, and not just the property.

And look at up-front cash. The operator's list is deposit, first month, and furniture. The owner's list starts with a down payment and closing costs, and it still needs furniture and reserves on top.

Slide 4 · Owning the deed does not give you the right to host; only local rules do

The approvals row changes shape when you own. In arbitrage, a landlord can say no, and many do. As an owner, there's no landlord gate. That feels like freedom. But the city, the county, and any homeowners association still apply.

Airbnb's own help center tells hosts to check HOA or co-op rules and local laws before hosting. Owning the deed doesn't give you the right to run a short-term rental at that address. Only the local rules do. We'll turn that into a hard gate in a minute.

Now the last row. An owner can earn from four places at once: cash flow, loan paydown, a change in value, and tax timing. An operator earns mainly from one. Four streams sound better, and sometimes they are. But each one carries its own cost. Some of them never reach your bank account until you sell.

That's exactly what lesson 0.2 tests with numbers. For now, just notice that "what you build" is a longer list with longer strings attached.

Slide 6 · Each stage hands the next one a document, so a skipped stage leaves a gap

Here's why the order matters. Each stage produces a document, and the next stage uses it.

PA.1 produces your buy box. Sam's PA.2 and PA.3 use that buy box to pick a market and build a list of properties. PA.4 produces an underwriting package for one property. PA.8 uses that package to set your offer ceiling. Then PA.9 checks the package against what the inspector and the city actually say.

So picture someone who skips the buy box and goes straight to touring houses. What does Sam's pipeline filter on? Nothing. What does the underwriting test against? Their mood that day.

Or picture someone who skips underwriting and makes an offer. There's no ceiling. The offer price is whatever feels right in the moment, and the diligence check has no numbers to compare against.

That's what I mean by a gap. Each document is the input to the next decision. When you're tempted to jump ahead, ask which document you're missing.

Slide 7 · Each professional answers inside one lane, and you combine the answers

Owning brings more people into the deal. Each one has a narrow job. Trouble starts when you ask someone a question that belongs to someone else.

Your agent knows comparable sales and offer terms. Your agent does not know whether short-term rental use is legal at that address, and shouldn't be guessing about your taxes.

Your lender tells you whether you qualify and what income they'll count. The lender does not tell you whether this is a good investment. A lender approval means they expect to be repaid. It says nothing about whether you'll be happy.

The inspector and the appraiser get mixed up all the time. The inspector looks at condition. The appraiser estimates value for the lender. Neither does the other's job.

And the insurance agent tells you whether they'll write a short-term rental policy on this specific house. Platform protection doesn't replace that policy. We'll come back to that in PA.12.

You make the final call on every gate. These people each hand you one fact.

Slide 8 · Title, tax, and local help each answer a narrow question, never your whole deal

Here's the rest of the team.

Title and escrow tell you who owns the property now, what liens and easements sit on it, and how money moves at closing. They don't tell you whether the city allows short-term rentals there. A clean title says nothing about zoning.

Your CPA handles depreciation, losses, personal-use days, and tax on a future sale, for your household. Your CPA can't tell you whether a lender will approve you.

Bring in a real estate attorney as needed. Contract terms, HOA documents, entity and title questions, and local short-term rental law are their lane.

Your local cleaner, handyman, and maybe a co-host run the ground game. Turnovers, access, repairs, guest problems. They don't set pricing strategy or make compliance calls.

And the STR Concierge team helps with the course process and the worksheets. We don't give legal, tax, lending, or insurance advice. Ask the team what support your membership includes.

Where buyers get caught

You can hand off tasks to others, but the accountability stays with you

  • The appraiser estimates market value for the lender. You, the buyer, hire the inspector (CFPB).
  • A lender's title policy protects the lender. An owner's policy protects your equity (CFPB).
  • On Airbnb, you set each co-host's permissions. You stay responsible for co-hosts, even ones a full-access co-host adds (article 1534).
  • Each professional gives you a fact or an opinion inside their lane. You make the final decision at every gate.
0.1 · Welcome to STR Concierge§ 0.1.49 / 18
Slide 9 · You can hand off tasks to others, but the accountability stays with you

Three places where responsibility splits in a way new owners miss.

First, inspection. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau is direct about this. The appraiser works for the lender's question, which is value. You, the buyer, are responsible for hiring the inspector. If you skip it, nobody else is checking the roof on your behalf.

Second, title insurance. The lender will require a lender's policy. That one protects the lender. If you want your own equity protected, that's an owner's policy. Ask about it at closing. PA.11 covers this.

Third, co-hosts. If you add a co-host on Airbnb later, you choose their permission level. But Airbnb says the listing owner remains responsible for co-hosts. That includes ones added by a full-access co-host. You can hand off tasks. You can't hand off accountability. OP.7 goes deeper on this.

The pattern across all three is the same. Other people do parts of the work. You carry the result.

Slide 10 · Gates 0 to 4 cost almost nothing to fail, so most deals should die here

A deal moves forward only when it clears a gate. At each gate, one answer ends it. I've numbered them 0 to 9 in the lesson, and this slide shows the first five.

Gate 0 is readiness, from lesson 0.3. Gate 1 is your buy box. Gate 2 is permission at the exact address. Gate 3 is underwriting. Gate 4 is a written loan quote.

Gates 2 and 3 are where most deals should die. They cost almost nothing to run. The city's rules are public. Sam's PA.4 underwriting runs in your own spreadsheet.

Here's the trap. A buyer who treats these gates as formalities tends to find the problem later, at Gate 6. By then the inspections are paid for and earnest money is on the line.

So when a house fails Gate 2, don't argue with it. Pass, or get a written answer before you spend another dollar. The rejection is the gate doing its job.

Slide 11 · Gates 5 to 9 put real money at stake, and Gate 6 runs on a contract clock

Here are the second five gates. Notice the money changes here.

Gate 5 is the offer. If the seller won't come to your walk-away number, you walk. PA.8 shows you how to set that number.

Gate 6 is diligence: inspection, insurance quote, title, appraisal, and permit. I phrased it so a No means a finding broke the numbers or the plan. Then you renegotiate or cancel. And this gate has a clock. Your inspection and financing contingencies run on deadlines in the purchase contract. Miss one, and your options can shrink, depending on the contract. PA.8 and PA.9 cover how to set those windows.

Gate 7 is closing. You check the Closing Disclosure and the wire instructions against what you agreed. If anything is off, stop before money moves.

Gate 8 is launch. Don't publish the listing until it's insured, safe, permitted, furnished, and staffed.

Gate 9 is the first time your underwriting meets reality. Sam walks through that review in OP.10.

Slide 12 · Cedar Ridge needs $142,688 on day one, 7 times Alder Street's $20,370

Let's make it concrete. Maya and Chris have a $385,000 contract price on Cedar Ridge, against a $399,000 asking price. All of these figures are assumptions from the course example file.

Step by step. The down payment is 25% of $385,000, which is $96,250. Closing costs are 3% of price, $11,550. The DSCR quote carries one point on a $288,750 loan. That's another $2,888. So closing costs total $14,438, and cash to close is $110,688.

Then furnishing. The budget is $32,000. That brings cash in to $142,688.

Now hold that next to Alder Street. The apartment needed $20,370 on day one. That was $12,050 of sunk setup, plus $8,320 tied up in the deposit, first month, and an operating reserve.

$142,688 divided by $20,370 is 7.0. Seven times the cash. And the Cedar Ridge number still doesn't include lender reserves or the family's own emergency fund. Lesson 0.3 adds both, and the total gets bigger.

Slide 13 · In a bad year Alder Street still nets $1,548, while Cedar Ridge loses $5,475

Here's the rest of the comparison.

Monthly fixed cost first. Alder Street's rent is $1,950. Cedar Ridge's principal, interest, taxes, insurance, and association dues on the DSCR quote is $2,557. There's no HOA, so the dues are zero. On top of that, the owner sets aside 5% of gross for repairs and 5% for capital items. At base-case revenue, that's $3,714 each, $7,428 a year together. A tenant usually doesn't carry those.

Now the bad year. In Alder Street's stress case, the unit still nets $1,548. In Cedar Ridge's weak case, the owners lose $5,475 after debt service. That money comes out of reserves or out of the household.

The way out is different too. At Alder Street, the worst case is usually the rest of the lease plus the furniture. Twenty-four months of rent is $46,800, and the real exposure depends on the early-termination terms. At Cedar Ridge, the way out is a sale. The five-year model shows $31,242 of selling costs, before any tax.

What Cedar Ridge offers back is $2,795 of loan paydown in year one, possible value change, and possible tax timing. Lesson 0.2 tests all of it.

Slide 15 · Log every serious property gate by gate, and leave a gate blank until it's in writing

This is your working tool for the whole acquisition phase. Use one log for every property you seriously consider. Start with the first one Sam's PA.3 pipeline produces.

Fill in one row per gate as you reach it. The evidence column is the important one. It's a document, a link, or an email. "The agent said it's fine" is not evidence. "City code section and the date I read it" is.

Leave a gate blank until you have a written answer. An open gate is honest. A gate marked pass on a hunch is how people end up at Gate 6 with a problem they could have found for free at Gate 2.

When a property fails, write down what ended it. After a few logs, you'll see patterns. Maybe every lake house in your range fails insurance. Maybe every one with an HOA fails permission. That tells you something about your buy box and your market.

The log also makes the calls with your agent and lender faster. You're asking specific questions with the evidence in front of you.

Slide 16 · Five professionals confirm the facts, and each one confirms before a set point

Here's who confirms what, and when.

The city or county licensing office confirms that short-term rental use is allowed at the exact address, and under any HOA rules. Do that before an offer. If the answer is unclear, get an STR attorney.

Your lender confirms reserves, credit, down payment, and any guarantee, in writing. A verbal "you should be fine" doesn't count.

Your insurance agent confirms they'll write a short-term rental policy on this specific house, and what it excludes. Get that before your diligence window closes. If you can't insure it, you can't run it.

Your agent or attorney explains how earnest money and the inspection and financing contingencies work in your contract. That includes every deadline. Put those dates on a calendar the day you sign.

And your CPA confirms how ownership, depreciation, and personal use affect your household's taxes. Do that before you count any tax benefit in a decision. Until then, the tax benefit is zero in your math.
