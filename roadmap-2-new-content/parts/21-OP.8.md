### OP.8.0 Learning outcome

After this lesson you'll have a short operating playbook for your house that a backup cleaner or a new co-host can follow without calling you. It covers a turnover checklist with photo proof, linen and consumable par levels, a missed-clean escalation, and a message sequence with a named owner for every message. It also covers an emergency matrix for seven kinds of incident. You'll see all of it run at Cedar Ridge: a normal turnover, a guest problem, and a real emergency.

### OP.8.1 What makes an SOP usable

A standard operating procedure (SOP) is a written routine that produces the same result no matter who follows it. Most STR checklists fail that test. They say "clean kitchen" and leave the standard to the cleaner's mood.

Every SOP in this lesson has the same seven parts:

1. Trigger: what starts it (a checkout, a message, an alarm).
2. Owner: one named role, taken from the OP.7 role map.
3. Steps, in order.
4. Standard: what "done" looks like, specifically.
5. Proof: a photo, a timestamp, or a reading.
6. Escalation: who to call, by when, if a step fails.
7. Record: where the result gets written down.

Keep each SOP to one page. If it runs longer, split it. The backup cleaner reads it in the driveway, not at a desk.

### OP.8.2 Turnover SOP with photo proof

The turnover is the SOP that runs most often. At Cedar Ridge's base case, it runs 61 times a year. Airbnb's [ground rules for hosts](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/2895) set cleanliness as an expectation and name the basics. Hosts should clean between every stay: laundry, trash, vacuuming or sweeping, and wiping surfaces. Your SOP turns that into a standard.

Trigger: guest checkout in the calendar. Owner: turnover cleaner (calendar access only, per OP.7). Window: checkout 10 a.m., check-in 4 p.m. (assumption), so six hours.

| Step | Standard | Photo proof |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Arrive and log start | Start time recorded in the task app | Front door on arrival |
| 2. Walk-through before touching anything | Note damage, missing items, smoke smell, extra guests' signs, anything left behind | Any damage, before cleaning |
| 3. Strip all beds, start laundry | All 6 beds stripped whether used or not | None |
| 4. Kitchen | Dishes clean and put away, appliances wiped inside and out, fridge emptied of guest food, trash and recycling out | Counters, open fridge |
| 5. Bathrooms | Toilets, showers, sinks, mirrors, floors; hair check on every surface | Each bathroom, wide |
| 6. Bedrooms | Beds made in the standard set from the shelf, surfaces dusted, under beds checked | Each bed |
| 7. Living, dining, work nook | Surfaces, cushions, remotes, floors vacuumed or mopped | Each room, wide |
| 8. Deck and grill | Furniture set, grill grates clean, cover on, no guest trash | Deck, grill |
| 9. Restock | Consumables to par (OP.8.3) | Supply shelf |
| 10. Safety check | Alarms present and not disabled; extinguishers in place; no indoor device added; owner closet locked | Each alarm location, weekly test logged |
| 11. Final walk and lock | Lights off, thermostat to vacancy setting, doors locked | Front door on exit |
| 12. Report | Checklist complete, damage and low-stock items flagged | All photos uploaded |

Photos serve two purposes. The owner can check the work from two hours away, and the photos are evidence if a guest later reports a problem. The retained lesson 9.3 Dealing With Damages covers how to use damage photos in a claim.

Airbnb's [safety requirements](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/2904) say that if smoke and CO alarms are present, hosts must keep them working and test them regularly. The turnover checks that no alarm has been removed or disabled. A full button test on a schedule goes in the monthly inspection. Set the test schedule with the alarm manufacturer's instructions and your fire marshal.

### OP.8.3 Linen and consumable inventory

Par level means the amount you keep on hand. When stock falls to the reorder point, someone orders more. Write both numbers on the shelf label so nobody has to guess.

Linens follow the OP.6 cycle. Cedar Ridge has 18 sheet sets (three per bed for six beds) and 24 towel sets (three per guest for eight guests). One set is on each bed, one is in the laundry, one is on the shelf. The cleaner counts the shelf at every turnover. If any bed has fewer than one clean set on the shelf after the beds are made, the cleaner flags it.

Consumables run about $25 per stay at Cedar Ridge ($1,525 a year across 61 stays). Keep stock for four stays on site (assumption), about $100 worth, and reorder at two stays. That leaves room for a back-to-back weekend without a supply run.

| Item | Par (stays) | Reorder at | Counted by | Ordered by |
|---|---:|---:|---|---|
| Toilet paper, paper towels | 4 | 2 | Cleaner | Maya |
| Soap, shampoo, conditioner | 4 | 2 | Cleaner | Maya |
| Dish soap, dishwasher pods, sponges | 4 | 2 | Cleaner | Maya |
| Trash bags | 4 | 2 | Cleaner | Maya |
| Coffee, tea, sugar, oil, salt, pepper | 4 | 2 | Cleaner | Maya |
| Laundry detergent | 4 | 2 | Cleaner | Maya |
| Sheet sets on shelf | 1 per bed | 1 per bed | Cleaner | Maya |
| Towel sets on shelf | 8 | 8 | Cleaner | Maya |

Replacements follow the approval limits from OP.6 and OP.7. The cleaner replaces a broken glass or a stained towel from the spares shelf and notes it. Anything over $200 (assumption) needs owner approval before purchase. The cleaner never pays for replacements out of pocket without a written OK.

### OP.8.4 Missed-clean escalation

The worst operational failure is a guest arriving to a dirty house. It usually starts with a cleaner who doesn't show up and a host who finds out at 3:30 p.m. The escalation ladder catches it by late morning.

| Time on turnover day | Check | If it fails | Who acts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8 p.m. the night before | Cleaner confirms tomorrow's turnover in the task app | Owner calls the cleaner | Maya |
| 9 p.m. the night before | Cleaner still not confirmed | Owner alerts the backup cleaner to hold the slot | Maya |
| 11:00 a.m. | Arrival photo received | Call the cleaner; if no answer in 15 minutes, send the backup | Maya |
| 11:30 a.m. | Backup cleaner en route | Tell the arriving guest there may be a short delay; offer a later check-in time | Maya |
| 2:30 p.m. | Turnover more than half done (photo count) | Decide: delay check-in, or send a second cleaner | Maya |
| 3:45 p.m. | All photos in, report complete | Owner reviews photos before releasing the door code | Maya |
| After | Missed clean recorded | Second miss in 90 days triggers a scope review | Chris |

The backup cleaner is paid a same-day premium, $200 instead of $150 (assumption). If that happens four times a year, it costs 4 × $50 = $200 more. That's cheap next to a refund and a one-star cleanliness review.

### OP.8.5 Message sequence from booking to checkout

Every guest gets the same core messages at the same moments. Automation sends most of them. A named person owns each one and checks it.

Airbnb lets hosts send [scheduled quick replies](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/2897) automatically, triggered by events like a new booking, check-in, or checkout. The same help page says hosts can skip, send early, or edit an upcoming scheduled message without changing the template. When hosting with a team, members with guest management permissions share the scheduled replies, and messages are sent from the team owner. Airbnb also offers suggested quick replies, which use AI to suggest one of your existing replies for common questions.

| When | Message | Sent by | Owner who checks it | What must be right |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5 minutes after booking | Thanks, what to expect, house rules summary, guest count check | Scheduled | Maya | Names the correct guest count and no-party rule |
| 7 days before | Trip prep: directions, parking, lake access rules, what to bring | Scheduled | Maya | Seasonal details current |
| 1 day before, 10 a.m. | Check-in details: address, door code, Wi-Fi, where the house manual is | Scheduled, held for review | Maya | Correct unique code for this stay |
| Check-in day, evening | Short check-in follow-up: "Anything missing?" | Manual | Maya | Sent by a person who can act on the answer |
| Mid-stay (stays of 4+ nights) | Offer a restock or answer questions | Manual | Maya | Optional; skip for short stays |
| 1 day before checkout | Checkout steps: dishes, trash, thermostat, lock | Scheduled | Maya | Steps match the cleaner's expectations |
| After checkout | Thanks for staying | Scheduled | Maya | Neutral wording; see OP.9 on review requests |

Human review is a written weekly step, not a hope. Every Monday, the message owner opens the list of upcoming scheduled messages for the week. They check each one against the booking: guest count, dates, the lock code, and any special arrangement.

Automation needs limits. Write these into the SOP and into any AI messaging tool you use:

- No automated or AI reply goes to any message that mentions smoke, fire, gas, an alarm, water leaking, injury, police, a lockout, or a refund. Those go to a person immediately.
- No automated message sends a changed door code. A person sends it after checking the lock.
- An AI suggestion is a draft. A person approves it before it sends, unless it's an approved template for a routine question.
- Any exception granted (early check-in, late checkout, extra guest) gets written into the booking notes so the cleaner sees it.

SR.5 compares AI messaging tools on these criteria. Sam's OP.4 shows where messages and tasks live in the tech stack.

### OP.8.6 Emergency matrix

An emergency matrix tells the person in front of the problem what to do first, who to call, who can stop work, and what to record. Write it for the guest and the team together, and put the guest's part in the house manual.

Airbnb's help center says to contact [local emergency services](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/3063) first if there's an emergency in progress. Airbnb also describes an [Urgent Support Line](https://news.airbnb.com/expanding-the-urgent-support-line) in its app for hosts and guests during active reservations. Neither replaces 911, and neither replaces your own on-call person.

Stop-work authority means the right to halt a turnover, a repair, or the next booking because something isn't safe. At Cedar Ridge, anyone on the team can stop work for safety. Only an owner can reopen the calendar, and only after the right professional signs off.

| Incident | First action (guest or first person on site) | Who responds | Stop-work authority | Record |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fire or smoke | Get everyone out, then call 911 from outside | 911; after-hours contact goes to site; owner notified | Anyone; owner blocks the calendar | Incident report, fire department report number, photos, insurer notice |
| CO alarm | Get everyone out to fresh air, then call 911 | 911 or fire department; gas utility; licensed HVAC tech | Anyone; no one re-enters until the fire department clears it | Incident report, alarm location, readings if given, technician report |
| Water leak | Shut off water at the main (location marked in manual); move away from electrical | Handyman; plumber; owner | Handyman or owner | Photos, shutoff time, plumber invoice, insurer notice if damage |
| Lockout | Guest messages or calls the on-call number | Messaging owner resends or resets code; handyman on site if lock fails | n/a | Time locked out, cause, fix |
| Injury | Call 911 for anything serious; first aid kit location in manual | 911; owner notified immediately | Owner decides whether the area stays closed | Incident report (facts only), photos of the area, insurer notice |
| Power, water, or internet outage | Check the utility's outage page or house manual steps | Messaging owner; handyman if on-site check needed | Owner decides whether the stay continues | Outage times, utility reference, any guest credit |
| Severe weather | Follow official local warnings; shelter location in manual | Messaging owner sends a warning message; handyman after the storm | Owner decides on arrivals and relocations | Warning time, messages sent, damage photos |

Keep a laminated contact sheet inside the owner closet door and a copy in the house manual. It lists 911, the non-emergency police and fire numbers, the gas and electric utilities, the water shutoff location, the on-call phone, the handyman, the plumber, the HVAC company, and the insurer's claim line. Fill in the real local numbers before the first booking.

**Confirm with:** the local fire department on CO and fire response steps for this house; the insurer on how fast they need notice of an incident and what records they want.

### OP.8.7 Guest privacy and safety devices in the SOPs

Privacy rules belong in the SOPs, because the people who break them are often well-meaning. A cleaner might want to set up a "nanny cam" to watch for parties. That's not allowed.

Airbnb's [device rules](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/3061) prohibit security cameras and recording devices that monitor indoor spaces, even if they're off. Exterior cameras must be disclosed in the listing and can't watch indoor spaces or areas like an enclosed outdoor shower. Noise decibel monitors that don't record sound are allowed in common spaces if disclosed, and not in bedrooms, bathrooms, or sleeping areas.

Put three lines in the turnover SOP. The cleaner confirms no new device has appeared indoors. The noise monitor is still in its disclosed common-area spot. The doorbell camera still points only at the entry. Put one line in the data SOP too: camera footage and guest details stay in the platform or the device's own app. Nobody downloads them to a personal phone or shares them outside the team. The retained lesson 9.4 How to Avoid Parties & Smoking covers the party-prevention side.

### OP.8.8 Worked example: three walk-throughs at Cedar Ridge

**Normal turnover (Sunday).** A family of seven checks out at 10:00 a.m. after a 3.5-night stay. The next group of eight arrives at 4:00 p.m.

- 8:05 p.m. Saturday: the cleaner confirms in the task app.
- 10:25 a.m.: arrival photo received. The walk-through finds one chipped mug and a lake towel with a stain.
- 10:40 a.m.: all six beds stripped and laundry started. Six clean sets go on the beds and six used sets go to the wash, leaving 18 - 6 - 6 = 6 clean sets on the shelf.
- 10:45 a.m. to 1:50 p.m.: kitchen, bathrooms, bedrooms, living areas, deck (about 3 hours, assumption).
- 1:55 p.m.: restock. Consumables for one stay cost about $25; the shelf drops from four stays to three, above the reorder point of two.
- 2:00 p.m.: the mug is replaced from the spares shelf and the towel goes to the rag bin. Both are logged. Total under $200, so no approval is needed.
- 2:10 p.m.: safety check. All alarms in place, extinguishers mounted, no indoor devices, owner closet locked.
- 2:20 p.m.: final photos uploaded. Maya reviews them at 2:45 p.m. and releases the scheduled check-in message.
- Cost: cleaner pay $150; the guest paid a $175 cleaning fee.

**Guest problem (Tuesday).** A guest on a four-night midweek stay messages at 9:05 a.m.: "Internet is down. I have work calls today." The listing advertises a dedicated workspace.

- 9:05 a.m.: the message mentions no safety words, so an automated acknowledgment is allowed. The SOP still requires a person within 30 minutes (assumption). Maya replies at 9:15 a.m. with the house manual's reset steps: unplug the router for 30 seconds, wait five minutes.
- 9:35 a.m.: still down. Maya checks the internet provider's outage page. It shows an area outage with an estimated fix by 6 p.m.
- 9:40 a.m.: Maya tells the guest what she knows and when she'll update them. She doesn't guess at a restore time the provider hasn't given.
- 10:00 a.m.: Maya offers a goodwill credit of $100, inside her OP.7 limit. She pays it through the platform, not in cash, and doesn't tie it to a review. Airbnb's review policy prohibits offering something of value in exchange for a review (OP.9 covers this).
- 6:20 p.m.: service returns. Maya confirms with the guest.
- Record: outage times, provider reference, the credit, and one root-cause note. An advertised workspace with one internet line has no backup. Chris gets a quote for a cellular backup router. It's above $200 (assumption), so it goes on the owner decision list for the month-end review.

**Emergency (Saturday, 1:40 a.m.).** A guest calls the after-hours number: the CO alarm on the lower level is sounding.

- 1:40 a.m.: the handyman answers, per the OP.7 after-hours row. His first words follow the matrix: "Get everyone outside now, then call 911." He stays on the line until the guest confirms all eight people are outside.
- 1:44 a.m.: the guest calls 911. The handyman texts Chris.
- 1:48 a.m.: the handyman leaves for the house (about 35 minutes away, assumption). He doesn't go inside; the fire department controls the scene.
- 2:05 a.m.: the fire department arrives. They find elevated CO near the furnace (a propane furnace, assumption) and shut off the gas supply. Nobody goes back in tonight.
- 2:15 a.m.: Chris uses the Airbnb app's urgent support option and messages the guest. Relocation spending is above any team member's limit, so Chris authorizes it himself as an emergency exception. He books a nearby hotel.
- 2:30 a.m.: stop-work. The handyman tapes a "do not occupy" notice to the front door. Chris blocks the calendar for the next arrival, due Sunday afternoon.
- 8:00 a.m.: a licensed HVAC technician inspects the furnace. The house stays closed until the technician and gas supplier clear it in writing.
- Record: incident report with times, alarm location, fire department report number, HVAC findings, hotel receipt, and messages sent. Chris notifies the insurer the same day.
- Follow-up: Chris decides whether to cancel or relocate Sunday's guests based on the technician's timeline. The monthly inspection adds a furnace service check before each heating season.
