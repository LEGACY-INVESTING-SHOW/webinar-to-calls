Get a written answer for one address and one plan

Slide 1 · Get a written answer for one address and one plan

This resource is the sheet you fill in before you sign a lease or make an offer. It ends in one of three words: permitted, prohibited, or unresolved. Every answer on it points to an official page, a date, and the person who checked it.

"By state and city" means the sheet walks the state, county, and city layers for your address. It isn't a list of rules for each state.

We'll go through the rules that decide the result. Then we drop the Alder Street unit into New York City and into Los Angeles, and look at Cedar Ridge, where almost every line is still blank. I'm teaching a method here. A local attorney still confirms the decision for a real address.

Slide 2 · Rules attach to one address and one plan

Rules attach to the address, the building, the zoning district, the permit category, and your plan for the unit. They don't attach to a city in general. Two units on the same street can get different answers.

So this sheet never rates a state or a city as legal or Airbnb-friendly. It gives you a written decision for one exact address and one exact plan.

It pairs with Sam's live demos: 3.2, How to Research Regulations Before You Sign Anything, and PA.2 for owners. Sam shows you where to click. This sheet is where you write down what you found and what you decided.

Slide 3 · Each line gets a source, quote, date, and reviewer

Work the sheet top to bottom. Write the plan first, in Section A, because most rules turn on it. Then these five habits make every line worth trusting.

Official sources only: the city, county, and state pages, the adopted ordinance, and the tax agency. A platform page tells you what the platform does, not what the city allows. Airbnb calls its own regulations overview a starting point.

Quote the line that decides the case, with the URL beside it. Don't paraphrase it. Date every check, because a result without a date is a guess. Name a reviewer. "Self" is fine on a first pass, but a deciding line should also show a second reviewer, like a local short-term rental attorney.

Save PDFs and screenshots in one folder named with the address.

Slide 4 · Apply the rules in order and stop at the first yes

Section G takes one of three answers, and these rules pick it. Go in order. If a rule is a yes, that's your result, and you stop.

Rule 1 is the city, county, or state saying no to this plan. Rule 2 is the private layer: the lease, the HOA, the condo documents, or the building rules. A landlord who refused written permission lands here. Rule 3 is the permit itself, capped, closed, or not open to someone like you.

Rule 4 catches the gaps: a blank line, a "to verify", a line backed only by a platform page or a phone answer, or a landlord who hasn't replied yet.

Only when all four are no do you write permitted. That needs an official source dated within 90 days, a written answer wherever the source was unclear, and nothing open that could flip it.

Slide 5 · A decision covers only this plan, on this date

Unresolved means don't commit. You can keep talking to the landlord or the seller. A renter doesn't sign the lease or pay a deposit. A buyer can make an offer only with a regulatory contingency, and doesn't waive it or let it expire.

Permitted only covers the plan you wrote in Section A. Add guests or nights and you rerun the sheet.

Prohibited isn't always the end. Some cities treat stays of 30 nights or more differently, so copy your city's exact wording and give a longer-stay plan its own sheet.

Recheck 90 days after the decision and 30 days before any permit renewal. The 90 days is my teaching default, not a legal rule. Check sooner if an ordinance is pending.

Slide 6 · Ten layers, and any one can stop the deal

Section B has ten layers, and a no on any one of them stops the plan.

The first six are government. The state may have its own law or limit what cities can do. The county matters if the parcel sits outside city limits. The city has the ordinance and its definitions, and a definition like "primary residence" can decide your case. Then zoning, the building, and rent regulation.

The last four are private: the HOA or condo documents, your lease (lesson 5.3 covers the addendum), your insurer in writing (lesson 2.4), and for owners, the loan documents.

Permit details and tax registrations get their own sections, C and D. They're separate filings. Federal income tax stays with your CPA.

Slide 8 · In NYC, the plan fails three rules at once

First test: New York City. The Mayor's Office of Special Enforcement runs registration under Local Law 18 of 2022. Anyone renting for fewer than 30 consecutive days has to register.

Then the plan hits the rules. The host has to be present for stays under 30 days, and you can't rent an entire apartment. Stays are capped at two paying guests. And the person registering has to be an individual who permanently lives in the unit. Alder Street fails every one.

I've summarized each rule to fit the slide. On your sheet, paste the exact sentence. The course team checked these from search results on nyc.gov, so open each page yourself, and have an NYC attorney review a real case.

Slide 9 · In LA, the plan fails on primary residence

Same unit, same plan, now inside the City of Los Angeles.

LA's Home-Sharing Ordinance, adopted in December 2018, limits short-term rental to the host's primary residence. The host signs a declaration saying they live there more than six months of the year. You live elsewhere, so the plan fails right there.

Nights would be a second problem. Standard Home-Sharing allows up to 120 days a year, and our plan is 255.

A few lines stay marked to verify. The city's housing department says rent stabilization covers most non-single-family rentals built before October 1, 1978, so the building's year built matters. A draft ordinance for non-primary homes exists, and the search didn't show it adopted. Those open lines don't change today's answer.

Slide 10 · Airbnb pays LA's 14% tax; you still file monthly

I filled Section D for Los Angeles even though the plan fails, so you can see how it reads. In real use you'd stop at Section B.

The city Transient Occupancy Tax is 14% of rent for stays of 30 days or less. You register with the LA Office of Finance within 30 days of starting.

Airbnb collects and remits the city tax on its bookings. You still file a monthly return, due by the 25th of the next month, and deduct what Airbnb already paid. Vrbo is marked to verify. On direct bookings, you collect it and pay it yourself.

That's why Section D runs by channel. Lesson 3.3 goes deeper on occupancy tax.

Slide 11 · Both cities say prohibited, for different reasons

Put the two decisions side by side. Both say prohibited under Rule 1, for different reasons.

New York fails the plan on host presence and guest count. Los Angeles fails it on primary residence, and the 255 nights also exceed the 120-day cap. If you'd written "LA: legal with a permit" in a notes file, you'd have missed both conditions.

Each decision also points to a next step. In New York, a furnished rental of 30 nights or more is a different plan, so it gets a new sheet. In Los Angeles, you ask City Planning in writing whether the draft ordinance has been adopted.

Both get a recheck date of December 24, 2026, 90 days after the decision. And because we wrote the plan first, the New York test ended at Section B.

Cedar Ridge shows you a sheet on day one of due diligence. It's the hypothetical three-bedroom lake house Maya and Chris would buy. It sleeps 8, has no HOA, and they'd use it about 10 days a year.

The course example names no state or county, so all 18 rule lines are blank. That's Rule 4: unresolved.

Those 18 blanks collapse into five questions, grouped by who can answer them. Zoning and permit transfer go to the office before the contingency deadline. The lender confirms its occupancy rule before the rate lock. The insurer confirms before closing. Taxes don't change whether the use is legal, but they get done before the first booking.

Maya and Chris keep the offer's contingencies open until items 1, 2, 4, and 5 are answered in writing.

  1. Is this use allowed, under which permit?
  2. Any cap, waitlist, or pending change?
  3. Does the permit transfer on sale?
  4. Which safety, contact, and tax rules apply?
Slide 13 · Ask the office in writing, one address per email

When an official page doesn't answer a deciding question, you write to the office. The full sample email is in SR.1.7.

Open with the address, the parcel number, the zoning district, and the page you read, with the date. Then state your plan in a few lines: whole unit or room, hosted or not, stay length, guests, nights a year, and whether you own or lease.

Keep each email to one address and one plan. If you only need one answer, ask only that question. Offices answer faster when they can reply yes, no, or see section X.

A phone call helps you find the right office, but it doesn't clear a deciding line. Ask them to confirm by email, then save the reply as a PDF in Section E.
