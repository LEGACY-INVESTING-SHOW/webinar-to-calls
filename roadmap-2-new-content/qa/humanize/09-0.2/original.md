### 0.2.0 Learning outcome

By the end of this lesson you can split an ownership return into four streams: cash flow, principal paydown, change in value, and tax timing. You can say which of the four you can actually spend this year. You can compare owning a house with leasing a similar one on the same assumptions. And you can run a simple test that tells you whether "ownership wins" is true for a specific deal, or only true on a slide.

### 0.2.1 Treat "wins" as a claim you have to test

The title of this lesson makes a claim: ownership wins. Sometimes it does. Sometimes it loses badly. The only honest way to use that title is as a question you answer for one property, one loan, and one household.

The case for owning usually lists three benefits: cash flow, tax savings, and equity. I'm going to split equity into two parts, because they behave differently. That gives four possible return streams:

1. Operating cash flow: what's left after operating costs and the loan payment.
2. Principal paydown: the part of each loan payment that reduces what you owe.
3. Change in value: what the house is worth later compared with what you paid.
4. Tax timing: deductions, mainly depreciation, that may lower your tax bill now. Part of that benefit may be taxed back when you sell, which is why I call it timing rather than savings.

Each stream has a cost or a risk attached. And only one of them lands in your bank account on a normal month. The mistake I want you to avoid is adding all four together and calling the total "income". It is not income. Three of the four are either locked inside the house or depend on facts that won't be known for years.

### 0.2.2 Stream one: operating cash flow

This is the only stream you can spend this year. It is the money left after the house pays for itself.

Our example all the way through is Cedar Ridge. It's a hypothetical three-bedroom lake house that Maya and Chris, a two-earner household, are buying for $385,000. They're financing it with a DSCR loan quote. DSCR stands for debt service coverage ratio. In plain words, it's how many dollars of property income the lender counts for every dollar of loan payment. Lesson PA.6 (DSCR Loans 101) covers it. All figures are assumptions from the course example file.

- Gross booking revenue: 212 booked nights at $300, plus 61 cleaning fees of $175, for $74,275. That's an average nightly rate (ADR) of $300. With 212 of 365 nights booked, occupancy is about 58%.
- Operating costs: platform fees, cleaner, utilities, supplies, software, an on-call handyman, property tax, insurance, and two reserves. The total is $45,066.
- Net operating income (NOI): $74,275 minus $45,066, which is $29,209.
- Annual principal and interest on the DSCR quote (the debt service): $23,637.
- Cash flow: $29,209 minus $23,637, which is $5,572 a year, or about $464 a month.

That $5,572 is real money, if the base case happens. Notice what it already pays for: a 5% repairs reserve and a 5% capital reserve. That's money you set aside for repairs and big replacements, such as a roof. It stays in your account, but it isn't free to spend. If you drop those reserves to make the number look bigger, you are borrowing from the next roof.

Now the cost side. Against $142,688 of cash in, $5,572 is a 3.9% cash-on-cash return. Cash-on-cash means one year's cash flow divided by the cash you put in. The $142,688 is a $96,250 down payment, $14,438 of closing costs including points, and $32,000 of furnishing.

Then the weak case. The nightly rate is $265 instead of $300, and occupancy is 50% instead of 58%. The same house then loses $5,475 a year after debt service. So stream one is small in a good year and negative in a bad one.

### 0.2.3 Stream two: principal paydown

Each month, part of the loan payment reduces the loan balance. That part isn't money gone. It becomes equity: the share of the house you own outright.

On the Cedar Ridge DSCR quote, the balance falls by $2,795 in year one. That number grows a little each year because more of each payment goes to principal as the balance shrinks.

Here's the catch. You paid that $2,795 out of your cash flow already. It's inside the $23,637 of debt service. You can't spend it. You get it back only when you sell or refinance.

Refinancing can turn some of it into cash. In the course example, a refinance at the end of year two frees $15,725 after costs. The new loan is larger, so base-case cash flow falls from $5,572 to $4,752 a year.

The equity it builds also depends on the house holding its value. If the value falls, you can pay down the loan and still have less equity than you started with.

### 0.2.4 Stream three: change in value

This is the stream people talk about most and control least.

For teaching, the course example assumes Cedar Ridge's value grows 3% a year (assumption). In year one that would be 3% of $385,000, or $11,550. It is an assumption, not a forecast. Values can fall. Lake markets can see new supply, rule changes, or a slow year.

Three rules for this stream:

- Never add modeled appreciation to spendable monthly income. $11,550 of assumed value change is not about $960 a month you can use.
- You only collect it by selling or borrowing against it, and both cost money.
- Selling costs are large. The course example uses 7% of the sale price. At 3% a year, the house is worth an assumed $446,321 after five years. (That's $385,000 grown 3% a year for five years.) Selling costs of 7% on that are $31,242.

Here is a test worth running on any deal. What happens if the value is flat for five years? For Cedar Ridge, that means selling at $385,000. Subtract 7% selling costs of $26,950 and the year-five loan balance of $272,519. Net proceeds before tax are $85,531. You put in $142,688. That's a $57,157 gap. Only cash flow and tax timing can close it. Section 0.2.8 runs this.

### 0.2.5 Stream four: tax timing

Depreciation is a paper deduction for wear on the building. You don't write a check for it, but it can reduce taxable income. Whether it reduces your tax bill depends on several facts. Each one is a question for your CPA, not a number to assume.

**Basis and land.** You depreciate the building, not the land. IRS Publication 527 states that you can never depreciate land ([IRS Publication 527](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p527)). So the first input is how much of the price belongs to the land. The course example assumes 20% (assumption). Basis is, roughly, what the property cost you for tax purposes. Some closing costs can be added to it, which is what "capitalized" means. Your CPA decides which ones. The example's basis is $391,000: the $385,000 price plus $6,000 of the $14,438 of closing costs (assumption). Take out 20% for land ($78,200), and $312,800 is left for the building.

**Placed in service.** Depreciation starts when the property is ready and available for rent, not when the first guest arrives. Publication 527 gives an example: a house ready for rent in July and first rented in September starts depreciating in July ([IRS Publication 527](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p527)). For Cedar Ridge, a late-year launch gives a small first-year deduction on the building.

**Recovery period and classification.** The example file shows the building on two schedules. The 27.5-year schedule, labeled residential rental, gives $11,375 per full year. The 39-year schedule, labeled nonresidential or transient use, gives $8,021. Those labels come from the course example file. Short average stays can push a property onto the 39-year schedule. Your CPA decides which applies to your facts. The example uses a full year in year one to keep the math simple. A real first year is shorter and depends on the month the house is placed in service.

**Accelerated depreciation.** A cost segregation study can move some of the building into 5, 7, and 15-year property. In plain words, it's a report from a specialist that sorts parts of the building into shorter-life categories. Those parts can then be written off faster than the building itself. Furnishings are also short-life property. IRS guidance describes a permanent 100% first-year (bonus) depreciation deduction for eligible property acquired after January 19, 2025 ([IRS newsroom, Notice 2026-11 guidance](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/treasury-irs-issue-guidance-on-the-additional-first-year-depreciation-deduction-amended-as-part-of-the-one-big-beautiful-bill)). It applies to eligible property, not to land and not to the whole building. Sam's PA.5 (Cost Segregation & Year-One Depreciation, Explained) walks through the study itself.

In the course example, a 22% reclassification (assumption) puts $68,816 of the building into short-life property. The real share comes from the study. Add the $32,000 of furnishings, and the year-one bonus amount is $100,816, if eligible. Add a full year of 39-year depreciation on the remaining building ($6,256) and year-one deductions are about $107,072. At an assumed 32% marginal rate, that could lower tax by about $34,263. Your marginal rate is the rate on your top slice of income. The 32% is an assumption for the example.

Read the word "could" carefully. That result depends on the facts below: the passive rules, your participation hours, the $25,000 allowance, and personal use.

**Passive activity rules.** Losses from passive activities generally offset only passive activity income. IRS Publication 925 explains the rules ([IRS Publication 925](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p925)). Two parts of it matter most for short-term rentals:

- If the average stay is 7 days or less, Publication 925 doesn't count the activity as a "rental activity" under these rules. You figure the average by dividing total days in all rental periods by the number of rentals. Cedar Ridge's average stay is 3.5 nights: 212 nights over 61 stays.
- That alone doesn't make the loss usable. The loss is non-passive, and can offset other income such as W-2 wages (your paycheck), only if you also materially participate.

Publication 925 lists the material participation tests. Three of them matter most here. You participated more than 500 hours. Or your participation was substantially all the participation by anyone. Or you participated more than 100 hours and at least as much as any other individual, owner or not ([IRS Publication 925](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p925)). That last point matters for a short-term rental. A cleaner who turns the house over 61 times a year may log more hours than you do. If you hire a full-service co-host, their hours may exceed yours by a wide margin. Keep a real log of your hours. Your CPA will ask.

If you don't meet a test, the loss stays passive and may be suspended. The course example file notes that a suspended loss isn't lost: it's carried forward to later years. Your CPA confirms how that works for you.

**The $25,000 allowance does not rescue a high-income household.** Publication 925 also describes a special allowance of up to $25,000 for rental real estate with active participation. It is reduced by 50% of modified adjusted gross income above $100,000 and is gone at $150,000 ([IRS Publication 925](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p925)). Maya and Chris have $185,000 of combined W-2 income. If their modified AGI is near that figure, the allowance gives them nothing. At a short average stay, it may not apply at all.

**Personal use.** Maya and Chris plan about 10 personal days a year. Publication 527 has a day test. A dwelling is used as a home if personal use exceeds the greater of 14 days or 10% of fair-rental days. It also requires dividing expenses between rental and personal use by days ([IRS Publication 527](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p527)). At 212 rented nights, 10% is 21.2 days, so the threshold is 21.2 days. Ten days is below it. More personal use would change the picture, so ask your CPA how it would affect the loss.

**It's timing, not a gift.** Depreciation lowers your basis. When you sell, some of the deduction can come back as taxable gain (often called depreciation recapture). That's why this stream is called "tax timing". Lesson OP.12 (Exit Strategy: Hold, Refinance, Sell, or 1031 Exchange) treats the tax on sale as a CPA input.

### 0.2.6 Owning compared with leasing a similar house

Part One of this course is about leasing a place and re-renting it to guests (arbitrage). So a fair question is: why not lease a house like Cedar Ridge instead of buying it?

To compare fairly, keep the operating assumptions identical. Same revenue, same cleaner, same utilities. Change only what differs between owning and leasing. The lease figures below are a hypothetical arbitrage-style lease on a comparable house. **All figures in this table are assumptions.** Rent is $2,900 a month on a 12-month lease with written short-term rental (STR) permission. The tenant carries minor repairs of $1,200 a year and a renter's STR liability policy of $1,800 a year. The landlord pays property tax, building insurance, and capital repairs.

| Line (annual) | Own Cedar Ridge (DSCR quote) | Lease a comparable house (assumption) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Gross booking revenue (base) | $74,275 | $74,275 |
| Platform fee, cleaner, utilities, supplies, software, handyman | -$30,588 | -$30,588 |
| Repairs + capital reserve (own) / minor repairs (lease) | -$7,428 | -$1,200 |
| Property tax + insurance (own) / renter's STR policy (lease) | -$7,050 | -$1,800 |
| Loan payment (own) / rent (lease) | -$23,637 | -$34,800 |
| **Base-case cash flow** | **$5,572** | **$5,887** |
| Weak-case cash flow ($265 a night, 50% occupancy) | -$5,475 | -$6,856 |
| Cash in (see below) | $142,688 | $44,500 |
| Cash-on-cash, base | 3.9% | 13.2% |
| Principal paydown, year 1 | $2,795 | none |

How the lease cash in adds up: $32,000 of furnishing, a $2,900 deposit, and $2,900 of first month's rent. Add a two-month operating reserve of $6,700 (rent plus $450 of utilities, times two). Total: $44,500.

- Base-case cash flow is about the same. Leasing is $315 a year higher.
- Owning needs $98,188 more cash ($142,688 minus $44,500). That cash is the cost of streams two, three, and four.
- In the weak case, the lease loses more, because rent is fixed and higher than the owner's loan payment. The owner, though, also carries capital repairs that the reserve may not fully cover in a bad year.
- The owner builds equity and carries the value risk, up or down. The tenant has neither.
- The owner may depreciate the building, improvements, and furnishings. The tenant may depreciate only furnishings and their own improvements.
- The tenant's exposure ends with the lease, or on its early-exit terms. The owner's continues until a sale (7% costs assumed) or a refinance.

This is also why the lease path is not a fantasy. For some households, $44,500 in a lease beats $142,688 in a house. For others, the long hold and the equity matter more than the cash-on-cash rate.

One practical caveat. The lease column is a teaching comparison. In some drive-to lake markets, owners of houses like this may not be willing to lease them for short-term rental. Sam's PA.2 (Choosing a Market for Ownership) and the Part One research lessons show how to check.

### 0.2.7 Worked example: stacking the four streams without cheating

Here is the right way to present Cedar Ridge's year one on the DSCR quote. Keep the streams in separate rows, and label each by whether you can spend it.

| Stream | Year 1 amount | Can you spend it this year? | What it depends on |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Operating cash flow | $5,572 | Yes | Base case revenue; weak case is -$5,475 |
| Principal paydown | $2,795 | No, locked until sale or refinance | Paying the loan every month |
| Change in value (3%, assumption) | $11,550 | No, and it may be negative | Market; only realized by selling or borrowing |
| Tax timing (illustrative, if usable) | $0 to about $34,263 of tax saved | Only if the loss is usable against other income this year | Basis, land split, placed-in-service date, classification, passive rules, material participation, personal use, household tax rate; partly taxed back at sale (recapture) |

Now look at return on cash in, one layer at a time:

- Cash flow alone: $5,572 / $142,688 = 3.9%.
- Cash flow plus principal paydown: $8,367 / $142,688 = 5.9%.
- Adding assumed appreciation: $19,917 / $142,688 = 14.0%.

Those three percentages describe three very different things. Only the first one pays a bill. The third one includes an assumption you can't check until you sell. When someone shows you a single "total return" number, ask for this table instead.

I left tax timing out of those percentages on purpose. It depends on your CPA's answers, and it can be zero in year one if the loss is suspended (held for later years, not lost).

### 0.2.8 One case where ownership works, one where it doesn't

Now a five-year view, using the course example's sale model. Assumptions: base-case cash flow stays flat for five years (assumption), and the original DSCR loan stays in place. The house sells at the end of year five with 7% selling costs. Every figure is before income tax, and before any tax on the sale or depreciation recapture.

**Case A: ownership works (self-managed, base case, 3% value growth).**

- Five years of cash flow: 5 x $5,572 = $27,860.
- Net sale proceeds before tax: $142,560 (value $446,321, less loan balance $272,519, less selling costs $31,242).
- Total back: $27,860 + $142,560 = $170,420.
- Less cash in: $170,420 - $142,688 = $27,732 ahead, before tax.

Principal paydown isn't a separate line here. It's already inside net sale proceeds. The loan balance falls from the original $288,750 to $272,519 by year five, so $16,231 of paydown is in there.

Plus whatever tax timing your CPA confirms, less whatever tax is due on the sale. It is a modest result for five years and $142,688. But it is positive, and Maya and Chris keep control. It can absorb a year or two of weak revenue only if they hold cash reserves beyond the $142,688. Lesson 0.3 sizes those reserves.

**Case B: ownership does not work (full-service co-host at 18%).**

Suppose Maya and Chris decide they don't want to run the house. They hire a full-service local co-host: a local manager who runs the listing and guests for a share of revenue. This one takes 18% of booking revenue. The co-host costs $13,370 a year in the base case.

- Annual cash flow on the DSCR quote: -$7,798.
- Five years: 5 x -$7,798 = -$38,990.
- Total back: -$38,990 + $142,560 = $103,570.
- Less cash in: $103,570 - $142,688 = $39,118 behind, before tax.

In this case they pay about $650 a month to own the house. The only path to breaking even is appreciation above the 3% assumption or tax timing, and neither is spendable while you're writing checks every month. If revenue is also weak, the co-host case loses $15,794 a year: weak NOI of $18,162, less the co-host's $10,319, less $23,637 of debt service.

Two more versions of the same test, Cases C and D:

- Case C. Weak case, self-managed, 3% value growth: 5 x -$5,475 = -$27,375 of cash flow. Add $142,560 of proceeds and subtract $142,688 of cash in. Result: $27,503 behind, before tax.
- Case D. Base case, self-managed, flat value: $27,860 of cash flow plus $85,531 of proceeds, less $142,688. Result: $29,297 behind, before tax.

So Cedar Ridge "wins" only on specific facts. Revenue hits the base case or better. The owners self-manage, with a local cleaner and handyman rather than a full-service co-host. The house holds or gains value. Change any one of those and it falls behind over five years, before tax. Whether a weak-case loss is a deal-breaker depends on how much you could accept losing. That's test 2 in the decision rule below.

**Decision rule.** Ownership "wins" for this deal only if all three are true:

1. The Part B base case is ahead.
2. The Part B weak case is behind by no more than you could accept losing.

### 0.2.10 Who confirms what

**Confirm with:** your CPA the land allocation, placed-in-service date, recovery period, and bonus depreciation eligibility. Also confirm whether the activity counts as a rental activity for your household, your participation hours, and personal-use days. Ask whether any loss is usable this year or suspended, and what tax is due on a future sale.

**Confirm with:** your lender the loan balance schedule (amortization) and the actual payment, so principal paydown uses real numbers.

**Confirm with:** a local agent or appraiser the value assumptions you use for the five-year test. Treat any growth rate as your assumption, not theirs.

**Confirm with:** a cost segregation provider and your CPA whether a study makes sense for the property, before counting accelerated depreciation. Sam's PA.5 covers the study, and resource SR.3 (Cost segregation & tax-impact estimator) helps you prepare.
