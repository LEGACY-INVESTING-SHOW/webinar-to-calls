Slide 1 · Legal is a written decision for one address and one plan

This resource is the sheet you fill in before you sign a lease or make an offer. It ends in one of three words: permitted, prohibited, or unresolved. Every answer on it points to an official page, a date, and the person who checked it.

I'll walk you through each section of the template and the rules that decide the result. Then we run two tests. We drop the Alder Street unit into New York City, and then into Los Angeles. After that we look at Cedar Ridge, where almost every line is still blank, because that's what day one of due diligence looks like.

One thing to hold onto the whole way through. I'm teaching a method. I'm not your attorney, and nothing on these slides says a specific address is legal. The checklist helps you ask the right questions and write down the answers. A local professional still confirms the decision.

Slide 2 · Rules attach to the address and your plan, so neighbors can get different answers

Here's the idea the whole sheet is built on. Rules attach to the address, the building, the zoning district, the permit category, and your plan for the unit. They don't attach to a city in general. Two units on the same street can get different answers.

So this sheet never rates a state or a city as legal or Airbnb-friendly. It gives you a written decision for one exact address and one exact plan, with the official page behind every line.

It sits next to two of Sam's lessons. In 3.2, How to Research Regulations Before You Sign Anything, he runs the live search. Owners use it with his PA.2 when they screen markets. Don't expect this deck to repeat those demos. Watch Sam find the pages. Then come back here and write down what you found, the date, and what you decided.

Slide 3 · Work the seven sections top to bottom, because each one feeds the next

The order matters. You start with the plan in Section A, because most rules turn on those facts. Is the host there during the stay? Is it the whole unit or a room? How long do guests stay, how many of them, and who lives there the rest of the year? A vague plan gives you a vague answer.

Then Section B walks each layer of permission, from the state down to your lease and your insurance. Section C records the permit details. Section D records tax registrations and who remits on each channel. Keep permit registration and tax registration apart. They're separate filings.

E, F, and G close the loop. Anything unclear goes out as a written question. Anything still open gets an owner and a due date. Then you apply the decision rules and write the result, the reason, and a recheck date.

Slide 4 · Each line needs an official source, the quoted sentence, a date, and a reviewer

These five habits are what make the sheet worth trusting.

First, official sources only for rules. That means the city, county, and state pages, the adopted ordinance, and the tax agency. A platform page can tell you what the platform does. Airbnb says its own regulations overview is a starting point, so treat it that way.

Second, quote the line that decides the case. Don't paraphrase it. Paste the sentence and put the URL right beside it. When your attorney reads the sheet, they can check your reading in a few seconds.

Third, date everything. Fourth, name a reviewer. "Self" is fine on a first pass. Any line that decides the deal should also show a second person, such as a local short-term rental attorney.

Last, save the evidence. One folder, named with the address, holding PDFs of each page, portal screenshots, and any written replies.

Slide 5 · Apply the five decision rules in order and stop at the first one that fits

Section G takes one of three answers, and these rules pick it. Go in order. Stop at the first rule that fits.

Rule 1 is the city, county, or state saying no to this plan. Rule 2 is the private layer: the lease, the HOA, the condo documents, or the building rules. If the landlord hasn't given written permission, that's a no until they do. Rule 3 is the permit itself. It might be capped or closed. Or it might not be open to someone like you, for example when permits can't pass to a new operator.

Rule 4 catches the gaps. A blank line, a "to verify", or a line that rests on a platform page or on something someone told you on the phone. That's unresolved.

Only when all of that clears do you reach Rule 5 and write permitted. Notice how much Rule 5 asks for. Official source, dated within 90 days, a written answer where the page was unclear, and nothing open that could flip it.

Slide 6 · Unresolved means don't sign, and permitted covers only the plan in Section A

Three more rules keep the decision honest.

A permitted result only covers the plan you wrote in Section A. If you later decide to add a bed, take more guests, or rent more nights, you've changed the plan. Rerun the sheet.

Unresolved is the answer people try to talk themselves past. Here's the line. You can keep talking to the landlord or the seller. You don't sign the lease, you don't release a deposit, and you don't waive a contingency while the sheet says unresolved.

And prohibited isn't always the end. Some cities treat stays of 30 nights or more differently from short stays. That's a different business, with a different lease clause and different numbers, so it gets its own sheet.

On the recheck: I use 90 days as a teaching default. If the city has an ordinance in the works, check sooner. Always check again 30 days before a permit renewal.

Slide 7 · Most rules turn on the plan, so Section A pins it down before any research

Section A is the part people skip, and it's the part everything else depends on.

The top half pins down the property. Address and unit, the parcel number from the assessor, and whether you're inside a city or in unincorporated county. Then the zoning district, off the official zoning map. Year built can matter too, because rent-control coverage turns on it in some cities.

The bottom half is your plan. Does anyone live there as a primary residence? Is the host present? Whole unit or rooms? How long do guests stay, how many can sleep there, how many nights a year, and on which channels?

The Status column keeps you honest. Done means an official source, dated. Written answer means you have a reply from the office saved. To verify means you don't know yet. N/A needs a reason next to it. Fill this in once, and every later section reads off it.

Slide 8 · Section B checks ten layers of permission, and any one of them can stop the deal

Section B is where most deals get decided. There are ten layers, and a no on any one of them stops the plan.

The first six are government layers. The state may have its own law, or may limit what cities can do. The county matters if the parcel sits outside city limits. The city has the ordinance and its definitions, and definitions matter. What counts as a host, or a primary residence, can decide your case. Then zoning, the building, and whether the unit is under rent regulation.

The last four are private. The HOA or condo documents. Your lease, with written permission, which lesson 5.3 covers. Your insurer, in writing, which 2.4 covers. And for owners, the loan documents.

For the HOA row, you won't find a government page. Write down where you got the documents. Every row still gets a date, a result with the quoted line, and a reviewer.

Slide 9 · Permit and tax registrations are separate filings, so they get separate sections

Section C is the permit itself. Name the category that fits your plan, not just "the STR permit". Then the prerequisites, the fee, and how long it lasts. Caps and waitlists go here, and so does the primary-residence test, with the exact definition and the proof it needs. Transferability is the one owners miss. Does the permit stay with the owner, the host, or the property? Can a new operator use it?

Section D is taxes, and it runs by channel. A platform may collect some taxes and not others. You may still need to register and file returns even when a platform remits. On direct bookings, you collect and remit. Write down each tax, the agency, the rate, whether cleaning fees are in the base, and how often you file.

Lesson 3.3 teaches occupancy tax properly. Here you're only recording it. And keep federal income tax out of this sheet. That's a conversation with your CPA.

Slide 10 · Sections E to G turn open questions into owners, due dates, and a dated decision

The last three sections are short, and they're where the sheet earns its keep.

Section E is your written questions. For each one, quote the rule you're asking about, note who you sent it to and when, and paste the answer when it comes back. Save the reply as a PDF.

Section F is the open list. Every item gets a person who clears it: you, your attorney, your CPA, your insurer, your lender, or the licensing office. It gets a due date. And it gets a yes or no on one question: could this item change the decision?

Section G is the decision. Write which of the three results you reached and which rule got you there. Add one sentence of reason with its source. If it's permitted, list the conditions, like night caps and posting rules. Then the names, the date, and the recheck date. Someone else should be able to read this row and see exactly why you decided what you did.

Slide 12 · In NYC, Alder Street fails at Section B on host presence and the two-guest limit

First test: New York City. The city's Mayor's Office of Special Enforcement, OSE, runs registration under Local Law 18 of 2022. Anyone renting housing for fewer than 30 consecutive days has to register.

Then the plan runs into three rules at once. OSE says the host has to be present for stays under 30 days, and you can't rent an entire apartment or home. It caps stays at two paying guests. And the person registering has to be a natural person who permanently lives in the unit. Alder Street is unhosted, whole unit, 5 guests, and the operator lives elsewhere. It fails on every one.

Platforms also can't process bookings for unregistered rentals, so this isn't a rule you can quietly ignore.

Notice the rows marked not reached. I didn't research the lease or insurance, because the city rule already ended it. One caution: these checks came from official-domain search, not from opening the pages. Open them yourself before you rely on any of this.

Same unit, same plan, now inside the City of Los Angeles.

LA's Home-Sharing Ordinance, adopted in December 2018, limits short-term rental to the host's primary residence. The host signs a declaration saying they live there more than six months of the year. Our operator lives elsewhere, so the plan fails right there.

Slide 14 · In LA, Airbnb remits the 14% city TOT, but the host still registers and files monthly

I filled Section D for Los Angeles even though the plan fails, so you can see how the section reads.

The city Transient Occupancy Tax is 14% of rent for stays of 30 days or less. You register with the LA Office of Finance and get a TOT Registration Certificate within 30 days of starting.

Now the part people get wrong. Airbnb collects and remits the city TOT on Airbnb bookings, and its article says the base includes cleaning fees. That doesn't mean you're finished. The host still files a monthly return, due by the 25th of the next month, and deducts what the platform already remitted. Vrbo's handling is marked to verify. On direct bookings, you collect and remit it all yourself.

That's why this section has a column per channel. The same tax can be handled three different ways depending on where the guest booked. Lesson 3.3 goes deeper on this.

Slide 16 · Most wrong answers come from a city label, a vague plan, or an unquoted source

Here are six mistakes to look for on any sheet, including your own.

The first one we just saw. A city label hides the conditions that actually decide your case. The second is starting research before the plan is written. If you don't know your guest count or whether you'll be on site, you can't tell whether a rule applies.

Third, a platform page or a phone call is not the final word. Platform pages are useful starting points. Phone calls are useful for finding the right office. Neither clears a deciding line.

Fourth, paraphrasing. Your summary of a rule is a claim. The quoted sentence with its link is evidence. Fifth, undated lines and no second reviewer on the lines that matter.

And the last one is the expensive one. Signing a lease or waiving a contingency while the sheet still says unresolved.

Email one topic at a time, quote the rule, and ask for the answer in writing

Give the address, parcel, zoning district, the page you read with its date, and your exact plan. Then ask:

  1. Is this use allowed at this parcel? Which permit or registration category applies?
  2. Is there a cap, waitlist, or pending change that affects a new application?
  3. If the property is sold, does the permit transfer, or must the buyer apply again?
  4. Which safety inspection and local-contact rules apply?
  5. Which lodging-tax registrations does your office or another office require?
A phone answer is not enough

A call helps you find the right office. It doesn't clear a deciding line under Rule 5. Ask the person to confirm by email.

Slide 18 · Email one topic at a time, quote the rule, and ask for the answer in writing

When an official page doesn't answer a deciding question, you write to the office. The full sample email is in SR.1.7. Here's how it's built.

Open with the facts: the full address, the parcel number, the zoning district, and the page you read, with the date. Then state your plan in one short paragraph. Whole unit or room, hosted or not, average stay, guests, nights a year, whether it's your primary residence, and whether you own or lease.
