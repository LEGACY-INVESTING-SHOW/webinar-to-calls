Slide 1 · Test the house and the business before your deadlines

This lesson starts the day your offer is accepted. You have a house under contract and a short window to find out what you actually bought.

I'll show you how to spend that window. We test the building, and we test the rental business Sam underwrote in PA.4. Then we rerun those numbers with what we found.

You leave with two things: a filled diligence tracker, and a dated decision to proceed, renegotiate, or walk.

We stay with Cedar Ridge, the course example. Maya and Chris have it under contract at $385,000. Every finding I show you is made up for teaching. Your attorney, inspector, insurer, and lender confirm the real answers for your address.

Slide 2 · Cedar Ridge starts diligence with $5,572 a year of cash flow

Here's what Maya and Chris bring into diligence. These are the pre-diligence numbers from Sam's PA.4.

Cedar Ridge is a three-bedroom lake house that sleeps 8, with no HOA. In the base case it earns $74,275 a year at $300 a night and 58% occupancy. NOI, net operating income, is revenue minus operating costs before the loan. That's $29,209. In a weak year it's $18,162.

The loan is a DSCR loan, sized mainly on the property's rental income. Principal and interest run $23,637 a year. So the base case clears $5,572, and the weak case loses $5,475.

Total cash in, with the $32,000 furnishing budget, is $142,688. Every finding in diligence gets tested against these numbers.

Slide 3 · Order the work by what can kill the deal fastest

Your accepted offer bought you time. That time is the real asset in this phase.

A contingency is a condition in the contract that lets you cancel if it isn't met. Most contracts give you a window to inspect and a window to get financing. Put every deadline in a calendar the day you sign. A problem you find after a window closes is still your problem, and most of your bargaining power is gone.

So order the work by what can kill the deal fastest. Legal use and the insurance quote go first, because no repair fixes a ban or an uninsurable house. Inspections come next, because specialists book up. Title and survey run alongside. The lender orders the appraisal, so calendar its due date.

Open one property folder on day one. Every report and quote goes in, dated.

Slide 4 · Inspect for eight guests, not one family

Start with a general home inspection by an inspector you've checked out yourself. Then tell them the plan. Eight guests, turnovers every few days, people who don't know the house.

This is the list I'd hand over for a house like Cedar Ridge. A roof near the end of its life is a bill you pay while guests are booked. On a private well, the water test is on you, so test during the contingency, not after closing. For septic, pump the tank, check the baffles and the drain field, and ask what bedroom count it was permitted for. That number comes back when we get to capacity.

Also check HVAC, pests, and winter access. Look up the address on the FEMA flood map, because flood is a separate policy. Every finding gets a written cost range.

Slide 5 · Five tests decide if this address can run an STR

A normal homebuyer asks one question: is this house sound? You have to ask a second one. Can this house run the business in your PA.4 numbers, at 8 guests and $300 a night? A normal home inspection never touches this half.

First, legal use. A market answer is not an address answer, so run the SR.1 checklist on this parcel. Second, does the permit transfer, or do you apply fresh? Third, recorded covenants can limit rentals even with no HOA. Fourth, get a bindable insurance quote before the inspection window closes.

Fifth, capacity. Put the septic, the permit cap, and the insurer's limit all in guests, and take the lowest. At Cedar Ridge that's still 8. If it's lower for you, your revenue line changes first.

Slide 6 · A low appraisal adds $7,424 to cash to close

You need an inspection and an appraisal, and neither covers the other. The inspector works for you and looks for flaws. The appraiser estimates market value for the lender. A clean appraisal doesn't mean the septic works. A clean inspection doesn't mean the house is worth your price.

PA.8 already set the low-appraisal rule. Maya and Chris ask the seller to drop to value and accept up to $380,000. If the seller holds at $385,000, they walk. Your job here is to know the cash effect the day the report lands.

Say it appraises at $375,000. Lenders generally size the loan on the lower of price or value, so confirm your lender's rule in writing. Cash to close becomes $118,112. That's $7,424 more than the planned $110,688.

Slide 7 · Title exceptions stay with the land after you buy

The title company searches public records and sends you a title commitment. Read it yourself, with your attorney or closing agent. You're the one who knows how you plan to use the land.

The requirements list what has to happen before the title company will insure, like paying off the seller's mortgage. The exceptions list what the policy won't cover. Anything in the exceptions stays with the land after you buy it.

On a lake lot with a dock and shared access, I'd order a survey.

Then the two policies. Most lenders require the lender's policy, and it covers only the loan. The owner's policy is optional and protects your equity. With a $96,250 down payment at stake, I'd price it and decide on purpose.

Slide 10 · The first rerun breaks three buy-box rules

Take $3,820 off the base-case NOI and it drops to $25,389. After the $23,637 of debt service, cash flow falls from $5,572 to $1,752 a year. The weak case falls to minus $9,295.

Maya and Chris wrote their buy-box rules down in PA.1 and PA.8. Base-case cash flow of at least $5,000. A weak year losing no more than $6,000. NOI at least 1.20 times principal and interest. The first rerun fails all three.

Their written rule for a broken buy box is renegotiate or walk. So they asked the seller for a $7,000 roof credit. The seller refused, and refused a price cut. The seller did agree to fix the septic, treat the well, and pay the lien. That helps the cash, but not these three rules. So before walking, they reread their own budget.

Slide 13 · Run four gates before your windows close

These are the decision rules. Run them in order, before your windows close.

Gate one is legal use. If STR use at this address is prohibited or still unresolved, walk, or get the window extended in writing. Don't close on probably.

Gate two is insurance. If no insurer will write a policy at a price your numbers can carry, walk.

Gate three is capacity. If the real number is lower than you assumed, rerun revenue before you touch anything else.

Gate four is your written rules: a positive base case, your buy box, and your PA.8 cash test. If the revised numbers break any of them, renegotiate or walk. A budget or rule change counts only in writing.

If everything passes, write one sentence with the decision, the date, and the reason.

Slide 14 · Put deadlines and owners on paper first

Three things this week.

First, put every contingency deadline in a calendar, with a reminder two days before each one. You don't want to decide on the last afternoon.

Second, copy the 20-row tracker from the lesson and fill in the Verified by and Deadline columns before any findings come in. Every row gets an owner and a date. Your attorney or agent confirms the deadlines and how to cancel without losing your earnest money.

Third, as findings land, fill in the rerun box in the lesson and run the four gates. No deal yet? Practice on Cedar Ridge.

Next is PA.10, whose name goes on the deed and the loan. Before you watch it, ask your lender whether it lends to an LLC, and ask your insurer who can be the named insured. Get both answers in writing.
