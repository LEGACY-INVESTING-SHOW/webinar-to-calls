### 0.3.0 Learning outcome

By the end of this lesson you can add up the full cash an ownership deal requires, not just the down payment. You can compare it with the money you actually have, after keeping your own emergency savings. You can test whether you'd survive a delayed opening or a weak first season. And you finish with a one-page readiness scorecard that sends you to one of four places: proceed to PA.1, improve your financing or liquidity first, use the lease path from Part One, or wait.

### 0.3.1 The down payment is the smallest question

Most buyers start with one number: the down payment. For Cedar Ridge, 25% of $385,000 is $96,250. Maya and Chris have $210,000 in liquid savings outside retirement accounts. On that math alone, they look ready twice over.

That math leaves out most of the bill. An owned short-term rental needs money for closing, furnishing, repairs, permits, the months before the first guest, reserves the lender checks, and reserves you keep for yourself. It also needs your own household to stay safe if the house has a bad year.

This lesson lays out every layer, then runs Maya and Chris through it. The goal is a clear answer before anyone sends you listings. It is much cheaper to learn "not yet" here than at a closing table.

### 0.3.2 The full cash stack, layer by layer

Think of the cash requirement as a stack. Each layer sits on top of the one before it. You need all of them funded, or you are relying on the house to perform from day one.

| Layer | What it covers | Spent or held? |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Down payment | Your share of the price | Spent (becomes equity) |
| 2. Closing costs and points | Lender fees, title, escrow, prepaid items, discount points | Spent |
| 3. Furnishing and setup | Furniture, linens, kitchen, safety equipment, photos | Spent |
| 4. Initial repairs and safety punch list | Items from the inspection you accept, plus work needed before guests | Spent |
| 5. Permit, registration, inspections | Local fees and specialist inspections | Spent |
| 6. Launch carrying costs | Loan payment, utilities, software, and vendor retainers before the first booking | Spent |
| 7. Property reserves | The lender's required reserves, which after closing become your operating cushion for the house | Held |
| 8. Personal emergency savings | Your household's own safety net, separate from the house | Held |

Layers 1 to 6 are money you will spend. Layers 7 and 8 are money you must still have after spending it.

The most common failure is merging layers 7 and 8. A buyer counts the same dollars as the house's reserve and the family's emergency fund. Then the water heater fails in the same month a paycheck is late, and there's only one pile of cash for two problems.

### 0.3.3 Where the money can come from, and the limits on each source

Money for the stack can come from several places. Each has limits, and your lender may treat them differently.

**Liquid savings.** Checking, savings, and taxable brokerage accounts. This is the cleanest source. Lenders usually want to see where it came from and how long it has been there. Ask your lender what documentation they need for large recent deposits.

**Retirement accounts.** Maya and Chris's $210,000 excludes retirement accounts. Some lenders may count part of a retirement balance toward reserves, and withdrawals can carry taxes and penalties. Treat this as a question for your lender and CPA, not as spendable cash.

**Home equity.** Maya and Chris own their primary home. It's worth $520,000 with a $260,000 mortgage balance, so they have $260,000 of equity on paper. A home equity line of credit (HELOC) lets you borrow against it. The CFPB notes that HELOCs usually carry a variable rate, so payments can change, and your home is the collateral. If you can't repay, you could lose your home ([CFPB, What is a HELOC?](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-home-equity-line-of-credit-heloc-en-107/)). A HELOC is borrowed money, not savings. It adds a payment to your household budget. Ask the lender whether HELOC funds can be used for a down payment or counted as reserves on their loan, and how the new payment affects your qualification. OP.11 covers HELOCs for scaling in more depth.

**Gifts or partners.** Lenders have rules on gift funds and on co-borrowers. If a partner puts in money, the ownership and the loan paperwork need to match. That's a PA.10 conversation with an attorney and CPA.

**Seller concessions.** A seller may agree to pay part of your closing costs. That can reduce layer 2. It's a negotiation item for PA.8, not something to count on now.

For this lesson, we count only liquid savings. If the stack only works with borrowed money, write that down as a finding. It changes your risk.

### 0.3.4 Reserves: the lender's minimum is not your plan

Lenders measure reserves in months of the new loan's payment. Fannie Mae's Selling Guide section B3-4.1-01 measures reserves as the number of months of the subject loan's qualifying payment (principal, interest, taxes, insurance, and association dues, or PITIA) that your financial assets could cover ([Fannie Mae Selling Guide, B3-4.1-01 Minimum Reserve Requirements](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-4.1-01/minimum-reserve-requirements)).

A few points from that section, as they apply to a buyer like Maya and Chris:

- There is no minimum reserve requirement for a one-unit principal residence. Second homes and investment properties do have requirements.
- For loans run through Fannie Mae's automated underwriting system (Desktop Underwriter, or DU), DU determines the required reserves. For manually underwritten loans, the minimum is in Fannie Mae's Eligibility Matrix.
- If you have multiple financed properties and the new loan is on a second home or investment property, additional reserves apply. They are calculated as a percentage of the unpaid balances on your other financed properties, excluding the subject property and your principal residence. The guide uses 2% for one to four financed properties, 4% for five to six, and 6% for seven to ten.

Maya and Chris have two financed properties after this purchase: their home and Cedar Ridge. The other-property add-on would exclude both, so it would likely be zero for them. Their lender confirms the actual figure.

The DSCR quote in the canonical example is not a Fannie Mae loan. Each DSCR lender publishes or quotes its own reserve rule. For teaching, we assume the DSCR lender requires **6 months of PITIA (assumption)**. On the DSCR quote that is 6 x $2,557 = $15,342. We use the same 6 months (assumption) on the conventional quote so the two are comparable: 6 x $2,484 = $14,904. Get the real number from each lender in writing. PA.6 and PA.7 go deeper.

Now the important part. After closing, the lender stops watching. The reserve becomes your operating cushion for the house. The lender's number is a floor to get the loan. It is not a plan for a bad season. Maya and Chris will set their own reserve target in the worked example.

### 0.3.5 Credit, eligibility, and debt capacity

Four questions decide whether a lender will lend, before the house is even chosen.

**Credit.** Maya and Chris have scores around 740. Each lender sets its own minimum and prices its rate by score. Kiavi's DSCR guide, for example, says the lender looks at the borrower's credit score and available down payment as well as the property's coverage ratio ([Kiavi DSCR guide](https://www.kiavi.com/the-complete-guide-to-dscr-rental-property-loans)). That is one lender's approach, not a market standard.

**Eligibility of the loan type.** A DSCR loan qualifies mainly on the property's income compared with its payment. A conventional loan qualifies mainly on your household income and debts. Fannie Mae updated its rental income guidance in September 2026, including a section on subject-property short-term rental income ([Fannie Mae Selling Guide, B3-3.8-03](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-3.8-03/rental-income-subject-property-short-term-rental)). Whether a particular lender will count projected or actual STR income for your loan is their call. PA.7 compares the two paths.

**Debt capacity.** A conventional lender compares your monthly debts with your income (the debt-to-income ratio, or DTI). Here is a rough illustration for Maya and Chris. **All figures are assumptions** except their income and the Cedar Ridge PITIA.

| Line | Monthly |
|---|---:|
| Gross income ($185,000 / 12) | $15,417 |
| Primary home payment (assumption) | $2,400 |
| Other debts: cars, cards (assumption) | $600 |
| Cedar Ridge PITIA, conventional quote | $2,484 |
| **Total debts** | **$5,484** |
| **DTI with no STR income counted** | **35.6%** |

The same calculation with the DSCR quote's $2,557 gives 36.0%. The lender sets the maximum ratio and decides how much rental income, if any, to credit. This rough check only tells you whether a conventional path is worth asking about.

**Liquidity.** That's the cash stack, and it's the question most buyers skip. Section 0.3.8 runs it.

### 0.3.6 A delayed opening or a weak first season

Two things go wrong often enough that you should budget for both.

**Delayed opening.** Permits take longer than expected. Furniture arrives late. A contractor misses a week. Every month between closing and the first guest costs the full carrying cost with no revenue. For Cedar Ridge on the DSCR quote, that is the $2,557 PITIA, plus $450 of utilities, $100 of software, and the $150 handyman retainer (the canonical figures), for $3,257 a month.

The base stack includes two months of carrying costs (assumption), or $6,514. A three-month delay beyond that adds another 3 x $3,257 = $9,771. Paid from the 6-month property reserve of $15,342, that would leave $5,571.

**Weak first season.** A new listing has no reviews and may book below its long-run potential. The canonical weak case, ADR $265 at 50% occupancy, loses $5,475 a year on the DSCR quote. That is about $456 a month. The $15,342 property reserve would cover about 2.8 years of that loss, if nothing else went wrong.

Both of those together, a delay and then a weak year, would use most of the property reserve. That is the scenario to plan for, because it's the one that forces people to sell at a bad time.

### 0.3.7 Time, involvement, and risk tolerance

Cash isn't the only resource. Owning also takes time and a stomach for variance.

**Time.** In the canonical base case, Maya and Chris self-manage with a local cleaner and an on-call handyman. Assume they spend 1.5 hours per stay on messages, scheduling, and checking the turnover, and 4 hours a month on bookkeeping, pricing review, and vendor calls (assumption). With 61 stays, that is 91.5 + 48 = about 140 hours a year, or 2.7 hours a week (assumption). Some weeks will take much more: a lockout at 11 p.m., a broken hot water heater on a Saturday.

That time estimate also matters for taxes. As 0.2 explained, whether losses are usable can depend on material participation. Publication 925's tests look at hours, including how your hours compare with those of other individuals, such as a cleaner ([IRS Publication 925](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p925)). If the cleaner spends 4 hours on each of 61 turnovers (assumption), that is 244 hours, more than the owners' 140. Your CPA confirms how your hours count.

**Involvement choice.** If you don't want to spend those hours, the alternative is a co-host or manager. In 0.2, a full-service co-host at 18% turned Cedar Ridge's base-case cash flow from $5,572 to -$7,798 a year. Decide now which kind of owner you'll be, because it changes whether the deal works.

**Personal use.** Maya and Chris plan about 10 personal days a year. IRS Publication 527 treats a dwelling as used as a home if personal use is more than the greater of 14 days or 10% of the days rented at fair rental price, and requires dividing expenses by days ([IRS Publication 527](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p527)). At 212 rented nights, that threshold is 21.2 days, so 10 days is below it. There is also a revenue cost. If those 10 nights would have booked at the $300 base ADR, they're worth up to $3,000 of nightly revenue (assumption). Plan your own stays for slower weeks.

**Risk tolerance.** In 0.2, the five-year test showed Cedar Ridge ahead $27,732 before tax in the base case and behind $27,503 in the weak case. Both of you should look at the second number and say out loud whether you could live with it. If the honest answer is no, that's a readiness result, not a failure.

### 0.3.8 Worked example: Maya and Chris's full cash stack

Maya and Chris have $210,000 in liquid savings. Their household's essential spending is $8,500 a month (assumption), and they want to keep 6 months of it as personal emergency savings (assumption): $51,000.

Here is the stack on each quote. Canonical figures come from the course file. New figures are marked (assumption).

| Layer | DSCR quote | Conventional quote |
|---|---:|---:|
| Down payment (25%) | $96,250 | $96,250 |
| Closing costs incl. points | $14,438 | $11,550 |
| **Cash to close** | **$110,688** | **$107,800** |
| Furnishing and setup | $32,000 | $32,000 |
| Initial repairs and safety punch list (assumption) | $4,000 | $4,000 |
| Inspections: general, septic, specialist (assumption) | $1,500 | $1,500 |
| Permit and registration fees (assumption) | $600 | $600 |
| Launch carrying costs, 2 months (assumption) | $6,514 | $6,368 |
| **Total spent (layers 1 to 6)** | **$155,302** | **$152,268** |
| Property reserve, 6 months PITIA (assumption) | $15,342 | $14,904 |
| Personal emergency savings, 6 months (assumption) | $51,000 | $51,000 |
| **Full stack** | **$221,644** | **$218,172** |
| Liquid savings | $210,000 | $210,000 |
| **Surplus / gap** | **-$11,644** | **-$8,172** |

Step by step for the DSCR column. Spent layers: $110,688 + $32,000 + $4,000 + $1,500 + $600 + $6,514 = $155,302. Add the $15,342 property reserve and the $51,000 emergency fund: $221,644. Subtract from $210,000: a gap of $11,644.

Two readings of the same table:

- **The lender's view.** After spending $155,302, Maya and Chris would still hold $54,698. That's about 21 months of the DSCR PITIA, well above a 6-month reserve rule. On paper, a lender may say yes.
- **Their own plan.** They can't fund the house's reserve and their own emergency savings at the same time. They are $11,644 short on the DSCR quote and $8,172 short on the conventional quote.

This is exactly the gap that the down payment check hides. The lender being satisfied does not mean the household is safe.

What can close the gap? Options, in the order I'd look at them:

1. **Time.** If they save $3,500 a month (assumption), the DSCR gap closes in about 3.3 months, the conventional gap in about 2.3 months. Searching, underwriting, and closing will take a while anyway.
2. **Financing.** The conventional quote needs $2,888 less at closing and has a lower payment, if they qualify for it. That's a PA.7 comparison.
3. **Price.** A lower price reduces the down payment and closing costs. PA.1 turns this into a hard cap.
4. **Furnishing plan.** A phased furnishing plan may cut the up-front budget. OP.6 covers this without cutting safety or listing accuracy.

What they should **not** do: shrink their emergency fund to make the table balance, or count a HELOC as savings.

### 0.3.9 The one-page readiness scorecard

Fill this in before you shop. Score each line Green, Yellow, or Red. The outcome rules follow the table.

**Readiness scorecard** (one page)

| # | Check | Green | Yellow | Red | Your score | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Full cash stack (0.3.8) from liquid savings | Surplus of $0 or more | Gap you can close with normal saving within 6 months | Larger gap, or funded only with borrowed money | | |
| 2 | Personal emergency savings kept intact after closing | 6+ months of essential spending | 3 to 6 months | Under 3 months | | |
| 3 | Property reserve after closing | At or above the lender's rule and your own target | At lender rule only | Below lender rule | | |
| 4 | Credit | At or above the lender's written minimum, with room | At the minimum | Below the minimum | | |
| 5 | Lender fit | Written pre-qualification or term sheet for at least one loan path | Verbal only | None, or declined | | |
| 6 | Debt capacity (conventional) or property coverage (DSCR) | Inside the lender's stated limit | Near the limit | Outside it | | |
| 7 | Delayed opening: 3 extra months absorbed | From property reserve, emergency fund untouched | Needs part of emergency fund | Cannot be absorbed | | |
| 8 | Weak first season | Weak-case loss covered 2+ years by property reserve | 1 to 2 years | Under 1 year | | |
| 9 | Time available | Weekly hours available exceed the plan's estimate, plus a local backup | Just enough | Not enough, and no budget for a co-host | | |
| 10 | Involvement choice tested | Deal still works with your chosen management model (0.2) | Works only if you self-manage and you're unsure you will | Doesn't work with the model you'll actually use | | |
| 11 | Risk tolerance | Both owners accept the five-year weak-case result in writing | One owner unsure | Either owner can't accept it | | |
| 12 | Personal use plan | Below the Pub 527 threshold, or reviewed by CPA | Plan unclear | Heavy personal use, not reviewed | | |
| 13 | Lease path considered | 0.2 Part C done; owning chosen for a stated reason | Not compared | Leasing clearly fits better and you'd still buy | | |

**Outcome rules**

| Result | Where you go next |
|---|---|
| All Green, or Yellow only on line 1 with a dated funding plan | **Proceed to PA.1.** Write the funding condition into your buy box: no offer until the stack is funded. |
| Yellow or Red on lines 3 to 6 | **Improve financing or liquidity first.** Get written quotes (PA.6, PA.7), pay down debt, or save. Rescore in 60 to 90 days. |
| Red on line 1 or 2, and lines 9 to 11 look fine | **Use the lease path (Part One).** A lease needs far less cash. Alder Street needed $20,370 on day one. Start with lesson 1.3. |
| Red on line 9, 10, or 11 | **Wait.** Fix the time or risk question before money is at stake. If the time problem is the only issue, consider whether a co-host still leaves the deal positive. |
| Two or more Reds anywhere | **Wait.** Rescore in six months. |

### 0.3.11 Who confirms what

**Confirm with:** each lender, in writing, the reserve months (and how they're calculated), minimum credit score, down payment, acceptable sources of funds (including HELOC funds, gifts, and retirement assets), and how any rental income is counted.

**Confirm with:** your CPA the tax effect of withdrawing from retirement accounts, how your hours count for material participation, and how your planned personal-use days are treated.

**Confirm with:** your insurance agent the premium and when it's due, so layer 2 or layer 6 of the stack is accurate.

**Confirm with:** the local permit office the fees and processing time, so the launch carrying-cost assumption is realistic.
