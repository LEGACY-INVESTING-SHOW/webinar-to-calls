Every exit spends the same equity, so price all four options before you pick one

Slide 1 · Every exit spends the same equity, so price all four options before you pick one

This is the last lesson in the STR Concierge track, and it's about the end of an investment, or at least a turning point.

You have four options for a property you own. You can hold it, refinance it, sell it, or sell it through a 1031 exchange. By the end of this lesson you'll be able to put those four side by side. You'll compare them on after-tax cash, time to complete, future debt, ongoing work, and opportunity cost.

You'll also learn how the gain on a sale is built, which parts depreciation touches, and which answers only your CPA can give. I'm not going to calculate your tax. I'm going to show you the structure so you can collect the right records and read your CPA's answer.

You'll leave with a decision worksheet you can hand to that CPA and to a qualified intermediary. That's the independent company that holds your sale money during a 1031 exchange. We'll use Cedar Ridge at the end of year 5.

Slide 2 · Each option answers one question: is this equity doing more here than elsewhere?

Every exit decision comes down to one question. Is the equity in this property doing more for you here than it would do somewhere else?

Equity is the value you could turn into cash. That's market value minus the loan balance, minus the costs of getting it out. Holding keeps that equity in the house. Refinancing pulls some of it out as a new loan. Selling turns it into cash, minus taxes. And a 1031 exchange moves it into another investment property and may defer the tax.

Each option mixes five things differently: after-tax cash, time, future debt, ongoing work, and opportunity cost. None of them wins on all five. You're choosing a trade-off you can live with.

You'll make this call more than once. Run it whenever your 90-day or annual review shows a real change. That's the review from Sam's lesson OP.10, the 30-Day and 90-Day Performance Review. A big capital repair, a new local rule, a life change, or equity that has outgrown the cash flow.

Slide 3 · Start with an honest equity number built from four documented inputs

Four inputs start every exit analysis.

The first is the updated value. Get it from a broker's opinion of value, recent comparable sales, or an appraisal. Don't use the price you'd like. Short-term rental houses sometimes sell with furniture and bookings, so ask the broker whether buyers in your market actually pay for that.

The second is the loan balance. Take it from the servicer's latest statement, not your own amortization schedule. The servicer is the company that collects your loan payments. If you plan to sell, request a payoff quote. It can include items the statement doesn't show.

The third is capital repairs coming in the next few years. A roof or HVAC replacement changes the hold case. It can also change the sale price if a buyer's inspector finds it.

The fourth is the cost of selling. The Cedar Ridge example later in this lesson uses 7% of value as an assumption. Your agent can give you a line-by-line net sheet.

The result is net proceeds before income tax. That's not your spendable number yet.

Slide 4 · Hold, refinance, and sell each fit a different situation; holding has a hidden cost

Holding is the default. It needs no transaction. But it has a cost you don't see on any statement.

Take Cedar Ridge, the example later in this lesson. It earns about $5,172 a year in cash flow after the mortgage, on about $142,560 of equity. That's roughly 3.6%. If you could sell and put the after-tax cash somewhere safer at a similar return, holding carries more risk for the same money. That's opportunity cost.

Holding makes sense when the property still covers its costs in a weak season, the capital repairs are funded, and the work is manageable. It also makes sense when a sale would trigger a big tax bill you'd rather defer. The property should still meet your PA.1 buy-box goals.

Refinancing keeps the property and pulls out cash. It doesn't trigger tax on a gain, but it adds debt. Refinance when you have a specific use for the cash and the property can carry the bigger loan in a weak year. That's the weak-season test: does it still pay every bill, mortgage included, in a bad year?

Selling ends the risk and the work. It also brings the tax consequences we'll look at next.

Depreciation reduces basis whether or not you claimed it (IRS Publication 527). Skipping it only loses the deduction. Bonus depreciation and section 179 also reduce basis.

Slide 5 · Gain is what you realize minus adjusted basis, and depreciation shrinks the basis

You don't need to calculate your own tax. You do need to know how the calculation is built, so you can collect the right records and read your CPA's answer. Every rule here depends on your facts and the tax year.

Gain equals amount realized minus adjusted basis. Amount realized is roughly the sale price minus selling costs. Adjusted basis starts with what you paid plus certain acquisition costs. It goes up by improvements and down by depreciation. IRS Publication 544 describes it that way.

Now the part that surprises people. Suppose you didn't claim all the depreciation you were entitled to. Publication 527 says you still reduce basis by the full amount you could have deducted. The IRS phrase is allowed or allowable. So skipping depreciation doesn't protect you at sale. It only means you lost the deduction.

Publication 527 also says bonus depreciation and any section 179 deduction reduce basis. Bonus depreciation means deducting a large share, or all, of an asset's cost in its first year, if it's eligible. Section 179 is another way to expense an asset up front.

Slide 6 · Your CPA splits the gain into three pieces, and cost segregation grows the ordinary one

Your CPA splits the gain. Here's the structure.

First, personal property. That includes furniture and many building parts a cost-segregation study moves to 5- or 7-year lives. It's generally section 1245 property. Publication 544 says gain on it is ordinary income to the extent of depreciation allowed or allowable. The ordinary part is the lesser of that depreciation or the gain on those assets. Say fully depreciated furniture is assigned $6,000 of the sale price. That's $6,000 of ordinary income. So it depends on how much of the price gets allocated to those items. That's a CPA and appraisal question.

Second, the building itself is section 1250 property. Gain tied to straight-line depreciation on it is taxed at a maximum 25% rate, per IRS Topic 409.

Third, anything left is section 1231 gain.

This is where Sam's cost-segregation lesson, PA.5, comes back. A study moves deductions earlier. At sale, more of the gain can land in the ordinary bucket. It can still be worth doing, and Cedar Ridge will show why. Resource SR.3 is how you have that conversation.

A 1031 exchange covers real property held for business or investment, not the furniture

  • Real property held primarily for sale doesn't qualify.
  • Furniture, equipment, and other personal property generally no longer qualify after 2017.
  • The gain is deferred, not erased. Ask your CPA how it affects the new property's basis and depreciation.

Rev. Proc. 2008-16 is a safe harbor for units with occasional personal use. Have your CPA check its section 4.02 day counts against the IRS text.

Slide 7 · A 1031 exchange covers real property held for business or investment, not the furniture

A like-kind exchange under section 1031 lets you sell one investment property and buy another. You defer the gain instead of paying tax on it now. Deferred means it waits. Ask your CPA how the deferred gain affects the replacement property's basis and future depreciation.

What qualifies? Since 2018, only real property held for use in a business or for investment. Both the property you sell and the one you buy have to be held that way. The IRS says property used primarily for personal use, like a primary residence, second home, or vacation home, doesn't qualify. And personal property generally no longer qualifies. So the furniture in your STR generally can't be part of the exchange.

Now the caveat for owners who also stay at the property. The IRS issued Revenue Procedure 2008-16, a safe harbor for a unit with occasional personal use. Summaries describe a holding period, a minimum number of fair-rental days, and a cap on personal use. I couldn't confirm the exact counts from the IRS text. Have your CPA check them before you rely on them.

Slide 8 · Five gates decide whether an exchange can work before you list the property

Here's the exchange as a set of gates. Any No stops it or sends you to your CPA.

Gate one: both properties held for business or investment. Gate two: your personal use either fits Rev. Proc. 2008-16 or your facts otherwise support investment use. Outside the safe harbor it depends on your facts, so don't assume.

Gate three is the qualified intermediary, the QI. In a deferred exchange the QI holds the sale proceeds. Your own agent can't serve. The IRS list includes your real estate agent or broker, accountant, attorney, and employee, and anyone in those roles in the past two years. The IRS also warns that some intermediaries have gone bankrupt or failed to perform. Ask how funds are held, what bonding or insurance exists, and who signs withdrawals.

Gates four and five are the clock. Identify in writing by day 45. Receive the replacement by day 180 or your return due date, whichever comes first. The IRS treats both as strict.
