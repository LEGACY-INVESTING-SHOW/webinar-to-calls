Set your walk-away price before you see theirs

Slide 1 · Set your walk-away price before you see theirs

This lesson is about one number: your walk-away price. You set it before you ever see the seller's number.

The asking price tells you what the seller hopes to get. It says nothing about what the house can carry as a rental. A common mistake is starting at $399,000 and nudging the nightly rate up until the spreadsheet works. So reverse the order. Decide the most you'll pay first, from numbers you'd defend to a lender. Then negotiate below it.

You'll build that number from the base-case and weak-case numbers you bring back from Sam's PA.4, your loan terms, and your cash. Then we'll cover the contract terms that protect it. We stay with Cedar Ridge and Maya and Chris, and every figure is a course example assumption. Asking is $399,000. They sign at $385,000.

Slide 2 · Your walk-away price is the lowest of three ceilings

Your walk-away price is the lowest of three ceilings.

Test one is base-case cash flow. In your expected year, the house must clear a minimum after the loan payment. Maya and Chris set $5,000 a year.

Test two is the weak year. Strict means the house breaks even. Funded means you carry a set shortfall, in writing, with the cash set aside. They have two salaries and savings, so they chose funded: up to $6,000 a year, with two years held in reserve.

Test three is cash. Down payment, closing costs, points, furnishing, lender reserves, the weak-year reserve, and a $51,000 emergency fund all have to fit inside their $231,000.

Run all three on the more expensive loan quote. For them that's the DSCR quote: 7.25%, 1 point, 25% down.

Slide 3 · Keeping $5,000 of cash flow caps the price at $394,310

Here's test one. Net operating income, or NOI, is revenue minus operating costs before the loan payment. Property tax and insurance already sit inside it. So cash flow is simply NOI minus principal and interest, which I'll call P&I.

Cedar Ridge's base-case NOI is $29,209. Keep $5,000, and the most P&I they can carry is $24,209 a year. That's about $2,017 a month.

At 7.25% over 30 years, that payment supports a loan of about $295,732. A loan calculator or the spreadsheet PV function does that step. The exercise in the lesson shows how.

With 25% down, the loan is 75% of the price. Divide by 0.75 and the ceiling is $394,310.

Test two, funded version. Weak-case NOI is $18,162. They'll carry up to $6,000 more, so the most P&I is $24,162. That supports a loan of about $295,158 and a ceiling of $393,544.

Test three, found by trying prices in a spreadsheet, lands at about $409,984.

The short bar is the strict version. Breakeven caps the price at $295,818, which is $103,182 under asking. Putting more of their own cash in doesn't fix it. At $385,000, a breakeven loan would take about $208,906 of cash, and they have $180,000 after the emergency fund. If you can't fund a weak year, this house is a pass.

Slide 6 · At $385,000 they keep $11,020 unallocated

Here's the cash plan at $385,000.

Cash to close plus furnishing is $142,688. Lender reserves are six months of the full payment, $15,342. That money sits in their account. It isn't spent. The weak-year reserve is two years of the $5,475 shortfall, $10,950. The emergency fund stays at $51,000.

Total committed is $219,980. That leaves $11,020 of their $231,000 unallocated.

They also wrote a buffer rule: after signing, any surprise has to leave at least $10,000 unallocated. That's why the walk-away price is the most they'll sign at, not the target. At $393,000 they'd keep only about $7,500. Hold on to that $10,000 buffer. We'll test it with a low appraisal and an inspection finding.

Slide 7 · Four terms decide how much cash you could lose

Price is one term. Eight others decide how much risk you carry between signing and closing. Contracts are state-specific, so take these to your agent and attorney as questions.

Four of them are about money. Earnest money is your deposit. It's credited toward cash to close, but a bigger deposit raises what you could lose if you miss a deadline. The financing contingency lets you cancel if the loan isn't approved by a date, so match it to your lender's real timeline. Lenders generally size the loan on the lower of price or appraisal, so only offer gap coverage you already counted in Test three. And seller credits have a lender limit. Get it in dollars before you ask the seller.

Slide 8 · Four terms decide whether you can test and launch

The other four are about time and scope.

The inspection period tests the business as well as the house: permits, association or deed rules, insurance, septic capacity. Make it long enough for an STR insurance quote and a written answer from the licensing office.

The closing date has to fit your rate lock and your launch. Closing in time to list before the lake season is worth more than a small price cut.

For furniture, check fit with your OP.6 plan, condition, and how your lender treats it. List what you want in the contract or on a bill of sale. Mark the rest excluded.

For existing bookings, ask whether they transfer, who keeps the payments, and whether the platform allows it.

Get written answers before you shorten a contingency

  • Inspection, septic, and insurance still fit?
  • Earnest money if I cancel late?
  • Permit answer before inspection ends?
  • Lender's real approval days?
  • Loan if appraisal is $10,000 low?
  • Rate lock expiry and extension cost?
Slide 9 · Get written answers before you shorten a contingency

Sellers sometimes ask for a shorter inspection period, no financing contingency, or appraisal gap coverage. Get written answers first.

Ask your agent whether the inspection, the septic check, and the insurance quote still fit. Ask what happens to your earnest money if you cancel late, what's normal in this market, and whether the permit answer arrives in time.

Ask your lender how many days it really needs, what happens if the appraisal is $10,000 low, and whether approval depends on permit eligibility. Ask for its seller credit limit and when the rate lock expires.

If any answer puts your earnest money at risk for a problem you can't test in time, keep the contingency.

Slide 10 · After a $375,000 appraisal, walk above $380,000

Two what-ifs on the $385,000 contract, both tested against the $10,000 buffer.

First, the appraisal comes in at $375,000. The lender lends 75% of the lower value, $281,250. That's $7,500 less loan, so $7,500 more cash at the full price.

Paying the full gap leaves $5,130 unallocated. That breaks the rule. Splitting it at $380,000 leaves $10,280. If the seller drops to $375,000, they keep $15,430.

So they renegotiate. Ask for $375,000 and accept up to $380,000. If the seller holds at $385,000 and the appraisal contingency is still open, walk. If it has expired, the earnest money may be at risk, so ask your attorney before you walk.

Slide 11 · Absorbing $4,500 of repairs breaks the buffer, so renegotiate

Second, the inspection finds $4,500 of work. Say the water heater is at the end of its life and some deck boards are soft.

If they absorb it, total committed rises to $224,480 and unallocated cash falls to $6,520. That's under the buffer, so they renegotiate. Ask the seller to repair before closing or cut the price. Ask your lender first whether a seller credit can go toward repairs. Guest safety items get fixed before launch either way, and PA.12 covers that.

A different finding changes the answer. A septic system sized for fewer guests than you planned can break the business plan. That's a walk unless the fix and the permit both work on paper.

Slide 14 · Set your walk-away price before any offer

Three things this week.

Write your ceiling rules before you look at another listing: minimum base-case cash flow, your weak-case rule, and your emergency fund. If you set them after, you'll set them to fit the house.

Run the three ceilings on your more expensive quote, using the worksheet in the lesson. Write your walk-away price, rounded down.

Start your decision log. Write your buffer first, then your first offer and your appraisal and inspection triggers. Send your agent and lender the contingency questions and keep their answers in writing.
