Slide 1 · The down payment is the smallest question; fund the full stack before you shop

This lesson answers one question before you look at a single listing. Can you actually afford to own?

By the end, you'll be able to add up the full cash an ownership deal needs, not just the down payment. You'll compare it with the money you really have, after you keep your own emergency savings. You'll test whether you'd survive a delayed opening or a weak first season. And you'll finish with a one-page readiness scorecard.

That scorecard sends you to one of four places. PA.1, the next lesson, where you write your buy box. Better financing or liquidity first. The Part One lease path, where you rent a place and host it instead of buying. Or wait.

I'll run one example couple, Maya and Chris, through all of it. You'll meet them on the next slide. On the surface they look very ready. You'll see where that changes. Every Cedar Ridge figure is an assumption from the course example file, and new ones are marked.

Hearing "not yet" here is much cheaper than hearing it at a closing table.

Before we do any math, meet the people and the house. Everything here is hypothetical, from the course example file.

Maya and Chris are a two-earner household. They make $185,000 combined, and their credit scores are around 740. They have $210,000 in liquid savings outside retirement. They also own their home, worth $520,000 with a $260,000 mortgage.

They're buying Cedar Ridge, a three-bedroom house in a lake market about two hours from a city. The price is $385,000 with 25% down. It's a short-term rental they'll also use about 10 days a year. It isn't their main home.

They have two loan quotes. A DSCR loan, short for debt service coverage ratio, qualifies mainly on the property's income against its payment. A conventional loan qualifies on their household income and debts. The DSCR quote carries one point. A point is an upfront fee of 1% of the loan, paid for a lower rate.

In the example, the house books about 212 nights a year across 61 stays, at $300 a night on average.

Slide 3 · A $96,250 down payment against $210,000 saved looks ready twice over

Most buyers start with one number: the down payment. For Cedar Ridge, 25% of $385,000 is $96,250. Maya and Chris have $210,000 in liquid savings outside retirement accounts. On that math alone, they could make the down payment twice.

That math leaves out most of the bill.

An owned short-term rental needs money for closing, furnishing, repairs, and permits. It needs money for the months before the first guest arrives. It needs reserves, for the lender and for you. And your own household has to stay safe if the house has a bad year.

So in this lesson I lay out every layer, one at a time. Then we run Maya and Chris through it and see what's left.

Keep the $210,000 in your head. We'll come back to it, and by the end the answer won't be "twice over". It'll be a gap.

Slide 4 · The cash stack has eight layers: six you spend and two you must still hold

Think of the cash requirement as a stack of eight layers. The order doesn't change the total. You need all of them funded. If you don't, you're counting on the house to perform from day one.

Layers 1 to 6 are money you'll spend. First come the down payment, closing costs and points, and furnishing. Then the repair and safety punch list, permits and inspections, and the carrying costs before the first booking. That last one catches people. The loan payment starts whether or not a guest has booked.

Layers 7 and 8 are money you must still have after spending all that. Layer 7 is the property reserve. The lender may require it to make the loan, and after closing it becomes your cushion for the house. Layer 8 is your household's own emergency savings.

A common failure is merging 7 and 8. A buyer counts the same dollars twice, once for the house and once for the family. Then the water heater fails in the same month a paycheck is late. And there's one pile of cash for two problems.

Where the money comes from

Count only liquid savings; retirement, HELOC, gift, and seller money have conditions

Counts in this lesson

Checking, savings, and taxable brokerage accounts.
  • The cleanest source. Lenders usually want to see where it came from and how long it's been there.
  • Ask your lender what documentation they need for large recent deposits.

Borrowed or conditional

Ask your lender and CPA before you count any of these.
  • Retirement: some lenders may count part toward reserves; withdrawals can carry tax and penalties.
  • HELOC: usually a variable rate, and your home is the collateral (CFPB).
  • Gifts or partners: lender rules apply; ownership and loan papers must match (PA.10).
  • Seller concessions: a negotiation item for PA.8 (offers), not money to count now.
0.3 · Capital & Readiness Checklist§ 0.3.35 / 21
Slide 5 · Count only liquid savings; retirement, HELOC, gift, and seller money have conditions

Money for the stack can come from several places. Each one has limits, and your lender may treat them differently. In this lesson, we count only liquid savings.

Liquid savings are the cleanest. That's checking, savings, and taxable brokerage. Lenders usually want to see where it came from and how long it's been there.

Retirement accounts are different. Some lenders may count part of a retirement balance toward reserves. But withdrawals can carry taxes and penalties. Ask your lender and CPA.

Home equity looks tempting. Maya and Chris have $260,000 of equity on paper. A HELOC lets them borrow against it. The CFPB notes that HELOCs usually carry a variable rate, and your home is the collateral. A HELOC is borrowed money, not savings, and it adds a payment. OP.11 covers HELOCs for scaling.

Gifts and partner money follow lender rules, and the ownership paperwork has to match (PA.10). A seller may pay part of closing costs, but that's a negotiation later (PA.8), not money to count now.

If the stack only works with borrowed money, write that down as a finding.

Reserves

The lender's reserve rule is a floor to get the loan, not a plan for a bad season

  • Fannie Mae measures reserves in months of the new loan's PITIA (Selling Guide B3-4.1-01).
  • No minimum for a one-unit principal residence. Cedar Ridge isn't their main home, so that rule doesn't apply.
  • Owning other financed properties can add more. For Maya and Chris, likely zero; their lender confirms.
  • DSCR lenders set their own rule. Get each lender's number in writing (PA.6, PA.7).
For teaching: 6 months of PITIA (assumption)

DSCR quote: 6 x $2,557 = $15,342. Conventional quote: 6 x $2,484 = $14,904.

0.3 · Capital & Readiness Checklist§ 0.3.46 / 21
Slide 6 · The lender's reserve rule is a floor to get the loan, not a plan for a bad season

Lenders measure reserves in months of the new loan's payment. That payment is principal, interest, taxes, insurance, and association dues. People shorten it to PITIA.

Fannie Mae's Selling Guide lays out the rules for loans it buys. There's no minimum for a one-unit principal residence. Second homes and investment properties do have requirements. Cedar Ridge isn't Maya and Chris's main home, so the no-minimum rule doesn't apply. Their lender decides which category it falls in.

If you already own other financed properties besides your home, lenders can require extra reserves. For Maya and Chris that add-on is likely zero. Their lender confirms it.

The DSCR quote isn't a Fannie Mae loan. Each DSCR lender sets its own rule. For teaching I assume 6 months: $15,342 on the DSCR quote, $14,904 on the conventional.

After closing, the lender stops watching. That reserve becomes your cushion. The lender's number is a floor, not a plan.

Besides cash, three things shape whether a lender will lend: credit, loan type, and debt capacity.

Credit first. Maya and Chris have scores around 740. Each lender sets its own minimum and prices its rate by score. Kiavi's DSCR guide, for example, says it looks at credit score and down payment, not only coverage. That's one lender, not a market standard.

Then the loan type. Fannie Mae's Selling Guide has a section on short-term rental income from the property being bought. Whether a lender counts that income for you is their call. PA.7 compares the paths.

Then debt capacity. Monthly income is $15,417. Their home payment and other debts are assumptions, $2,400 and $600. Add the $2,484 conventional PITIA, and debts total $5,484. A DSCR lender checks a coverage ratio instead: property income over PITIA. In the course example it runs from 1.18 to 2.42, depending on the lender's definition. PA.6 walks through it.

Slide 8 · Each month between closing and the first guest costs $3,257 with nothing coming in

Two things go wrong often enough that you should budget for both. The first is a delayed opening.

Permits take longer than expected. Furniture arrives late. A contractor misses a week. Every month between closing and the first guest costs the full carrying cost, with no revenue.

For Cedar Ridge on the DSCR quote, that's $3,257 a month. It's the $2,557 PITIA, plus the example's $450 of utilities, $100 of software, and $150 for the handyman retainer.

The base stack already includes two months of carrying costs as an assumption. That's $6,514. Now suppose you get three more months of delay on top of that. That's another $9,771.

Where does that money come from? Not from the house. The house isn't earning yet. It comes from the property reserve, and if that's not enough, from your household.

When you build your own stack, don't use the fastest timeline anyone's quoted you. Ask the permit office for real processing times. Ask the furniture vendor for realistic delivery dates.

Slide 10 · Self-managing Cedar Ridge takes about 140 hours a year, and the cleaner may log more

Cash isn't the only resource. Owning also takes time, and a stomach for ups and downs.

In the base case, Maya and Chris self-manage with a local cleaner and an on-call handyman. Assume 1.5 hours per stay on messages, scheduling, and turnover checks. With the example's 61 stays, that's 91.5 hours. Add 4 hours a month on bookkeeping, pricing, and vendor calls. That's 48 more. Together, about 140 hours a year, or 2.7 hours a week.

That hour count matters for taxes too. A rental can show a paper loss. Whether that loss is usable against your other income can depend on material participation. That's a set of IRS tests about how involved you are, and 0.2 covers it. Publication 925's tests compare your hours with other people's, like the cleaner's. At 4 hours on each of 61 turnovers, the cleaner logs 244 hours. That's more than the owners' 140. Your CPA confirms how your hours count.

The alternative is a co-host. In 0.2, one charging 18% of booking revenue sank the base case to minus $7,798.

Slide 11 · Ten personal days stay under the IRS limit; the harder test is living with -$27,503

Two more resources: your own calendar, and your nerves.

Personal use first. Maya and Chris plan about 10 personal days a year. IRS Publication 527 treats a dwelling as used as a home if personal use passes a threshold. The threshold is the greater of 14 days or 10% of fair-rental days. It also requires dividing expenses by days. Counting 212 rented nights as fair-rental days, the threshold is 21.2 days. So 10 days is below it, and the CPA confirms the count.

There's also a revenue cost. Those 10 nights at the $300 base rate are worth up to $3,000. So plan your own stays for slower weeks.

Now risk tolerance. In 0.2, the five-year test added five years of cash flow to the sale proceeds. Then it compared that total with the $142,688 they put in. The base case came out $27,732 ahead before tax. The weak case came out $27,503 behind. I want both owners to look at that second number and say out loud whether they could live with it.

If the honest answer is no, that's a readiness result, not a failure.

Slide 14 · Close the gap with time first, then financing, price, and a phased furnishing plan

So what can close the gap? Here are the options in the order I'd look at them.

Time first. If Maya and Chris save $3,500 a month, which is an assumption, the DSCR gap closes in about 3.3 months. The conventional gap closes in about 2.3 months. Searching, underwriting, and closing take a while anyway, so time is often the cheapest fix.

Financing second. The conventional quote needs $2,888 less at closing, because it has no point. The payment is lower too, if they qualify. That shrinks the gap to $8,172. It doesn't close it. PA.7 compares the two loans.

Price third. A lower price reduces the down payment and closing costs. In PA.1 we turn this into a hard price cap.

Furnishing fourth. A phased furnishing plan may cut the up-front budget. OP.6 covers how to do that without cutting safety or listing accuracy.

And two things they should not do. Don't shrink the emergency fund to make the table balance. And don't count a HELOC as savings. Both of those make the table look fine and leave the household exposed.

Slide 19 · Your lender, CPA, insurer, and permit office each confirm a line of your stack

Here's who confirms what for your stack.

Each lender, in writing: the reserve months and how they calculate them, the minimum credit score, and the down payment. Also which sources of funds they accept, like HELOC money, gifts, or retirement assets. And how they count rental income. Ask every lender the same questions so you can compare the answers side by side.

Your CPA: the tax effect of any retirement withdrawal, how your hours count for material participation, and how personal-use days are treated.

Your insurance agent: the premium and when it's due. Depending on timing, it lands in closing costs or in launch carrying costs. Either way, it needs to be a real number.

The local permit office: the fees and the processing time. That's what tells you whether two months of launch carrying costs is realistic, or whether you need to plan for more.

Every one of these turns an assumption on your stack into a confirmed number. The more lines you confirm, the more you can trust your scorecard.
