Get a clear yes or no, in writing

Slide 1 · Get a clear yes or no, in writing

This lesson covers the part of the landlord call that often decides the answer: the owner's worries about legality and liability.

You're after one of two outcomes. Written approval with a signed addendum, which is a written add-on to the lease. Or a clear pass that you log and move on from. Both are fine results.

You'll leave with a 60-second opening, an evidence packet for regulation and liability objections, and an accurate way to explain how your insurance differs from a platform's protection program. That last one is where operators most often say something inaccurate, so we'll spend real time on it. We'll run it all through Alder Street, with a role play against a fictional landlord named Dana.

Build the pitch on facts the owner can check

  • Who runs the unit and who stays
  • How it's cleaned and maintained
  • Who answers at 2 a.m.

Slide 2 · Build the pitch on facts the owner can check

Sam's 5.6 lesson showed you how to find the owner. Bring back the owner's name and a working phone number. Lessons 5.1 and 5.2 cover the basic script. This lesson handles legality and liability.

Set your goal before you dial: a clear yes or a clear no, in writing. You aren't trying to talk a reluctant owner into something they don't understand. An owner who signs without knowing what they agreed to panics after the first neighbor complaint, and that costs you the lease and your setup money.

So the pitch rests on facts the owner can check. If you can show each of these on paper, most objections turn into questions with answers. The address should already have cleared the hard stops in 3.4. If the city bans your use, there's nothing to pitch.

Slide 3 · Cover four points in the first 60 seconds

Owners decide in the first minute whether you sound like a business or a risk. So cover four points, in this order, in plain words.

Who operates: your name, your company, and that you'd be the tenant on the lease. Who stays: paying guests for a few nights, booked through named platforms, with the expected volume. How the unit is maintained: professional cleaning after every stay, a local contact for repairs, and scheduled inspections. Why it can help the owner: the asking rent, a professional tenant, a clean furnished unit, and problems reported fast.

Be careful on that last point. Don't promise things you can't control. "Zero wear and tear" and "no complaints ever" are promises you'll break.

Slide 4 · The Alder Street opening ends by asking for questions

Here's the Alder Street version, something like this: "Thanks for taking the call. I run a small furnished-rental business, and I'd like to lease Unit 4B myself at your asking rent of $1,950 on a 24-month term. I'd host guests for short stays, usually around three nights, through Airbnb and a couple of other channels. That's roughly one or two check-ins a week. A professional cleaner turns the unit after every stay, and I'll have a local person on call. I'll carry my own short-term-rental liability policy, apply for the city permit, and put all of it in a written addendum you'd review. What questions do you have?"

That last line invites objections now, while your evidence is ready. If this is your first unit, say so plainly.

Slide 5 · Bring the paper before the owner asks for it

Owners respond to paper more than to reassurance. Your evidence packet is a folder of seven documents.

Permit status comes from your 3.4 scorecard and SR.1 research, with official links. The declarations page is the summary page of your policy: who's covered, the limits, the dates. The draft addendum carries the ten topics from 3.5, reviewed by your attorney. Then an operating summary, house rules, a local contact sheet with a 24-hour number, and a short complaint and damage process.

Missing something? Say so and give a date. "The insurer quoted $1,300 a year; I'll send the declarations page when the policy binds, before the first guest." That's honest and specific.

Slide 6 · Answer each worry, then put the answer in the addendum

Every objection has a real worry behind it. Answer it with evidence, then write the answer into the addendum so it outlasts the phone call.

Legality gets the ordinance link, the permit category, the date you checked, and the licensing office's written answer. Parties get house rules and a guest cap. Damage gets your declarations page, your damage process, and the lease deposit. Noise gets quiet hours and a response time. Vacancy gets your stress case and cash reserve. A worry about guest injury gets your policy, with the owner as an additional insured if the insurer allows.

The last row is honest about limits. You can't answer for the owner's insurer or mortgage. Offer to wait while they check. If the answer is no, log the pass.

Airbnb says its host damage protection isn't insurance

  • Damage requests within 14 days of checkout
  • Wear and tear isn't covered
  • Liability part has its own exclusions
  • Liability part covers Airbnb bookings only

Tell the owner you rely on your own policy.

Slide 7 · Airbnb says its host damage protection isn't insurance

This is where operators most often say something inaccurate. Get it right, because the owner may repeat it to their own insurer.

Airbnb's AirCover for hosts has two parts. Airbnb says Host damage protection isn't an insurance policy. You file within 14 days of checkout, wear and tear isn't covered, and Airbnb can deny a request if you didn't take reasonable care. Host liability insurance is real insurance, with exclusions, and it treats a host's landlord as insured only narrowly. It covers Airbnb bookings, not Vrbo or your direct site.

So I'd say: "I carry my own short-term-rental liability policy. Here's the declarations page. The platform program only applies to Airbnb bookings, so I don't rely on it." Ask your broker how the two interact, and check Airbnb's current terms before you pitch.

Slide 8 · Alder Street means $46,800 of rent and 1.6 check-ins a week

Now the Alder Street role play. I'm assuming the city allows unhosted stays with an annual permit that needs the owner's signed consent. You've cleared the hard stops in 3.4 and drafted the addendum. The landlord owns the four-unit building and agreed to a call.

The course's standard example numbers: $1,950 a month on a 24-month lease, sleeps 5, about 85 stays a year. Insurance is $1,300 a year and the permit is $250 a year. All assumptions for teaching.

Before the call, run two numbers. Rent over the term is $46,800. That's what you're committing to pay the owner. And 85 stays over 52 weeks is about 1.6 check-ins a week. That tells the owner how busy the hallway will be.

Slide 9 · Answer Dana with a document or a clause

Dana is a fictional landlord. You deliver the opening, and Dana starts asking.

On legality, you don't argue. You show the ordinance link, the permit page, the date you checked, and the licensing office's written reply. The $250 permit is yours to pay. On the permit itself, you don't have it yet, because the application needs Dana's signed consent. That's clause 4. No guest checks in before the permit is issued and your policy is bound.

Insurance gets the $1,300 quote and proof at every renewal. Damage gets about 85 cleans a year and a monthly inspection. Then Dana says, "Put all of that in writing." It already is. You hand over the addendum and a one-page summary, and ask Dana to check with their own insurer and lender.

Slide 10 · A signed addendum and a logged pass are both good endings

From there, the call ends one of two ways.

In the first, Dana's insurer and lender both say short stays are fine. Dana's attorney asks that the 30-day cure period start only after written notice by email and by mail. You accept, and Dana signs. On your 3.4 scorecard, lease risks L1 and L2 drop to zero.

In the second, Dana's insurer says the building policy excludes short-stay use, and Dana declines. You thank Dana, ask whether a standard long-term lease interests them, and log the pass. You don't argue with the insurer, and you never suggest listing anyway.

Either ending is a good result. The bad one is "let me think about it" with nothing written, or a yes Dana doesn't fully understand.

Close every call with a same-day email

  • Signed documents and their dates
  • What you still owe, with dates
  • No guests before permit and policy
  • For a pass: log reason and date
Slide 11 · Close every call with a same-day email

A yes on the phone isn't approval. Close every conversation with an email the same day.

For approval, list the signed documents and their dates. Then list what you still owe, like the declarations page, the permit number, and the local contact, with a date next to each. And state the condition plainly: no guest checks in before the permit is issued and the policy is bound.

For a pass, thank the owner and state their reason in neutral words. Ask whether you can check back if things change. Then log it: date, address, owner, outcome, reason given, and a follow-up date. If three owners in a row cite their building insurer, that's a market-level fact worth knowing before your next pitch.

Fill in the prep sheet before you call

  • Address and 3.4 label
  • Ordinance link and licensing office reply
  • Permit cost and owner consent
  • Insurance quote and channels covered
  • Draft addendum, attorney reviewed
  • Your opening and top objections
Slide 12 · Fill in the prep sheet before you call

Your exercise is the prep sheet. Fill it in for one real address before you call. No address yet? Use Alder Street, then redo it for a real one.

Start with the address and its 3.4 label. Then the regulation evidence: the ordinance link with the date you checked, and the licensing office's written answer. Add the permit category, cost, and whether the owner has to consent.

For insurance, note the limits, premium, and channels covered, and find out whether the landlord can be an additional insured. Then the addendum, the operating summary, house rules, and your local contact. Write out your opening, your top three objections, and the one you can't answer. Then practice the opening out loud until it runs under 60 seconds.

Slide 13 · Prepare one landlord call this week

Three things this week.

Write your 60-second opening for one real address, then say it out loud with a timer. Cut anything that sounds like a promise you can't keep.

Email your broker two questions: which channels does my quote cover, and can the landlord be named as an additional insured? Get the answers in writing.

Build the evidence packet as a folder, all seven documents, with a date on anything missing. And set up your pass log before you need it.

A signed addendum promises the owner a clean unit after every stay and a local person who answers. Module 6 is where you build that team, starting with 6.1, Cleaners. Bring your local contact commitments. They set the standard your cleaner has to meet.
