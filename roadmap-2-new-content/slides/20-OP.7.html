Slide 2 · List the jobs every stay needs before you choose a job title

"Should I hire a co-host or a property manager?" is the wrong first question. Those titles cover very different scopes, and the scopes overlap. One co-host does messaging only. Another does everything but cleaning. You can't compare them until you know the jobs.

So start with the list of jobs every stay needs. I group them by when they happen. Some happen every stay. Some happen on demand. Some happen at any hour. Some run on a schedule, like the filters, the alarms, the grill, the deck, and the water heater.

Then look at the last group. Pricing approval, compliance decisions, the bank and platform accounts, and the performance review stay with the owner.

You can hand off a lot of work. You can't hand off responsibility for it. The owner keeps title to the house, the loan, and the platform account. When the city, the lender, or the insurer has a question, they ask the owner.

Once you have the job list, write one name beside each job. Then write a second name.

That's the backup rule. Every role that touches a guest stay needs a backup who has already been trained and given access. The access part matters. If your cleaner gets sick at 7 a.m., you don't want to be adding the backup to the calendar and texting them a door code while a guest is on the road.

Decide five kinds of access separately, and give each person the least they need

  1. Platform access: see the calendar, message guests, or change prices and the listing
  2. Spending authority: how much, on what, without asking
  3. Guest data: names, phone numbers, messages, and whether they store any of it
  4. Keys and codes: physical keys, lock codes, the owner closet code, the Wi-Fi admin login
  5. After-hours responsibility: who answers at 2 a.m., and how fast they must get there
The common mistake

Full platform access for someone who only needed a calendar. Access given for convenience is hard to take back later.

OP.7 · Building Your Ops Team§ OP.7.34 / 19
Slide 4 · Decide five kinds of access separately, and give each person the least they need

Every person on the team needs some access. The rule is to give each one the least access that still lets them do the job.

There are five kinds of access, and they aren't the same thing. Someone can have a lock code and no platform access. Someone can message guests and have no spending authority. Decide each one separately.

The mistake most owners make is full platform access for someone who only needed the calendar. It feels easier at the start. Then it's hard to take back without friction, because it looks like you've stopped trusting them.

Guest data deserves a moment. Names, phone numbers, and messages should stay in the platform or the PMS. Sam's OP.4 lesson on tech stack architecture shows where messages and tasks live. Bring back from it where each team member logs in, so you can set access there instead of sharing passwords.

And after-hours responsibility is a real assignment. Somebody's phone is on at 2 a.m., and you need to know whose.

Slide 5 · Airbnb's co-host levels let a cleaner see the calendar without touching money

Airbnb lets a listing owner add co-hosts at different permission levels. Its co-host page describes three.

Full access lets someone manage the listing and the calendar, message guests, and view payouts and transaction history. They can also manage other co-hosts and their permissions, and submit reimbursement requests for you. That's a lot. It fits a full-service co-host or manager you've vetted and signed an agreement with.

Calendar and messaging access lets someone view the calendar, without editing it, and message guests. That fits a messaging co-host.

Calendar access lets someone see the calendar and the check-in and checkout details. These co-hosts don't show on the listing. That fits a cleaner or a handyman who only needs the turnover schedule.

One caution. I pulled these descriptions from search summaries of Airbnb's help pages. Check the exact labels in your own account before you rely on them.

For Cedar Ridge, the cleaner gets calendar access. Nobody gets full access without a written agreement.

OP.7 · Building Your Ops Team§ OP.7.46 / 19
Slide 6 · The owner stays responsible for every co-host, and co-hosts get paid first

A few more points from Airbnb's help pages matter for owners.

First, you're responsible for your co-hosts. That includes co-hosts that a full-access co-host added on their own. Airbnb's co-host page tells owners to choose them with care.

Second, only the listing owner can set up co-host payouts. You can set them up for a co-host at any permission level.

Third, payment order. When your earnings on a booking are bigger than the co-host payout, the co-host gets paid first. That's convenient for them. It also means a wrong percentage leaves your account before you see it. So check the payout math monthly.

Fourth, co-hosts can't see or change your payout method or your taxpayer information. That stays with you.

And the Co-Host Additional Terms say you stay responsible for what a co-host did before you removed them. Removing someone doesn't erase what they already did on your listing.

Slide 7 · A short written agreement answers the questions you'd otherwise argue about later

A handshake works until the first damaged sofa. So every role that touches the house or guests gets a short written agreement. It doesn't need to be long. It needs to answer the questions you'd otherwise argue about.

Scope comes first. List the jobs by name from the job list. Anything not listed is extra or not included.

The fee base catches people. A percentage of what? Nightly revenue only? Nightly revenue plus cleaning fees? Payout after platform fees? Those give different numbers, and I'll show you how different in the Cedar Ridge math.

The quality standard is the OP.8 checklist, with photos. Insurance means general liability for anyone working in the house. Workers' compensation and auto coverage may apply, depending on the state and how the vendor is set up.

Keys and codes are individual. No shared master code. When someone leaves, their code goes with them.

And termination covers the handover: keys, platform removal, future bookings, and records.

Slide 8 · Vet before you sign, and ask whether a paid co-host needs a license in your state

Vetting runs before the agreement, not after.

Ask for two references from other short-term rental owners. Friends don't count. Ask for proof of insurance and look at it. Ask the cleaner to do one paid trial turnover to the OP.8 standard before the first guest ever arrives. You'll learn more from that trial than from any interview.

For a co-host, ask how many properties they handle. Then ask who covers when two emergencies happen at the same time. The answer tells you whether there's a real backup or just one busy person.

Then the licensing question. Some states and cities require a real estate or property management license for anyone who manages a rental for someone else for pay. That can apply to a co-host or a manager. I can't tell you the rule for your state. An attorney or the state licensing board can, so ask before you sign.

OP.7 · Building Your Ops Team§ OP.7.69 / 19
Slide 9 · A task ledger with one payer per job stops you paying twice for the same work

There are three broad setups for an owned house.

A vendor set means you hire each role yourself and coordinate them. You do the guest messaging and the approvals. It costs the least in fees and the most in your time.

A co-host usually takes on some or all of messaging, coordination, restocking, and local response. Cleaning and repairs are often still billed separately. Scope varies a lot, which is why the agreement matters.

A full manager usually takes the listing, pricing, messaging, coordination, and owner statements. Many use their own cleaners and vendors, and some add markups.

Paying twice happens quietly. You add a co-host whose scope includes monthly inspections, and you keep paying the handyman's inspection retainer. That's the example we'll price at Cedar Ridge.

The fix is a task ledger. Write every job in one column. Beside each job, name exactly one payer. If two names show up, cut one or rewrite a scope.

This is the number I'd work out before talking to any co-host. How big a percentage fee can the base case carry before cash flow hits zero?

Slide 15 · Run your own cost check: find your break-even fee before you read any proposal

The exercise has three parts. Part A is the task ledger. List every task, from turnover cleaning to pricing. Beside each, write who does it, who pays, and how they're paid. If a task has two payers, fix it before you sign anything.

Part B is the access matrix. One row per person, including backups. Write their platform level, spending limit, guest data, keys and codes, after-hours duty, and backup name.

Part C is on the slide. It's the cost check, and it uses your own underwriting from Sam's PA.4 package.

Work down the lines in order. Get your cash flow with a vendor set. Divide it by gross to get your break-even fee. Only then look at the proposal. Put in the fee, the base it's charged on, and the dollar amount. Take out any duplicate costs. Then work out the cost per owner hour.

At the end, you'll have one number to compare any quote against.

Slide 16 · Four decision rules settle the team before the calendar opens

These four rules turn the worksheet into a decision.

First, compare the proposed fee with your break-even fee. If it's higher, the base case goes negative. You can still go ahead. But only if you've decided to fund the gap from other income, and you've written that down. I want that decision on paper, not discovered in month four.

Second, check the weak case. If it goes negative by more than your reserves can cover for a year, reduce the scope or change the fee base. At Cedar Ridge, the 10% messaging co-host in the weak case is negative $11,208. That's a real number to hold against the reserve.

Third, every role needs a trained backup. If one doesn't have it, the calendar doesn't open.

Fourth, check access. If anyone has more platform access than their row requires, lower it now. It's easier on day one than after six months.

Pass all four, and the team is ready for its instructions.

Slide 19 · Airbnb's co-host pages set the permission and payout rules used here

The platform rules in this lesson come from four Airbnb pages. I checked them on 2026-09-25 through search summaries, because direct fetches were blocked. That's why I keep telling you to confirm the permission labels in your own account.

The co-host page, article 1534, covers the permission levels and the owner's responsibility for co-hosts. The resource center article gives background on how the levels were introduced. The payouts page, article 3389, covers who sets up payouts and the rule that co-hosts are paid first. The Co-Host Additional Terms, article 3264, cover your responsibility for what a co-host did before you removed them.

The Cedar Ridge numbers come from the canonical example file, sections B1 through B4. The new scenarios were computed for this lesson and marked as assumptions. That covers the 10% messaging co-host, the fee-base variant, the flat service, the break-even fee, and the owner hours.

Licensing rules aren't sourced here, because they depend on your state. That's the attorney's call.
