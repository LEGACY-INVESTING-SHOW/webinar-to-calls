A lender's ratio isn't your cash flow

Slide 1 · A lender's ratio isn't your cash flow

This lesson is about reading a DSCR loan quote the way the lender reads it. DSCR stands for debt service coverage ratio.

By the end you'll know which income the lender puts on top of the ratio and which payment goes underneath. You'll see why a ratio well above 1.0 can still leave you losing money. And you'll leave with a question list for two lenders and a way to compare their written offers on the same house.

We'll use Cedar Ridge the whole way through. Maya and Chris are buying it for $385,000 with 25% down. Every Cedar Ridge number is an assumption from our course example. Your job is to get your own quote in writing.

Slide 2 · The lender swaps your pay stubs for rent, but still checks you

The title of this lesson is shorthand. A DSCR lender sizes the loan mostly on the property's rental income, not your pay stubs.

But the lender still looks at you. Kiavi is a lender that publishes its DSCR terms online. Its guide says it considers your credit score, your down payment, and the property's coverage ratio. I use Kiavi's pages as examples because they're public. That isn't an endorsement.

Your down payment sets your loan-to-value, or LTV, the loan as a percent of the price. Put 25% down and your LTV is 75%.

So the property's income replaces your salary in the math. It doesn't mean nobody asks about you. Other lenders publish different terms, and terms change. Get your own quote in writing.

Slide 3 · Three lender choices decide your ratio

DSCR is how many dollars of property income the lender counts for every dollar of loan payment. Income on top, payment underneath.

Every lender writes its own definition. Kiavi describes a common one: gross rent divided by PITIA. That's principal, interest, property taxes, insurance, and any HOA dues.

Three choices move the number. Which income: gross bookings, a discounted version, the appraiser's market rent, or net operating income. Whose estimate: your pro forma, the appraiser, a data report, or booking history. And which payment: principal and interest only, or full PITIA.

Kiavi says a ratio of at least 1.1 is needed to prequalify and calls 1.25 or higher strong. Your lender's minimum may differ. Ask for the definition in writing before you compare any two quotes.

Slide 4 · The payment underneath is $2,557 a month

Let's put numbers under the ratio. The hypothetical DSCR quote on Cedar Ridge is 7.25%, 1 point, 30-year fixed.

The loan is 75% of $385,000, or $288,750. Monthly principal and interest is $1,970. Any mortgage calculator gives you that from the loan, rate, and term. Property tax is $3,850 a year and STR insurance is $3,200. Together that's $7,050 a year, or $587.50 a month. There are no HOA dues.

So monthly PITIA is $2,557. Times 12, that's $30,684 a year.

Hold on to that $30,684. It's the bottom of the ratio for all three versions on the next slide. The only thing that changes is what the lender puts on top.

Slide 5 · The same house scores 2.42, 1.94, or 1.18

Now three different incomes on top of the same $30,684.

Ratio one uses gross booking revenue: $74,275. That's 212 booked nights at a $300 average nightly rate (ADR), plus $10,675 of cleaning fees. That gives 2.42.

Ratio two haircuts revenue by 20%. The 20% is my assumption, not any lender's rule. That's $59,420, and 1.94.

Ratio three uses net operating income, or NOI, which is revenue after operating costs. Operating costs are $45,066, so NOI is $29,209. Tax and insurance are already inside those costs and inside PITIA, so I add them back. That's $36,259, and 1.18.

Same house, same loan, same year. The lender's written definition decides which number counts.

Slide 6 · In a weak year, the NOI ratio drops to 0.82

Now the weak case. The course example drops ADR from $300 to $265 and occupancy from about 58% to 50%. Gross revenue falls to $57,330, and NOI falls to $18,162.

On the gross definition, the weak case still shows 1.87. On the haircut version, 1.49. On the NOI version, 0.82. That last one means the income the lender counts doesn't cover the payment.

So one weak year can look strong to one lender and fail with another. That's why I keep saying: get the definition in writing. Next, you'll see how a high gross number can hide a loss for you as the owner.

Slide 7 · A 1.87 ratio can still lose $456 a month

Look at the weak case on the gross definition: 1.87. It sounds like the house brings in almost twice its payment.

Now look at what Maya and Chris keep. Weak-case NOI is $18,162. Principal and interest is $23,637 a year. Tax and insurance are already out of NOI. So cash flow is minus $5,475 a year, about minus $456 a month. Even the base case is thin, $5,572 a year. Both are pre-diligence figures; PA.9 adjusts them.

A gross ratio never sees the $45,066 of operating costs. The NOI ratio does, so it drops below 1.0 exactly when you lose money.

So use the lender's ratio for one question: will they lend? Use your own NOI and cash flow from PA.4 for the other: should you buy?

Slide 8 · A 1.20 minimum cuts the loan to about $282,989

The definition does more than pass or fail you. It can shrink the loan.

Say a lender uses the NOI definition with a 1.20 minimum. That minimum is my assumption. $36,259 divided by 1.20 is $30,216 of PITIA. Take out $7,050 of tax and insurance, and it allows $23,166 a year of principal and interest. At 7.25% over 30 years, that supports about $282,989.

Maya and Chris asked for $288,750. They'd bring about $5,761 more cash to closing.

Now take the 80% gross definition with a 1.25 minimum. It supports roughly $494,600, more than the house is worth. If that lender caps LTV at 75%, the cap sets the loan, not the ratio.

Ask each lender for the maximum loan its definition supports on your numbers.

Slide 9 · With no booking history, the lender picks the income

Cedar Ridge has no booking history under Maya and Chris. Neither will most houses you buy to convert. So where does the lender's income number come from? Each lender picks its own source.

Kiavi's rental page says qualifying rent is the lower of 110% of appraised market rent or a valid lease rent. That fits a long-term lease, so ask how it applies to nightly bookings. Kiavi also lends on nightly revenue in some markets, but that page doesn't say which data or haircut it uses. Some lenders accept an STR data report. On an existing STR, a lender may want statements, and the seller's history may not fit your pricing.

The lender will usually lean low. If its number is lower than yours, ask whether your pro forma is too high. Get the source, any haircut, and the appraiser requirement in writing.

Slide 10 · A year-two penalty would take $5,659

The rate gets the attention, but other terms decide the real cost. Kiavi's guide says down payment is often 20% to 25%. A point is 1% of the loan, paid upfront to lower the rate. Reserves, entity rules, a personal guarantee, and permit proof all vary by lender.

The prepayment penalty is the one I want you to see. Many DSCR loans charge a fee to pay off early, including a refinance. A common shape is a step-down, like 3-2-1: 3% in year one, 2% in year two, 1% in year three.

The course example refinances at month 24, which is still year two. The Cedar Ridge quote doesn't state a penalty, so this is a what-if. On a $282,951 balance, 2% is $5,659. The $15,725 cash out drops to $10,066. One more month and it falls to 1%. State law can limit these, so ask the lender how its penalty applies in your state.

Slide 12 · Send the same questions to two lenders

This is the first tool you take away. The full list in the lesson has 12 questions. Send them in writing to at least two lenders, on the same house, the same day if you can. Rates change daily, so quotes from different days can differ for reasons that have nothing to do with the lender.

These six are the ones that matter most. The formula, the income source, the minimum ratio, and the maximum loan decide whether the loan happens at the size you need. Rate, points, fees, and the prepayment schedule decide what it costs.

The rest of the list covers reserves, the LLC and guarantee, permit proof, the appraiser, and the rate lock. A rate lock holds your quoted rate for a set number of days while you close.

If a lender won't answer in writing, that's an answer too.

Put the written answers side by side. Lender A is our course example's quote. Lender B is invented for this exercise, so its numbers are assumptions.

Lender B looks cheaper upfront, with no points. But it uses the NOI definition with a 1.20 minimum. At 7.50% over 30 years, that supports about $276,094. Maya and Chris need $288,750, so they'd bring about $12,656 more cash.

Read offers in this order. First, loan size. Second, full upfront cost, points and fees. Third, what it costs to leave early. Rate comes last. A lower rate with a longer penalty can cost more if you plan the year-two refinance in OP.11.

Federal rules treat a loan to buy a rental you won't live in as business-purpose credit. So a DSCR lender may send a term sheet instead of a Loan Estimate. Ask for every fee in writing either way.
